# Chapter 2. At Dawn, Prepare for Difficult People

> *When you start your day, tell yourself: today I will meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, and unsociable. They act this way because they can't tell good from evil. But I, who have seen what good is, know that the person who wrongs me shares my own nature. None of them can harm me, and I cannot be angry with someone who is, in effect, my own kin. We were born to work together, like feet, hands, eyelids, or the upper and lower rows of teeth.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, II.1 (paraphrase)

The alarm goes off at 6:30. Before you even open your eyes, you know how the day will go: the meeting with the director who interrupts everyone, the supplier who will say the order "already shipped," the parents' group chat arguing about the school holiday party, your mother-in-law coming for dinner on Sunday. You pull the pillow over your head.

Marcus Aurelius knew that feeling too. That's why he began one of his notebooks with an exercise that sounds pessimistic and is really a vaccine.

## The Context: Writing Among Enemies

An old note in the manuscripts places these entries "among the Quadi, on the river Granua," that is, in the middle of a military campaign against Germanic peoples, far from Rome. Marcus spent much of the last decade of his life at the front. But what worried him most weren't the barbarians. It was his own people: ambitious generals, flattering senators, officials who said one thing and did another.

He didn't write "today everything will go well." He wrote the opposite: today you'll run into difficult people. And then he reminded himself of three things: that they act out of ignorance, that they share his nature, and that we were born to work together.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Rehearsing Adversity

The Stoics practiced *premeditatio malorum*, the premeditation of adversity. Seneca recommended it often: imagine in advance what might go wrong so that, if it happens, it doesn't catch you off guard. The point isn't to invite misfortune or to live in fear. It's that an expected blow hurts less than a surprise one.

When Marcus tells himself "today I'll run into ungrateful people," he isn't being bitter. He's lowering false expectations. Most of our anger at others comes from a silent demand: "people should be reasonable." People aren't always reasonable. Knowing that at 6:30 a.m. saves you the outrage at 11.

### Reading 2. The Socratic: No One Does Wrong Knowingly

Marcus says difficult people act this way because they "can't tell good from evil." The idea comes from Socrates: no one chooses evil knowing it's evil; they choose it because they mistakenly believe it's good for them. The coworker who steals your idea thinks it secures his job. The client who haggles to the point of insult thinks he's being savvy.

This reading changes the underlying emotion. If the other person is acting out of error, the right response isn't fury but something closer to patience with someone who took a wrong turn. It doesn't excuse them. It explains them.

Today psychology calls something similar the *fundamental attribution error*: we tend to explain our own failings by circumstances ("I was late because of traffic") and other people's by their character ("he was late because he's irresponsible"). Marcus asks you to extend to others the same generosity you extend to yourself.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: So I Have to Put Up with Everything?

The objection is obvious: if everyone is "my kin" and "can't harm me," do I have to tolerate the abusive boss, the partner who humiliates me, the business partner who steals from me?

No. Look at exactly what Marcus says: the other person *can't harm you* in the Stoic sense, meaning they can't force you to become a worse person. Your character is still yours. But that doesn't forbid you from protecting yourself, keeping your distance, reporting someone, or ending a relationship. Feet and hands cooperate; if one hand were infected, the body would treat it, not pretend it was healthy.

The meditation is a rule for not poisoning yourself with resentment, not a contract to let yourself be walked on. The practical difference is this: you can walk away from someone without hating them, and you can demand something without humiliating anyone.

**In a nutshell:** understanding a difficult person doesn't oblige you to stay close to them.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: "It Seemed Right to Him"

Epictetus gives advice almost identical to Marcus's for when someone mistreats us or speaks ill of us: remember that they do it because they think it's what they ought to do. They can't follow what seems right to you, only what seems right to them. If they're wrong, the one who is harmed is them, since they're the one deceived. And he concludes: start from here and you'll be gentle with whoever insults you, saying each time, "It seemed right to him."

Those few words, "it seemed right to him," are a portable tool. They don't say the other person is right. They say he acted according to what he understood, and that your job isn't to correct his mind from a place of outrage, but to decide what you will do.

### Seneca: Don't Be Angry with Someone Who Stumbles in the Dark

In his treatise *On Anger*, Seneca offers a useful image: the wise person doesn't get angry with people who make mistakes, any more than he would get angry with someone who stumbles in the dark, or with a sick person who complains. Human errors are so many and so common that being angry at each one would mean living angry.

Seneca adds something realistic: don't expect people to be other than they are. Whoever leaves the house expecting everyone to be kind will spend the day offended. Whoever leaves knowing they'll meet all kinds will be able to enjoy the good and bear the bad.

### What Science Says Today

In 1977, psychologist Lee Ross gave a name to a bias we all practice: the *fundamental attribution error*. We tend to explain other people's behavior by their character ("he's rude") and our own by circumstances ("I was tired"). Knowing about the bias doesn't eliminate it, but it helps you catch it, which is exactly what Marcus does when he reminds himself that others act out of ignorance rather than malice.

Meanwhile, psychologist Gabriele Oettingen has spent years studying what she calls *mental contrasting*: imagining not only the goal you want but also the specific obstacles that will get in the way. Her research suggests that people who picture the obstacles and plan how to respond tend to make more progress than those who only imagine success. It's a modern version of *premeditatio malorum*: preparing for difficulty isn't pessimism, it's strategy.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Making a list of difficult people every morning sounds like starting the day in a bad mood.

**Author:** If you do it with resentment, yes. If you do it the way a pilot checks the weather before takeoff, no. The pilot doesn't want the storm; he just doesn't want to be surprised by it. What puts you in a bad mood isn't anticipating difficult people. It's being caught unprepared.

**Skeptic:** Isn't it unfair to think badly of someone before you even see them?

**Author:** It isn't about thinking badly of anyone in particular, but about accepting something statistical: on an ordinary day, someone is going to be rude, late, or unfair. It might be your boss, a stranger, or you. Marcus doesn't say "my colleague is an idiot"; he says "I will meet people like this." It's humility about how the world is, not a verdict on a person.

**Skeptic:** "They act out of ignorance" sounds naive. Some people know perfectly well they're doing harm.

**Author:** That's the strongest objection, and you're partly right. Some people know they're hurting you. But the Socratic thesis is subtler: that person believes hurting you is good for them. They're wrong about what's good for them, even if they aren't wrong about the effect on you. That doesn't make them innocent. It just helps you not turn their mistake into your obsession.

**Skeptic:** And if the difficult person is my own partner, or my mother?

**Author:** Then the exercise matters even more, because you can't simply walk away from them. Preparing for the difficult side of someone you love isn't loving them less. It's loving them with your eyes open.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Sunday Dinner

Lauren knows that on Sunday they're having dinner at Andrew's mother's house and that, like every month, her mother-in-law will say something about the house, about Lauren's job, or about how "it's about time" they had another child. Lauren usually arrives tense, endures it in silence, and then explodes at Andrew in the car on the way home.

**The Stoic version of Sunday morning:** Lauren tells herself, "Today my mother-in-law will make some comment. She does it because she thinks she's looking out for her son and her family, even if she gets the delivery wrong. She can't make me be rude or ruin my day."

And then something practical: talking to Andrew beforehand. "If your mom brings up the baby thing again, can you change the subject?" Preparing isn't just a mental exercise. It's planning.

**A variation:** the exercise works the other way, too. Andrew can prepare for the day Lauren comes home drained from work and snaps at him. Preparing for the difficult side of someone you love is a form of love.

### At Work: The Director Who Interrupts

Rick has a weekly meeting with a director who interrupts, changes the subject, and dismisses ideas before hearing them out. Rick leaves every meeting with a headache.

Rick tries meditation II.1 at 7 a.m. on Monday. He writes on an index card: "Today the director is going to interrupt me. It's not about me; he does it to everyone. My job is to make my proposal clear, not to change him."

With that expectation, he adjusts his strategy: he brings his proposal on a single page, leads with the conclusion, and sends an email after the meeting summarizing what was agreed. The meeting is the same as always. Rick isn't.

### In Business: The Client Who Haggles

Megan has a prospect who always asks for a discount, compares her quote with his nephew who "also does design," and wants everything yesterday. Before every call, Megan feels her blood pressure rise.

The Stoic version: before the call, Megan writes down three things the client will *probably* say ("it's too expensive," "my nephew does it cheaper," "I need it by Friday") and prepares her answer to each. She also sets her limit: the lowest price she'd accept and the point at which she'd politely say no.

That's the Stoic business: it doesn't expect perfect clients; it designs its process for real ones. And it understands something else: the client who haggles isn't insulting her. He's doing what he thinks is good for him. Sometimes the right response is to negotiate. Sometimes it's to let him go without resentment.

### At School: The Group Project and the Parents' Chat

**If you're studying as an adult:** George has to do a group project with three classmates in his MBA program. One doesn't respond, another wants to do everything his way, and the third turns things in late. George, who works all week, feels he's carrying the team.

Before the first meeting, George tells himself: "Someone on this team won't pull their weight. That's normal in group projects." And he proposes something concrete: a shared document with tasks, owners, and deadlines. If someone falls short, the problem is visible without anyone needing to fight.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire is in the parents' group chat for Sophie's class. Every week there's an argument: the contribution for the holiday party, too much homework, the dad who scolds the teacher in all caps. Claire decides to apply the meditation before opening the chat: "Someone here will be angry. I don't have to reply to everything." She mutes the chat and checks it once a day. She answers only what concerns her. She's gained two hours a week and a little peace.

### In Your Family: The Holiday Dinner

Every December, Lauren's family gathers at her mother's house. And every December, her Uncle Ernie brings up politics, mocks anyone who thinks differently, and ends up fighting with someone. Lauren arrives at dinner with a knot in her stomach.

This year she tries meditation II.1 starting in the morning: "Today my uncle will talk politics and try to provoke people. He does it because he thinks that's how he defends what he believes. He can't make me argue." And she prepares three things: a friendly line to stay out of it ("Uncle Ernie, today I'm not debating, I'm eating"), an ally (her cousin, who also hates fights), and an exit plan (helping with the dishes when the tone rises).

Her uncle talks politics. Lauren smiles, says her line, and goes to the kitchen. For the first time in years, she gets home without a headache.

The following December, however, the plan doesn't work as well. Her uncle no longer talks about politics in general: he mocks Lauren's job directly, in front of everyone. She says her line, but he keeps going. Lauren ends up answering more sharply than she meant to and leaves before dessert. In the car, Andrew asks whether Stoicism "failed." Lauren thinks about it and says not entirely: this time she got angry, but she didn't spend three days rehashing the fight, and the next day she texted her uncle to tell him calmly not to make those kinds of comments again. The practice didn't make her invulnerable. It helped her recover faster.

### In Money and Health: The Call to the Bank

Rick has to call his bank about an incorrect charge on his credit card. He knows how it will go: twenty minutes on hold, three transfers, a representative who says there's nothing they can do. Last time he ended up yelling and hanging up with nothing resolved.

This time he prepares the way Marcus would. He writes down the details beforehand: card number, date and amount of the charge, the reference number from his previous claim. He lists what they'll probably tell him ("you'll have to wait 45 days," "it doesn't show up in our system") and his answer to each. He decides he won't yell, because the representative isn't the bank, and yelling at him won't get the money back.

It takes forty minutes. He's transferred twice. But he hangs up with a case number and a resolution date. The call was just as annoying. He wasn't.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Think the worst of everyone."** No. Preparing for difficult people isn't assuming everyone is difficult. It's not being shocked when someone is. Most people, on most days, are reasonable.

**"If the other person acts out of ignorance, they're not responsible."** No. Marcus explains other people's behavior so he won't hate them, not to spare them the consequences. An employee who steals gets fired, even if you understand him.

**"You have to suffer in silence."** No. Often the Stoic response is to speak up, set a boundary, or ask for a change. What you avoid is the impulsive reaction, not action.

## This Week's Exercise: The Morning Rehearsal

For seven days, before checking your phone:

1. Think about the people you'll see or talk to today.
2. Identify the most difficult one and write one line about what they're likely to do ("criticize," "show up late," "ask for a discount").
3. Write one sentence of understanding: why they might act this way, without excusing it.
4. Decide your response: what you will do and what you won't.

At night, review: did what you imagined happen? How did you respond? Over time you'll find that difficult people become predictable, and predictable things can be managed.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** each morning, name just one difficult person for the day and one sentence of understanding.
- **Intermediate:** add the concrete plan: what you'll say, what you won't say, and your exit if things get heated.
- **Advanced:** prepare for your own difficult side, too. Write: "Today I'll probably ___ (interrupt, get impatient, judge)." The Stoics knew that the difficult person we meet most often in a day is ourselves.

## Key Ideas

- Preparing for difficult people isn't pessimism: it's strategy.
- Almost no one does wrong knowing it's wrong; they believe it's good for them.
- Explain other people's behavior with the same generosity you use to explain your own.
- Understanding someone doesn't oblige you to stay close to them.

## Journaling Question

> **What am I silently demanding from someone who never promised to give it to me?**

---

Tomorrow the alarm will go off at 6:30 again, and the difficult people will still be on your calendar. You won't change them. But before you take the pillow off your head, you can do what an emperor did in a tent: name what's coming, remember that those people belong to your own species, and get up to work alongside them, or to set a boundary with them, without letting them steal your day.
