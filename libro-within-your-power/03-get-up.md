# Chapter 3. Get Up and Do the Work of a Human Being

> *At dawn, when you have trouble getting out of bed, keep this in mind: I am getting up to do the work of a human being. Why complain about going to do what I was born for? Was I made to lie warm under the blankets? "But it's more pleasant." So you were born for pleasure, and not for action? Don't you see the plants, the birds, the ants, the spiders, and the bees each doing their part?*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, V.1 (paraphrase)

Here is a scene almost no one admits to: the most powerful man in the world, wrestling with his blankets. The emperor of Rome, with the sun coming up, bargaining with himself for five more minutes. This meditation is proof that Marcus Aurelius was human. And that procrastination isn't a modern invention.

You know the scene. The alarm goes off. You hit snooze. It goes off again. You do the math: if I skip the shower, skip breakfast, leave without fixing my hair, I can still sleep ten more minutes. The day begins with a small defeat.

## The Context: Duty as Identity

Marcus never asked to be emperor. He was adopted as a young man by Antoninus Pius at the request of the emperor Hadrian, and from then on his path was set. According to some accounts, he would have preferred the life of a philosopher. Instead he got government, war, and the courts.

That's why this meditation isn't about motivation but about something deeper: what a person is for. For the Stoics, every being has a function according to its nature. The bee's is to make honey; the spider's, to weave. A human being's is to act with reason and for the good of others. Getting up isn't an obligation imposed from outside. It's being what you are.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Your Nature Is to Act

Marcus compares human beings with the plants and animals that fulfill their function without complaint. The underlying idea is both Aristotelian and Stoic: what is good for each thing is to perform its function well. A knife is good if it cuts; a human being is good if they think, choose well, and contribute to their community.

From this angle, the question each morning isn't "Do I feel like it?" but "What is mine to do today as a person?" Feelings come and go. The function remains.

**In a nutshell:** you don't get up because you feel like it; you get up because it's your job.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Identity Before Motivation

We now know that motivation is a poor strategy for consistency. Waiting to "feel like" going to the gym, studying, or making the difficult call is waiting for something that almost never arrives on time. What works better is identity and habit: "I'm someone who works out," "I'm someone who follows through."

Marcus doesn't tell himself "come on, you can do it." He tells himself "this is who I am." It's the same strategy. He doesn't argue with fatigue; he puts it in its place. The fatigue is real, but it doesn't get the deciding vote.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Burnout?

Read in a hurry, this meditation sounds like hustle culture: get up at five, never rest, sleep is for the weak. That would betray it.

A few lines after the passage quoted above, Marcus acknowledges that nature also sets limits on eating and drinking, and that we need rest. What he criticizes isn't sleep but indulging in comfort while skimping on effort. His question is: do you rest so you can do your work, or do you live to rest?

There's a difference between laziness and exhaustion. Laziness is cured by starting; exhaustion is cured by stopping. If you've been unable to get out of bed for months, with no energy for anything, that may not be a lack of discipline but depression or burnout, and the Stoic response, the one reason chooses, is to ask for help. Marcus himself praises, in Book I, how his adoptive father took care of his body in moderation, and he gives thanks for the remedies he received when he was ill.

**In a nutshell:** get up to fulfill your function, and understand that rest is part of it, too.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Reclaim Your Time

The first letter Seneca writes to his friend Lucilius opens with a command: claim yourself back. Recover the time that until now has been taken from you, stolen from you, or has simply slipped away. Seneca says some of our time is taken, some is stolen, and some just drains away, and that the most shameful loss is the one that happens through carelessness.

For Seneca, getting up to do what you should isn't only about meeting other people's expectations: it's about reclaiming your own life. Every morning you put off what matters, someone or something else decides where your day goes.

### Epictetus: Nothing Great Happens All at Once

To an impatient student who wanted to change his character overnight, Epictetus answered with an image from the farm: nothing great comes into being suddenly, not even a bunch of grapes or a fig. If you tell him you want a fig now, he'll tell you to wait: first let it blossom, then bear fruit, then ripen.

The discipline of getting up works the same way. No one becomes consistent in a week. The person who becomes consistent is the one who gets up today, and tomorrow, and the day after, even if some days they fail.

### What Science Says Today

A widely cited study by researcher Phillippa Lally, published in 2009, followed people trying to adopt a new habit. On average it took them about 66 days for the behavior to become automatic, though the range was huge: from 18 to more than 250 days. Missing a day now and then didn't ruin the process. The practical conclusion is very Stoic: consistency matters more than perfection.

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer has studied what he calls *implementation intentions*: plans of the form "when X happens, I will do Y" ("when the alarm goes off, I'll put my feet on the floor before I pick up my phone"). In many studies, this kind of concrete plan increases the likelihood of reaching a goal compared with intention alone.

And a warning: since 2019, the World Health Organization has included burnout in its international classification as an occupational phenomenon, not a failure of willpower. If you've been exhausted for months, you don't need more discipline: you need to look at your circumstances and, perhaps, ask for help.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Get up and do the work of a human being" is easy to say when you own slaves and don't have to pay rent.

**Author:** That's a fair criticism, and we shouldn't forget it. Marcus lived in a slave-owning society, with privileges you and I don't have. But notice that Epictetus, who was a slave, says practically the same thing. The idea doesn't depend on social position; it depends on something we all share: the temptation to stay comfortable when we know something is ours to do.

**Skeptic:** But today the problem isn't laziness. Lots of people work too much.

**Author:** Completely agree. That's why the chapter distinguishes between laziness and burnout. If you work twelve hours a day, meditation V.1 isn't asking you to get up and work more; it's asking you why you work and whether what you do is really your function or just someone else's demand. Sometimes the Stoic answer is to quit, renegotiate your hours, or rest.

**Skeptic:** And what about someone who finds no meaning in their job? Should they just put up with it?

**Author:** They should look for meaning, and if they can't find it, change what they can. But in the meantime, a human being's function isn't reduced to their job title. You can be a good coworker, a good parent, someone honest in a place where no one else is. That's human work, too, and it doesn't depend on your job being fascinating.

**Skeptic:** It still sounds like military discipline to me.

**Author:** Marcus was literally a military commander, so there's some of that. But the discipline he proposes doesn't come from an outside drill sergeant; it comes from you. It isn't "obey"; it's "remember who you want to be." That difference changes everything.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Invisible Work

In Lauren and Andrew's house there's a to-do list no one ever wrote down: pay the gas bill, book the pediatrician, remember the nephews' birthdays, buy toilet paper. For years, that list lived in Lauren's head. Every morning she woke up tired before she'd even started.

Meditation V.1 helps here in two ways. For Andrew: "the work of a human being" in a relationship includes his share of the household, not "helping out." One morning he gets up and says, "From now on, doctor's appointments and the gas bill are mine." It isn't a favor. It's his function.

For Lauren: doing her part doesn't mean doing everything. Fulfilling her function also includes asking for what belongs to the other person, instead of carrying it silently and resenting it.

### At Work: The Pointless Monday

Rick has spent weeks feeling that his work doesn't matter. He fills out reports no one reads. Every Monday it gets harder to start.

Rick can't change jobs tomorrow. But he can ask Marcus's question: what is my function here, beyond my title? He comes up with three answers: training the two new analysts who rely on him; supporting his family; doing things well even when no one notices, because that says something about who he is.

The report is still boring. But now he gets up for something else. And, along the way, he starts looking seriously for a position where his function is more visible. Both things fit.

### In Business: The Entrepreneur Without a Boss

Megan doesn't have a boss making her show up. What felt like freedom became a trap: some days she starts working at eleven, after scrolling social media, and finishes at midnight, feeling guilty.

She applies the meditation with a simple rule: at 8:30 she sits down at her computer, whether she feels like it or not, and does the most important task of the day for an hour before opening her email. She doesn't wait for inspiration. She does her job. The rest of the day falls into place.

Not always. Some weeks an urgent client breaks her routine on Monday and Megan slips back into old habits: working in a scattered way until midnight and blaming herself for it. The difference is that now she knows the way back. On Thursday she sits down at 8:30 again. Marcus didn't write to himself that he must never stay in bed; he wrote down what to do every time it happened.

**For entrepreneurs:** when you're your own boss, your identity has to do the job the time clock used to do.

### At School: Studying While Exhausted

**If you're studying as an adult:** on Saturday at 6:30 a.m., George has to drive an hour to get to his MBA class. He's worked all week. Every Saturday he asks himself why he signed up for this.

The meditation offers him an answer that doesn't depend on feeling like it: learning is part of the work of a human being, and he decided this stage of his life would be for growth. On the first page of his notebook he writes why he started the program: "so my kids can see me finish something hard." When the alarm goes off, he doesn't argue with the blanket. He reads that sentence.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie doesn't want to get up for school. Claire could yell, plead, or bribe. She tries something else: at night, she asks her daughter what she has to do tomorrow that only she can do (help her friend with the presentation, take care of the class plant). Without naming it, she's teaching her that getting up has a purpose.

### In Your Family: Caring for Aging Parents

On Saturdays, Rick takes his mother to the farmers' market and then to lunch. Some Saturdays he doesn't feel like it: he's tired, he wants to sleep in, he wants to watch the game. One morning, as he hits snooze, he remembers Marcus's question: were you born to be comfortable, or to act?

He doesn't experience it as a punishment. His function as a son, at this stage, includes this. And when he does it from there instead of from guilt, he discovers something: those mornings with his mother are among the few hours of the week when no one asks him for a report. What felt like a burden slowly becomes a refuge.

### In Money and Health: The Gym Membership

George paid for an annual gym membership in January. In February he went three times. In March, not once. Every month that passes he feels he's throwing money away, and the guilt makes him go even less.

He applies the blanket sentence to his health: he writes down why he wants to exercise. Not "to lose weight," but "to be able to play soccer with my kids without running out of breath." He changes the plan: instead of an hour five days a week, he goes for twenty minutes three days a week, always at the same time, right after dropping the kids off. He packs his gym bag the night before.

He didn't become an athlete. But by June he'd gone twelve weeks without missing a session, and for George that mattered more than any number on the scale.

## Author's Note

For a season of my life I worked and studied at the same time. There were nights I came home so drained I couldn't tell whether what I felt was laziness or genuine exhaustion. Over time I learned to ask myself this chapter's question: is this cured by starting or by stopping? Some nights the answer was to open my notebook for twenty minutes even if I didn't feel like it. Other nights it was to shut everything down, eat dinner, and sleep. Both were part of my function. What never helped was staying halfway: neither studying nor resting, just feeling guilty in front of a screen.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Getting up early makes you a better person."** No. Marcus is talking about getting up to fulfill your function, not about a specific hour. Some people's work starts at night. What matters is not running from what's yours to do.

**"Rest is laziness."** No. Rest is part of human nature. Laziness is using rest to avoid what you should be doing.

**"If you don't feel like it, you're weak."** No. Feeling or not feeling like it is irrelevant to a Stoic. Your feelings aren't within your power; the decision to act is.

## This Week's Exercise: The Blanket Sentence

1. Write on an index card, or on your phone's lock screen, a single sentence that answers: **why do I get up?** "To pay the bills" doesn't count. Look for something that's truly yours: a person, a project, a value.
2. For seven days, when the alarm goes off, read the sentence before you think about anything else.
3. Get up within the first two minutes. No negotiating.
4. At night, note whether you fulfilled your main function for the day, even if it was small.

If after a week you can't find any sentence that moves you, that's valuable information too. Maybe you don't need more discipline but a change of direction.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** write your blanket sentence and read it when you wake up for one week.
- **Intermediate:** create an implementation intention: "When the alarm goes off, I put my feet on the floor and drink a glass of water before looking at my phone."
- **Advanced:** look back over the last seven days and ask yourself honestly whether your fatigue is cured by starting or by stopping. If it's the latter, write down what you're going to stop doing so you can truly rest.

## Key Ideas

- Feelings don't decide; your function does.
- Laziness is cured by starting; exhaustion is cured by stopping.
- Consistency matters more than perfection.
- When you slip, don't punish yourself: get back on the path.
