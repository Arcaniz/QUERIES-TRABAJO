# Chapter 6. Kindness Is Invincible

> *Kindness is invincible, as long as it is genuine and not a fake smile or a mask. What can the most insolent person do to you if you remain kind to him, if when the chance comes you gently correct him and, right when he's trying to hurt you, calmly show him: "No, my son, we weren't born for this"?*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, XI.18 (paraphrase)

> *The best way to get revenge is not to become like the one who wronged you.*
> — *Meditations*, VI.6 (paraphrase)

The driver who cuts you off on the freeway. Your boss's 11 p.m. message with "URGENT" in all caps. Your partner, mid-argument, bringing up the thing they promised never to bring up again. The customer who leaves you a one-star review full of lies.

In each case there's an instant, barely half a second, when anger rises like a wave. And in that half second, almost everything that follows is decided.

## The Context: An Emperor with a Temper

Marcus wasn't naturally calm. In Book I he thanks his grandfather for teaching him good character and control of his temper, which suggests he needed it. Ancient sources describe him as serious, sometimes stern. His notes on anger are so numerous they read like those of someone fighting it every day.

In meditation XI.18 he makes a list of nine reminders for when someone offends him: that we were all born for one another; that the other person acts according to their beliefs; that if the other is right there's nothing to complain about, and if not, they act out of ignorance; that he himself has made similar mistakes; that he can't always be sure the other person really did wrong; that life is short; that it isn't the other person's act that disturbs him but his judgment about it; that the consequences of anger are usually worse than what caused it; and, finally, that kindness is invincible. (Some readers count a tenth reminder as well, which Marcus presents as a gift from Apollo: expecting bad people not to do wrong is madness.)

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Anger as Brief Madness

Seneca wrote an entire treatise, *On Anger*, in which he calls it a brief madness. For the Stoics, anger is the worst state because it disguises itself as justice: the angry person always believes they're right. But while angry, they do things they regret: they say words they can't take back, they make decisions they'd never make calmly.

Marcus says it with almost mathematical precision: the consequences of anger are usually far worse than its cause. The driver cut you off and you lost two seconds; if you chase him, you could lose your life. Your partner said something hurtful; if you answer with something worse, you could lose years.

### Reading 2. The Strategic: Kindness as Strength

"Kindness is invincible" can sound naive. But Marcus isn't talking about weakness. He's talking about a kindness that corrects: "No, my son, we weren't born for this." It's firm and it's calm.

In an argument, the other person's anger needs yours in order to grow. It's a fire that needs fuel. If you respond calmly, their anger runs out of fuel. Not because you give in, but because you refuse to play their game. In negotiation they say something similar: whoever loses their temper loses the negotiation.

And VI.6 takes the idea further: the best revenge is not to become like the one who wronged you. If you answer rudeness with rudeness, the offender wins twice: they offended you, and they turned you into someone like them.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Righteous Anger?

This is an ancient debate. Aristotle believed in a just anger: being angry at the right person, at the right time, for the right reason, and to the right degree. Without anger, he said, no one would defend the weak. Many social movements were born of anger at injustice.

The Stoics answer that you can fight injustice without anger, and that you fight it better that way. A judge who sentences in anger is dangerous; one who sentences with justice is trustworthy. Indignation may be the spark, but just action needs a cool head.

The honest position probably lies in between: the first flash of anger at injustice is human and even healthy, because it tells you something is wrong. What doesn't help is letting that anger drive your actions.

**In a nutshell:** let anger alert you; let reason decide.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Best Remedy Is Delay

Seneca devoted three whole books to anger, and in them he keeps returning to one idea: the greatest remedy for anger is delay. The first wave is the strongest; if you let it pass, anger loses force on its own.

He tells a story about Plato. One day, angry with a slave, Plato raised his hand to strike him. But he realized he was furious and froze, hand in the air. A friend came by and asked what he was doing. Plato answered: "I'm punishing an angry man." He was punishing himself for nearly acting in anger. Seneca adds that Plato then decided not to punish the slave himself, because he didn't trust his own judgment while angry.

### Epictetus: Buy Yourself Time

Epictetus gives similar advice in the *Handbook*: when someone offends you, remember that it isn't the person who insults or strikes you who wrongs you, but your opinion that it's an insult. And he adds a very concrete instruction: don't let yourself be carried away by the first impression. Buy yourself time, take a breath, and you'll find it easier to master yourself.

"Buy yourself time" may be the most useful advice against anger ever written. Counting to ten, going for a walk, waiting until tomorrow to answer the email: all of them are ways of buying time.

### What Science Says Today

For a long time people believed that "venting" by hitting something or yelling helped release anger. In 2002, psychologist Brad Bushman published a widely cited experiment: people who hit a punching bag while thinking about someone who had made them angry ended up, on average, more aggressive than people who did nothing. Venting that way seemed to feed anger rather than put it out, just as Epictetus would have predicted with his idea of habits.

What does seem to help, according to various lines of research, is creating distance: calming the body, waiting, and then reinterpreting the situation. In other words, exactly what Seneca and Epictetus proposed two thousand years ago.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Being kind to someone who treats you badly is for suckers.

**Author:** Being kind and letting yourself be walked on are not the same. Look at Marcus's line: "No, my son, we weren't born for this." It's a correction. It's firm. Stoic kindness isn't smiling while you're humiliated; it's telling the truth without humiliating anyone. And in practice that's much harder than yelling.

**Skeptic:** Sometimes anger is the only thing that makes people take you seriously.

**Author:** Sometimes, in the short run, yes. But think of the people you respect most at work or in your family. Are they the ones who yell, or the ones who, when they speak calmly, make everyone go quiet? Anger gives you power for five minutes. Calm firmness gives you authority for years.

**Skeptic:** What about Aristotle? He said there's such a thing as righteous anger.

**Author:** That's a real disagreement between schools, and there's no need to hide it. Aristotle believed anger, in the right measure, is a virtue. The Stoics believed you can fight injustice without anger, with reason. I think everyday experience gives each of them a point: the first flash of indignation warns you that something is wrong; but the decisions you make in the middle of a rage are rarely your best.

**Skeptic:** And if I hold my anger in, it'll blow up later.

**Author:** If you suppress it, yes. That's why the Stoics don't talk about suppressing it, but about waiting and examining the judgment. It isn't stuffing anger into a pressure cooker; it's letting the flame die down while you think. You can feel the difference in your body: suppression leaves you tense; delay leaves you calmer.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Fight That Escalates

Lauren and Andrew argue about money. She says he spends without checking with her. He says she does too. She brings up the trip from two years ago. He brings up her mother. Five minutes in, they're no longer talking about money. They're talking about who's worse.

In therapy they learn to use meditation XI.18 as an emergency brake. They agreed on a word ("pause") that either of them can say when they feel the argument getting out of hand. When it's said, they separate for twenty minutes. It isn't running away: it's letting the "brief madness" pass. Then they come back to the topic with one rule: talk about the problem, not about the person.

The first time they try it, it doesn't go well. Lauren says "pause," and Andrew takes it as a door slammed in his face: "You always leave when it suits you." The pause turns into another fight. It takes them several weeks to learn that the word works only if both commit to coming back to the topic, and that whoever calls the pause is the one who has to seek out the other afterward. Stoic tools don't work like a light switch; you learn them by practicing them, and by failing.

Invincible kindness in a relationship isn't keeping quiet. It's telling the truth without trying to wound. "It worries me that we spend without talking about it" is firm. "You're just like your father" is fuel.

### At Work: The Flaming Email

The director emails Rick, copying the whole team, saying his report "is completely useless." Rick feels the blood rush to his face. He starts writing a reply, reply-all.

He remembers Seneca's rule: don't act while hot. He saves the draft and goes for a walk. An hour later he writes a different email, only to the director: "I'd like to understand what the report was missing so I can fix it. Do you have fifteen minutes tomorrow?" In the meeting, the director dials it down. It turns out he was under pressure from corporate.

Rick didn't concede the director was right. But he also didn't give him the satisfaction of seeing him lose control in front of everyone. That's the revenge of VI.6.

### In Business: The Unfair Review

A customer leaves a one-star review on Megan's business profile. He says the work was bad and she was rude. It's false: the customer asked for changes outside the contract and got angry when she quoted him for the extra work.

The temptation is to respond by exposing him. Megan knows prospective clients won't just read the review; they'll read her response. She writes: "I'm sorry you weren't satisfied. We completed the agreed-upon work and offered to quote the additional changes. We're happy to talk it through." Firm, brief, kind.

Three weeks later, a new client tells her: "I hired you because of how you answered that review." Invincible kindness is good for business, too.

### At School: The Unfair Professor and the Kid Who Pushes

**If you're studying as an adult:** George's professor mocks him in front of the class for a question. George feels like firing back. Instead, he waits until the end of class and tells him privately: "Professor, I felt uncomfortable with that comment. My question was sincere." The professor half-apologizes. It isn't a total victory, but George walked out of that class being who he wanted to be.

**If you're supporting your kids:** a boy pushes Sophie in line. She comes home furious: "Tomorrow I'm going to push him." Claire doesn't tell her "don't be angry." She says: "You have every right to be angry. And do you want to be someone who pushes?" Together they figure out what to do: tell the boy firmly, and tell the teacher if it happens again. Sophie learns that she can defend herself without becoming like the one who hurt her.

### In Your Family: The Teenage Son

George's oldest son is fifteen and talks back to him almost every day. George, who comes home tired from work, feels his blood boil. Several times they've ended up yelling at each other and slamming doors.

George tries Seneca's rule: when his son talks back, he doesn't respond in the moment. He says: "I'm not going to talk to you like this right now; we'll talk at dinner." At dinner, calmer, he tells him what bothered him and listens to what's going on with his son. It turns out the boy is having trouble with a group of classmates and comes home carrying that.

The back talk didn't end. But the slammed doors did. And George discovered that behind a lot of his son's rudeness was something he wasn't telling him.

### In Money and Health: Traffic and Blood Pressure

Rick finds out at a checkup that his blood pressure is high. The doctor asks about stress. Rick realizes he gets angry in traffic every day, twice a day, for more than an hour.

He can't change the traffic. But he can change what he does in it. He starts leaving ten minutes earlier so he isn't rushing, listens to audiobooks, and every time he feels anger rising, he says under his breath: "Buy yourself time." It feels ridiculous at first. Three months later, his blood pressure has come down. The doctor credits part of it to diet and exercise. Rick knows another part came from no longer fighting with the freeway.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Be kind to everyone even if they mistreat you."** No. Stoic kindness includes correcting, setting boundaries, and walking away. It isn't tolerating abuse.

**"Kindness means pretending you're not bothered."** No. Marcus says kindness must be genuine, not a fake smile. If you smile on the outside and boil on the inside, you're not being Stoic; you're bottling it up, and sooner or later it explodes.

**"You shouldn't feel anger."** No. The first movement is involuntary. The point is not to obey it blindly.

## This Week's Exercise: The List of Nine

Write a short version of Marcus's reminders on an index card for when someone offends you:

1. We were born to work together.
2. The other person acts according to what they believe.
3. If they're right, there's nothing to complain about; if not, they act out of ignorance.
4. I've made mistakes like this too.
5. I don't know everything that led them to do it.
6. Life is too short for this fight.
7. What bothers me is my judgment, not their act.
8. Anger will cost me more than the offense.
9. Kindness is invincible.

Every time you feel anger this week, before responding, read the whole card. It'll take thirty seconds. In those thirty seconds, the wave passes.

Also practice the 24-hour rule: no important email, message, or decision while you're hot. Write it if you want, but don't send it until the next day.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** apply the 24-hour rule to every message and email you write while angry this week.
- **Intermediate:** keep Epictetus's count: mark on a calendar every day you get through without blowing up. Try to reach seven in a row.
- **Advanced:** when someone offends you, before responding, write the best possible version of their motives, the most generous you can think of. You don't have to believe it. You just have to consider it before you act.

## Key Ideas

- Anger costs you more than the offense took from you.
- The best remedy for anger is delay: buy yourself time.
- The best revenge is not to become like the one who wronged you.
- Stoic kindness is firm: it corrects without humiliating.

## Journaling Question

> **In what recent situation did I become something like the person who offended me?**

---

The driver on the freeway goes on his way without knowing he upset you. Your boss's email has already been sent. The review is already posted. You can't change any of that. But in the half second that comes next, the one that decides whether the wave drags you under or rolls past, you still have the last word. And it can be kind.
