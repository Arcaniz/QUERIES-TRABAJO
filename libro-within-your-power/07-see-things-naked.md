# Chapter 7. See Things Stripped Bare

> *When you're served meat or fish, think: this is the corpse of a fish, a bird, or a pig. The finest wine is grape juice, and the purple robe is sheep's wool dyed with the blood of a shellfish. Just as these thoughts get to the bottom of things and show them as they are, we should do the same throughout life: when something seems most worthy of esteem, strip it bare, see how little it's worth, and peel away the story it puffs itself up with.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.13 (paraphrase)

In a department store window there's a $2,500 handbag. Next to it, an almost identical one for $120. The difference is a logo about an inch wide. Some people go into debt for a year for that logo.

In an expensive restaurant, the server describes the dish for a full minute: "a reduction of," "an emulsion of," "on a bed of." What arrives is a huge plate with three bites in the center.

On LinkedIn, someone describes himself as a "visionary, disruptor, and transformation leader." He's a sales manager.

Marcus Aurelius had an exercise for all of this: strip things bare. Remove the name, the packaging, and the story, and see what's left.

## The Context: The Man in the Purple Robe

It's no small detail that Marcus writes about the purple robe. Purple was the color of imperial power. Only the emperor could wear it in full. It was the highest status symbol in the Roman world. And Marcus, who wore it, reminded himself in private: it's sheep's wool stained with the blood of a snail.

In the same passage, he applies the exercise even to sex, which he describes with almost clinical bluntness, as a friction of bodies. He doesn't do it out of contempt for pleasure, but so as not to be enslaved by it. His method is always the same: remove the emotion, the fame, and the decoration, and look at the naked fact. Scholars sometimes call this "physical definition": describing something exactly as it is, without the value-laden adjectives we add.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Value Lies in Virtue, Not in Things

For the Stoics, wealth, fame, pleasure, and status are "indifferents." They aren't bad; many are "preferred" (it's better to be healthy than sick, to have money than not). But they aren't good in themselves, because they can be used well or badly. The only true good is virtue.

Stripping things bare is a way of remembering that hierarchy. If the purple robe is dyed wool, then the person wearing it isn't worth more for wearing it. If the title of director is a chair and a word on a business card, then the person who holds it isn't a better person for holding it.

**In a nutshell:** things are worth what they are, not what they appear to be.

### Reading 2. The Practical: An Antidote to Marketing

Today we're surrounded by machines that do exactly the opposite of what Marcus proposed: they dress things up. Advertising doesn't sell a car; it sells freedom. It doesn't sell a perfume; it sells seduction. It doesn't sell a phone; it sells belonging.

The VI.13 exercise is a filter for spending decisions. Before buying something expensive, describe it stripped bare: "a piece of leather with a logo," "a computer that does the same thing as the one I have, with a better camera," "an SUV I'll use to drive to work, alone, every day." If you still want it after stripping it bare, buy it without guilt. Often you'll discover that what you wanted was the story, not the thing.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Beauty?

There's a risk in this exercise: becoming cynical. If everything is "just" something, then a symphony is "just" vibrating air, a kiss is "just" skin touching skin, and a child is "just" a collection of cells. Down that road you end up valuing nothing.

That isn't what Marcus is after. In other passages he marvels at the beauty of natural things: bread splitting as it bakes, ears of wheat bending, the foam at a wild boar's mouth. The exercise isn't for despising everything but for deflating what's inflated. It applies above all to what stirs excessive desire, envy, or vanity.

The rule might be this: strip bare what enslaves you; leave clothed what makes you better.

**In a nutshell:** stripping things bare isn't despising them; it's breaking their spell.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: Remember What It Is You Love

Epictetus proposes an exercise similar to Marcus's, but applied to what we're fond of. With everything that delights you or is useful to you, he says, remember what it is, starting with the smallest things. If you're fond of a jug, say: "I'm fond of a jug." That way, if it breaks, you won't be upset.

The example seems trivial, but the method is powerful. When you describe something as it is, you take away its power to overwhelm you. A new car is a car; a phone is a phone. You can enjoy them, but they won't destroy you if they get scratched or lost.

### Seneca: Poor Is Not the One Who Has Little

Seneca wrote a line that has been quoted for centuries: it's not the person who has too little who is poor, but the one who craves more. Wealth, for him, isn't measured by what you have but by the distance between what you have and what you want. Whoever desires without limit will always be poor, even with millions.

Seneca, by the way, was one of the richest men in Rome, and his critics threw that contradiction in his face. He answered that the wise man may have riches as long as he doesn't keep them in his heart. It's a debatable answer, and it's worth reading him with that tension in mind: a rich philosopher preaching simplicity reminds us that this idea is easier to write than to live.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologists call *hedonic adaptation* our tendency to get used to good things: the new car, the raise, or the bigger house gives us a joy that, over time, returns to roughly the previous level. A classic 1978 study by Philip Brickman and colleagues compared lottery winners with other people and did not find the winners to be much happier in a lasting way. It's a small, old study, but the general idea has been explored by much later research.

Other lines of research, such as psychologist Tim Kasser's, have linked a strong orientation toward materialistic values (wealth, image, status) with lower well-being on average. And work by Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich suggested that, in many cases, spending on experiences brings more satisfaction than spending on things.

None of this means money doesn't matter. It means that, once the essentials are covered, what we buy for the story rarely gives us what the story promises.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Stripping things bare seems sad. If everything is "just" something, nothing has any charm.

**Author:** The exercise isn't for everything. It's for what enslaves you: what puts you in debt, what makes you envious, what makes you feel small. You don't need to strip bare your child's hug or a sunset. Marcus himself marveled at the beauty of natural things. What he strips is prestige, not beauty.

**Skeptic:** But brands, titles, and reputation matter in the real world. If I don't look after them, I miss opportunities.

**Author:** Of course they matter, as tools. A good title can open a door; a good reputation can win you a client. The Stoics called them "preferred indifferents": it's reasonable to prefer them. The problem begins when you confuse the tool with your worth as a person, and then you lose sleep over a logo or a job title.

**Skeptic:** Seneca was incredibly rich. Isn't it hypocritical for a millionaire to tell you not to care about money?

**Author:** It's a real contradiction, and his contemporaries threw it in his face. But the idea can be separated from the man. Besides, Seneca didn't say not to care about money; he said not to depend on it. A rich man who could lose everything without losing his head is freer than a poor man obsessed with having more. Seneca's contradiction works as a warning: this is easy to preach and hard to live.

**Skeptic:** So it's wrong to buy myself something expensive?

**Author:** No. If after stripping it bare you still want it, and you can pay for it without mortgaging your peace of mind, buy it and enjoy it. The exercise doesn't forbid; it clarifies. It makes sure you're buying the thing and not a story someone sold you.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Wedding and the Feed

Lauren and Andrew have friends who spent the equivalent of a down payment on a house on their wedding. Others post vacation photos every month. Sometimes Lauren feels their married life is boring by comparison.

She does the exercise. A lavish wedding, stripped bare, is a one-night party. A vacation on social media, stripped bare, is one photo chosen out of fifty and edited. None of it says how those couples treat each other on an ordinary Tuesday.

Lauren and Andrew decide their "luxury" will be the things that don't show up on social media: Sunday breakfast together without phones, and one simple trip a year, just the two of them.

**The flip side:** stripping things bare also helps in a fight. "You left your socks on the floor" is, stripped bare, two socks on the floor. It isn't "you don't respect me." Sometimes an argument dies down just by describing the fact without the story.

### At Work: The Title and the Corner Office

Rick gets two job offers. One comes with the title "Director of Operations," a private office, and a salary 10 percent higher. The other is "Senior Manager" at a company with a better culture, flexible hours, and a boss he knows and respects.

The title dazzles him. He does the exercise: "Director" is a word on a business card. The office is a room with a door. The extra 10 percent comes to about $400 a month, in exchange for a boss known for burning out his team and two hours of commuting a day.

Stripped bare, the second job is worth more. Rick takes it. A year later, he doesn't miss the word.

### In Business: Real Value

Megan wonders why other studios charge three times what she does. She realizes many of them are selling the story: designer offices, slick presentations, phrases like "360-degree brand strategy." Behind it, sometimes, the work is the same as hers or worse.

The exercise works two ways for a business. First, so as not to be intimidated: the competition, stripped bare, is people doing similar work. Second, to learn to communicate her own value without lying. Megan stops selling "logos" and starts explaining what a good logo does for a business: it makes it recognizable, trustworthy, distinctive. She doesn't embellish; she translates. She raises her prices without inflating anything.

**For entrepreneurs:** good marketing doesn't dress things up as what they aren't; it explains what they are.

### At School: The Diploma and the Brand Name

**If you're studying as an adult:** George chose his MBA program because the university is prestigious. Halfway through, he realizes some professors barely prepare for class. He wonders whether it's worth what he's paying.

Stripped bare, an MBA is a set of classes, readings, classmates, and a piece of paper at the end. The paper opens some doors, and that has value. But what he really takes away is what he learns and the people he meets. George changes how he studies: he stops chasing only the grade and starts seeking out the good professors, the classmates who know their stuff, and the topics that help him at work.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie wants a pair of brand-name sneakers because "everyone in my class has them." Claire doesn't lecture her. She does the exercise with her: "What do those sneakers do that these don't?" Sophie thinks. "Nothing, but they're the ones everyone wears." Claire explains, at her level, that this is paying for the story. In the end they strike a deal: if she wants them, she can pay half from her savings. Sophie decides they're not worth it. She's learned something many adults don't know.

### In Your Family: The Sweet Sixteen

Lauren's niece is turning sixteen, and Lauren's sister wants to throw a huge sweet sixteen: a rented ballroom, a designer dress, dinner for three hundred guests. She asks Lauren for a loan.

Lauren wants to help, but she does the exercise. A sweet sixteen, stripped bare, is a night to celebrate someone you love. The story being bought with the ballroom and the banquet is something else: not looking bad in front of the family, proving you can. Lauren tells her sister she can give her the cake and help her plan, but she won't lend her money for the venue. Her sister is upset for a while.

In the end they throw a smaller party in grandma's backyard. Her niece dances all night. Years from now, no one will remember whether the venue was fancy. Everyone will remember that they were there.

### In Money and Health: The New Car

Andrew wants to replace his car, which is eight years old and runs fine. He sees a new one he loves. It would mean a five-year loan.

Before signing, he writes the stripped-bare definition: "A metal machine that would take me to the same job, with a bigger screen and a new-car smell." Then he writes down what story he's buying: feeling successful, feeling he's arrived. And he asks himself whether there's another way to get that. He decides to keep his car serviced, save the equivalent of the monthly payment for a year, and, if he still wants it at the end, buy it with a bigger down payment.

After a year he has solid savings, and his desire for the new car has faded a lot. He uses part of the money for a trip with Lauren. He leaves the rest as an emergency fund, just in case.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Nothing is worth anything."** No. The exercise deflates what's inflated, not what has real value.

**"It's wrong to enjoy luxury."** No. The Stoics weren't ascetics. Seneca was very rich. What they criticize is depending on luxury and measuring your own worth by it.

**"Brands, titles, and reputation don't matter."** They matter as tools. They don't matter as a measure of who you are.

## This Week's Exercise: The Stripped-Bare Definition

1. Choose three things that stir excessive desire, envy, or admiration in you: something you want to buy, someone you envy, a position you want.
2. Describe each one "stripped bare," in a single sentence, without value-laden adjectives. Example: "A luxury car: a metal machine with four wheels that would take me to the same job."
3. Next to it, write what story you're buying: status, security, belonging, admiration.
4. Ask yourself whether there's another way, cheaper or healthier, to get what you're really after.

Bonus: the next time you're angry with someone, describe what they did in one stripped-bare sentence, without interpretation. You'll see that many offenses lose half their weight.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** before any purchase larger than what you spend on a normal day, write its stripped-bare definition in a single sentence.
- **Intermediate:** apply the 72-hour rule: if something dazzles you, wait three days before buying it. If after stripping it bare and waiting you still want it, buy it without guilt.
- **Advanced:** strip bare something of yours you're proud of: your title, your house, your degree. Ask yourself what would remain of your worth as a person if you lost it tomorrow. That answer is what truly belongs to you.

## Key Ideas

- Describe things as they are, without the story that inflates them.
- Ask yourself how much you're paying for the thing and how much for the story.
- Wealth and fame are "indifferents": tools, not a measure of your worth.
- Strip bare what enslaves you; leave clothed what makes you better.

## Journaling Question

> **What in my life am I paying more for the story than for what it is?**
