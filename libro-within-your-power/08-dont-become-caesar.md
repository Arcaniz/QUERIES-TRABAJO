# Chapter 8. Don't Turn into Caesar

> *Take care not to turn into Caesar, not to be dyed purple, because it happens. Keep yourself simple, good, honest, serious, unpretentious, a friend of justice, reverent, kind, affectionate, and strong in your duty. Strive to remain the person philosophy wanted to make you.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.30 (paraphrase)

We all know someone like this. He was a down-to-earth coworker who loved a good joke. He got promoted. Six months later he doesn't say hello in the elevator, talks about "my team" as if it were his property, and no longer answers messages from his old colleagues. It's not that he was a bad person. It's that the position changed him.

Marcus Aurelius coined a word for this. In Greek he wrote something that could be translated as "to be Caesarified": to become Caesar, to let power transform you. And he wrote it not as a criticism of others, but as a warning to himself.

## The Context: The Man Who Had All the Power

Few people in history have held as much power as Marcus. He could sentence people to death, declare war, change laws. Before him, Rome had known emperors who went mad with power: Caligula, Nero, Domitian. Marcus knew those stories. He knew power doesn't only corrupt bad people; it also wears down good ones.

That's why, right after this warning, he writes a long portrait of his adoptive father, Antoninus Pius: an emperor who listened patiently, who didn't let himself be flattered, who deferred to people who knew more than he did, who could change his mind, who was content with little, and who treated everyone with the same courtesy. Marcus doesn't tell himself "be a great emperor." He tells himself: "be like him." One concrete role model is worth more than a thousand pieces of advice.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Power Is a Test of Character

There's a saying attributed to many people: "if you want to know what someone is really like, give them power." The Stoics would agree. Power, money, and success are "indifferents," but they're dangerous indifferents, because they tempt you to believe you're worth more than others.

Marcus lists the virtues he must protect: simplicity, goodness, honesty, seriousness, unpretentiousness, justice, reverence, kindness, affection, and steadfastness. Notice that he doesn't mention effectiveness, intelligence, or vision. For him, the danger of power wasn't becoming less capable but becoming a worse person.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Leadership as Service

For Marcus, power wasn't a prize but a responsibility. To govern was to serve. The idea echoes what today is called "servant leadership": the leader exists so the team can work well, not the other way around.

From here, a Stoic boss's question isn't "How do I get them to obey me?" but "What do my people need to do their job well?" And the sign that you're turning into Caesar is that you start asking the first question and forget the second.

Some concrete signs that power is dyeing you:
- No one contradicts you anymore, and that seems normal.
- It bothers you to be questioned.
- You surround yourself with people who agree with you.
- You talk more than you listen.
- You treat the CEO differently from the janitor.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: You Don't Have to Be the Boss to Turn into Caesar

It would be convenient to think this meditation is only for powerful people. It isn't. We all have small empires: the parent at home, the customer facing the waiter, the teacher facing the student, the expert facing the beginner, the person with more followers facing the one with fewer.

Every time you have a little power over someone, even a tiny amount, you're facing Marcus's test. How you treat someone who can't give you anything in return (the waiter, the delivery driver, the intern) says more about you than how you treat your boss.

And there's a subtler trap: moral power. The person who feels superior because they meditate, eat clean, or read philosophy. Yes: you can turn into Caesar through Stoicism, too.

**In a nutshell:** we all have a small empire; the test is how we treat those who can't give us anything back.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Tyrant's Teacher

Seneca's story is the best warning about power that Stoicism has to offer, precisely because it's the story of a failure. Seneca was tutor and adviser to the young Nero. He wrote him a treatise, *On Mercy*, to teach him that the best ruler is the one who masters himself and forgives. For the first few years, Nero's government was relatively moderate. Then the emperor turned cruel, murdered his own mother, and eventually ordered Seneca to take his own life.

What does this tell us? First, that even the best advice can't protect someone who doesn't want to listen. Second, that Seneca himself, in order to stay close to power, tolerated things that contradicted what he taught. It's an uncomfortable and valuable lesson: power can corrupt not only the person in charge, but also those close to the person in charge.

### Epictetus: What the Tyrant Can't Touch

Epictetus, from the other end of the social ladder, talks about power as seen from below. In one of his lessons, he imagines a tyrant threatening him: I'll put you in chains. "You'll chain my leg? Yes. But not even Zeus can overpower my will." For Epictetus, any boss's power, however great, stops at the border of each person's will.

The idea has two sides. For whoever holds power: there's a part of every person you will never control, so don't try to rule minds; try to win people over. For whoever is under someone else's power: your boss can decide your tasks, but not who you are.

### A Word from Antoninus: Equanimity

According to an ancient source, the *Historia Augusta*, when Antoninus Pius was dying, the officer of the guard asked him for the day's watchword, as was customary. Antoninus answered with one word: *aequanimitas*, equanimity. It was the last message of the man Marcus chose as his model for how to wield power.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologist Dacher Keltner has spent years studying what he calls the *power paradox*: many people gain power thanks to qualities like empathy and collaboration, but once they have it, those very qualities tend to weaken. Various experiments have observed that feeling powerful is associated with paying less attention to others and acting more impulsively, though the effects vary by person and context.

That science confirms Marcus's intuition shouldn't surprise us. What should give us pause is that no one is immune: whoever believes power won't change them has probably already started to change.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** This chapter is for bosses. I'm not in charge of anyone.

**Author:** Are you sure? Does no one depend on you, at home, at work, in your family? Have you never dealt with a waiter, a delivery driver, or a clerk behind a counter? We all have small empires. Marcus's test isn't for emperors; it's for anyone who ever has a little power over someone else, even for five minutes.

**Skeptic:** If I'm kind and down-to-earth as a boss, they'll see me as weak.

**Author:** Notice that Marcus's list includes "strong in your duty." Simplicity isn't weakness. Antoninus, his model, was an emperor who listened, who changed his mind when given good reasons, and who didn't let himself be flattered. No one considered him weak. Firmness shows in your decisions, not in your tone of voice.

**Skeptic:** But power sometimes demands toughness. You have to fire people, you have to push them.

**Author:** Yes, and Marcus made very hard decisions: he passed sentences, he waged war. The chapter doesn't ask you to avoid difficult decisions, but not to become someone who enjoys power over others. You can fire someone with respect. You can push without humiliating. A hard decision doesn't oblige you to be cruel in how you carry it out.

**Skeptic:** And if power has already changed me?

**Author:** The fact that you're asking is a good sign. People who have been fully Caesarified don't ask. Marcus's remedy is simple and humble: find a role model, review your behavior, and ask someone you trust to tell you the truth. And above all, listen to the answer without defending yourself.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Power at Home

When Lauren left her job for a year to care for their newborn son, Andrew became the only one earning money. Without realizing it, he started deciding alone how money was spent, asking her to account for grocery receipts, saying "my money."

Lauren pointed it out to him one night: "Ever since you're the only one earning, you talk to me like I'm your employee." Andrew felt attacked at first. Later he admitted she was right. He had turned into Caesar in his own home.

Couples have many sources of power: the one who earns more, the one who knows more about a subject, the one who gets angrier, the one who loves less. The meditation asks you to look at what power you have over your partner and how you use it. In a healthy relationship, power is shared or put at the service of both.

### At Work: The Promotion

At his new job, Rick is finally promoted to lead a team of six. In his first week he notices that people laugh at his jokes even when they aren't funny, and no one contradicts him in meetings.

He sets three rules so he won't turn into Caesar:
1. **A devil's advocate in every meeting.** He asks a different person each week to critique his proposal.
2. **Eat lunch with the team, not with the other managers.** At least once a week.
3. **The Antoninus portrait.** He writes down the name of the best boss he ever had, the one he worked for at twenty-eight, and three things that boss did well. Every month he checks whether he does them.

### In Business: Success Goes to Your Head

Megan's business is doing better. She hires two designers. One day she catches herself yelling at one of them over a small mistake, in front of a client. That night she can't sleep.

A growing business brings two temptations: believing you did it all yourself, and starting to treat people as tools. Megan decides to apologize to the designer the next day, in front of the same client if possible. It's hard. It's the most Stoic thing she's done all year.

**For entrepreneurs:** as you grow, your character flaws are multiplied by the number of people who depend on you. One bad day for the owner becomes the company culture.

### At School: The One Who Knows More

**If you're studying as an adult:** after retaking statistics, George becomes the one in the group who understands it best. He starts explaining it to others. And one day he catches himself correcting a classmate impatiently: "That's obvious, isn't it?"

He remembers Alexander the grammarian, whom Marcus thanks in Book I for teaching him not to correct others with scorn, but to offer the right form tactfully, almost in passing. George changes his approach: instead of "that's wrong," he asks, "What if we look at it this way?" His classmate feels free to keep asking questions.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie is class representative. At home she proudly tells how she scolded a classmate for misbehaving. Claire asks her: "And how do you think he felt?" She explains that being class rep means helping the group, not bossing it around. It's her daughter's first lesson in leadership, and the most important one.

### In Your Family: The Sister Who Took Charge

When Megan's father died, she became the sister who "knows about numbers" and started handling the estate and the family paperwork. Over time, without realizing it, she began deciding alone: what to sell, what to keep, when to pay what. Her siblings stopped weighing in.

One day her younger sister tells her: "Since Dad died, you're not my sister anymore; you're the administrator." It hurts Megan, but she recognizes it's true. She had turned into Caesar in her own family. From then on, every estate decision is made in a meeting with everyone, even if it takes longer. She loses efficiency and gets her siblings back.

### In Money and Health: Whoever Pays Is in Charge

Lauren has been earning more than Andrew since her promotion. At first nothing changed. But one day, in an argument about a vacation, it slips out: "In the end, I'm the one paying." Andrew goes quiet and sleeps on the couch that night.

Lauren remembers what happened to them the other way around, when Andrew was the only one earning, as we described at the start of this very chapter. She realizes money is a form of power and that she'd fallen into the same trap. The next day she apologizes, and they agree on something: both their incomes belong to the household, and big decisions are made together, no matter who contributed more.

## Author's Note

I was given a promotion with more responsibility, more pressure, and the same salary. The first temptation was to complain; the second, more dangerous, was to unload that pressure on the people who now depended on me. This chapter forced me to separate two things. What wasn't within my power: the company's decision about my salary. What was: preparing, with data, the conversation to ask for what was fair; organizing my time better; and above all, not turning my frustration into my team's bad day. I discovered that you can claim what's owed to you without becoming the boss you never wanted to have.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Don't seek power or success."** No. Marcus governed energetically and accepted his responsibility. The problem isn't having power, but letting power have you.

**"A good leader is never firm."** No. The list of virtues includes "strong in your duty." Simplicity isn't weakness.

**"The Stoic is humble because he's worthless."** No. Stoic humility isn't self-contempt; it's knowing you're equal to others in what matters most, even if you occupy a different place.

## This Week's Exercise: Your Antoninus Portrait

1. Think of the person with power who has treated you best in your life: a boss, a teacher, a coach, a relative.
2. Write down five concrete things they did. Not adjectives ("they were good") but behaviors ("they asked for my opinion before deciding").
3. Identify an area where you have power over someone: children, employees, partner, students, clients, suppliers.
4. For one week, imitate one of those five behaviors every day.
5. At the end, ask someone you have power over: "Is there anything I do that makes you feel small?" Listen without defending yourself.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** for one week, greet three people who can't do anything for you by name and with real attention.
- **Intermediate:** in your next important meeting or conversation, speak last, after everyone else has given their opinion. Notice what you learn.
- **Advanced:** ask someone you trust, over whom you have some kind of power, to tell you frankly one thing you do that makes them feel small. Listen without defending yourself and thank them. Then change that behavior for a month.

## Key Ideas

- Power is a test of character.
- We all have a small empire: someone we have power over.
- Treat people who can do something for you and people who can't the same way.
- Find a concrete role model and someone who will tell you the truth.

On that day, remember the emperor who had all the power in the world and wrote to himself, in secret, not to let himself be dyed purple.
