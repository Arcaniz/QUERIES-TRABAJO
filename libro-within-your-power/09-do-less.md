# Chapter 9. Do Less, but Do What Matters

> *"If you want peace of mind, do few things," says the wise man. But isn't it better to do what's necessary, what reason demands of a social being, and in the way it demands? That brings the peace of doing things well and the peace of doing fewer of them. Most of what we say and do isn't necessary; cut it, and you'll have more time and less turmoil. So at every moment, ask yourself: is this necessary? And cut not only unnecessary actions but unnecessary thoughts, so that pointless actions don't follow them.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, IV.24 (paraphrase)

Open your calendar for the week. Count the meetings. Now count how many of last week's meetings actually changed anything. Open your inbox and count the unread messages. Open your to-do list and count the items that have been sitting there for over a month.

If you're like most adults, the answer is the same every time: too many. We're busy all the time, but not necessarily with what matters. Marcus Aurelius, who had an empire to run, found the question that cuts through the tangle: **is this necessary?**

## The Context: Correcting a Wise Man

The line Marcus quotes at the start, "do few things if you want peace of mind," comes from the philosopher Democritus. Marcus respects it, and then improves on it. The goal is to do the necessary things, however many that turns out to be. An emperor couldn't do few things: he had to hear cases, write laws, lead campaigns. But he could stop doing what served no purpose.

The distinction matters. "Doing little" can just be laziness. "Doing what's necessary" is a choice. And for Marcus, what's necessary is defined by two things: what reason requires, and what your role in the community requires.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Necessary, According to Reason

For the Stoics, every person plays roles: son or daughter, parent, citizen, professional, friend. Epictetus said every role comes with duties. What's necessary is what your roles ask of you. Everything else is noise.

Marcus's question works as a moral filter. Does one of my roles ask this of me? Does it help anyone? Or am I doing it out of vanity, fear, habit, or because I don't know how to say no?

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary is what your role asks of you; the rest is decoration.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Essentialism, Two Thousand Years Early

There are whole shelves of productivity books saying the same thing in different words: the 80/20 rule (20 percent of what you do produces 80 percent of your results), minimalism, "no" as a strategic tool. Marcus got there in one line.

The practical version goes like this: every "yes" you give to something unnecessary is a "no" to something necessary. Say yes to the pointless meeting, and you've said no to an hour of real work. Say yes to the social obligation you don't want, and you've said no to dinner with your partner.

And Marcus adds something almost no productivity book mentions: **unnecessary thoughts**. The worries on a loop, the imaginary conversations, the replaying of something someone said last week. They're invisible tasks clogging up your mental calendar.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Who Gets to Decide What's Necessary?

This one's a serious objection. What's unnecessary to me may be necessary to someone else. The meeting I think is pointless may matter to my boss. The family get-together that bores me may mean everything to my mother.

Marcus's answer is tucked into the phrase "what reason demands of a social being." What's necessary isn't what I feel like doing. It's what, thought through calmly, serves the whole. Sometimes going to the family dinner that bores you is necessary, because being a son or daughter asks it of you. Sometimes skipping the meeting is what's necessary, because your actual work is waiting.

The test is function, not comfort. And it takes some nerve to tell "I don't want to" apart from "it isn't necessary."

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary is whatever serves, which isn't always what you like.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Life Isn't Short; We Waste It

In *On the Shortness of Life*, Seneca writes a line that could have been written for us: it isn't that we have too little time, but that we waste so much of it. Life is long enough, he says, if we use it well. But when it gets spent on luxuries, on carelessness, on busywork that leads nowhere, we wake up to find it's gone and we never actually lived it.

Seneca describes the people he calls the *preoccupied*: people who rush from one obligation to the next, who take care of everyone except themselves, who are waiting for "retirement" to start living. And he asks, a little dryly, who promised them they'd make it that far.

### Epictetus: Be Willing to Look Foolish

Epictetus has advice for anyone who can't say no: if you want to make progress, be willing to look foolish and uninformed about outside matters. Don't pretend to know everything or be part of everything. If people start thinking you're important, be suspicious of yourself.

Saying no to what's unnecessary costs something. Sometimes you'll come across as rude, uncommitted, or out of the loop. Epictetus asks you to pay that price. Whoever tries to please everyone ends up with no time for what matters.

### What Science Says Today

Researcher Gloria Mark has spent years studying how people work at screens. One of her most widely cited findings is that after an interruption, it can take people quite a while to get fully back into what they were doing, around twenty minutes or more in some studies. Her research has also found that our attention on screens is jumping from thing to thing faster and faster.

Other research on multitasking suggests that, most of the time, we aren't doing two things at once. We're switching rapidly between them, and every switch has a cost. In Marcus's terms: every unnecessary thought and action eats up not only its own time but also pollutes the time you meant for what's necessary.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Is this necessary?" is an easy question when you don't have a boss. I can't just cancel meetings.

**Author:** Not all of them, no. But a lot of people find they can do more than they thought: ask for an agenda, suggest an email instead of a meeting, ask for the notes. And even if you can't cancel anything on your calendar, you can always prune what's going on inside your head: unnecessary thoughts, replaying yesterday's meeting, imaginary arguments. Marcus insists on that second kind of pruning because it's the one that's always within your power.

**Skeptic:** What's unnecessary to me might matter to someone else. Isn't this just selfish?

**Author:** It would be if "necessary" meant "whatever I like." But Marcus defines it by what your role as a social being calls for. Sometimes what's necessary is going to the family dinner that bores you, because your mother needs you there. The question doesn't excuse you from your duties. It frees you from what you do out of habit, vanity, or fear.

**Skeptic:** If I say no to everything, I'll end up with no friends and no opportunities.

**Author:** Saying no to everything is just as thoughtless as saying yes to everything. You take it case by case. And here's something you discover with practice: when you say no to what's unnecessary, your "yes" starts to mean more. People know that if you agree to something, you'll actually show up.

**Skeptic:** What about downtime? Is watching a show unnecessary?

**Author:** Rest is necessary. Watching a show you love, with your partner, after a rough day, might be exactly what your life needs that night. What isn't necessary is spending three hours flipping between streaming apps without watching anything, or watching something you don't even like just to avoid thinking. The activity itself hardly matters. What matters is whether you chose it or it just pulled you along.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Packed Calendar

Lauren and Andrew's week is full: their son's activities, birthday parties, work obligations, dinner at his mother's, the gym, groceries. They hit Sunday exhausted, without having had a single conversation longer than ten minutes.

They sit down with the month's calendar and go through it, commitment by commitment: is this necessary? It turns out they're going to three birthday parties for classmates of their son's they barely know, that Andrew plays in a rec basketball league twice a week though he only enjoys one of the two nights, and that Lauren agreed to organize a work event because she didn't have the nerve to say no.

They cut four commitments. With the time they free up, they schedule something that's never been on their calendar: dinner for just the two of them, every other week. Sometimes you have to write down what's necessary, because what's unnecessary will find its own way onto the calendar.

### At Work: Meetings and Email

Rick runs the numbers: he spends fourteen hours a week in meetings. In half of them, he doesn't say a word. In a third of them, nothing gets decided.

He applies Marcus's question. Before accepting any meeting, he asks for an agenda. If there's no agenda, he asks whether it could be handled over email. He drops two weekly meetings where he just listens and asks for the notes instead. He checks email three times a day instead of every time a notification pops up.

Within a month he's gotten back six hours a week. He uses them for the things nobody asks for but everybody needs: planning, thinking, mentoring his team.

**For leaders:** the most Stoic meeting is the one that never gets called because it didn't need to happen.

### In Business: The Client Who Isn't Worth It

Megan realizes that 20 percent of her clients cause 80 percent of her headaches: endless revisions, late payments, constant complaints. And they barely bring in any money.

Letting them go scares her; she's afraid of losing income. But she asks: is this client necessary? She runs the numbers and finds that dropping the three worst would cost her 10 percent of her revenue and give her back 30 percent of her time. With that time, she could land a better client.

She raises her rates for those three. Two of them walk. The one who stays pays what her work is worth.

**For entrepreneurs:** not every client is a good client, not every product deserves a spot in the catalog, and not every opportunity is really an opportunity. Saying no is a business decision.

### At School: Studying What Counts

**If you're studying as an adult:** George doesn't have much time. He works, he has a family, and he's in school. At first he tried to read every assignment, underline every page, and write perfect summaries. He burned himself out.

He applies the question: what do I actually need to do to learn this well? He identifies the core concepts in each course, practices with problems instead of rereading, and accepts that he can't read everything with the same care. His grades go up while his study hours go down.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie is signed up for Spanish, swimming, piano, and robotics. She's worn out and cranky. Claire and Sophie go through the activities together, and Claire asks which ones Sophie actually enjoys. They keep two. Sophie gets her afternoons back for playing, which, when you're ten, is necessary.

### In Your Family: The Go-To Guy

Rick's family has a habit of handing him every piece of paperwork: his aunt's property tax payment, his cousin's tax return, his brother's driver's license renewal. "You know how to do that stuff." Rick never says no, so he spends his weekends running other people's errands.

He asks himself: is this necessary? Some of it is: his mother really does need help with her paperwork. Some of it isn't: his cousin and his brother can do it themselves. So he offers something different. Instead of doing it for them, he walks them through it once. They grumble at first. A year later, they don't need him, and Rick has his Saturdays back.

### In Money and Health: The Subscriptions

Megan looks at her bank statement and finds eleven subscriptions: three streaming services, two music apps, a meditation app she hasn't opened since March, design software she no longer uses, a digital magazine, an online fitness program, and two services she doesn't even remember signing up for.

She puts Marcus's question to each one: is this necessary? She cancels seven and saves about $90 a month. But she also notices something else. With fewer platforms, she spends less time picking what to watch and more time doing other things. Pruning her spending turned out to be pruning her time, too.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Do the bare minimum."** No. Sometimes what's necessary is a lot. Marcus worked incredibly hard. The point is cutting what's superfluous, not doing as little as possible.

**"Say no to everything."** No. Saying no to everything is just another way of not thinking. You take it case by case.

**"Necessary means whatever's convenient for me."** No. What's necessary includes what's yours to do for other people, even when it isn't convenient.

## This Week's Exercise: The Pruning

1. **Calendar:** go through next week's commitments. Put an X next to the ones that aren't necessary. Cancel or hand off at least one.
2. **To-do list:** delete every item that's been sitting there for more than a month with no consequences for not doing it. If it really mattered, it'll come back.
3. **Thoughts:** when you catch yourself replaying something (a conversation, a fear, a slight), ask quietly: "Is this thought necessary?" If it isn't, go back to what you were doing.
4. **The missing necessary thing:** write down one necessary thing you've been putting off. Put it on your calendar with a date and time.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** every morning, pick the three necessary things for the day and write them down before you open your email.
- **Intermediate:** practice one kind "no" a week. Turn down a commitment, a meeting, or a favor that isn't necessary, without a long explanation.
- **Advanced:** track your time for three days, writing down what you did in half-hour blocks. At the end, mark what was necessary and what wasn't. Figure out how many hours a week go to the unnecessary, and decide what you'd do with half of them.

## Key Ideas

- At every moment, ask: is this necessary?
- Every yes to the unnecessary is a no to what matters.
- Prune unnecessary thoughts, too.
- What's necessary is defined by your role toward others, not by your comfort.

## Journaling Question

> **What am I doing only because I'm afraid to say no?**

---

Open your calendar again. The meetings, the emails, the to-dos are all still there. But now you've got a three-word question that an emperor used to run an empire. You don't need to do less to have peace of mind. You need to do what matters. The rest can wait, or it can disappear.
