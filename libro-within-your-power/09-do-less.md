# Chapter 9. Do Less, but Do What's Necessary

> *"If you want peace of mind, do few things," the wise man says. But wouldn't it be better to do what's necessary, what the reason of a social being requires, and in the way it requires? That brings not only the peace of doing things well, but the peace of doing few. Because most of what we say and do isn't necessary; cut it out and you'll have more time and less turmoil. So at every moment, ask yourself: is this necessary? And it isn't only unnecessary actions we should cut, but unnecessary thoughts too, so that pointless actions don't follow them.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, IV.24 (paraphrase)

Open your calendar for the week. Count the meetings. Now count how many of last week's meetings actually changed anything. Open your inbox and count the unread emails. Open your to-do list and count the tasks that have been sitting there for more than a month.

If you're like most adults, the answer is the same in every case: too many. We live busy, but not necessarily doing what matters. Marcus Aurelius, who had an empire to run, found the question that cuts through the tangle: **is this necessary?**

## The Context: Correcting a Wise Man

The line Marcus quotes at the beginning, "do few things if you want peace of mind," comes from the philosopher Democritus. Marcus respects it but corrects it. The point isn't to do few things, but to do the necessary ones. An emperor couldn't do few things: he had to judge cases, write laws, lead campaigns. But he could stop doing what served no purpose.

The distinction matters. "Doing little" can be laziness. "Doing what's necessary" is a decision. And what's necessary, for Marcus, is defined by two things: what reason requires and what your role in the community requires.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Necessary According to Reason

For the Stoics, each person has roles: son or daughter, parent, citizen, professional, friend. Epictetus said each role comes with duties. What's necessary is what your roles ask of you. The rest is noise.

Marcus's question ("is this necessary?") works as a moral filter: does one of my roles ask this of me? Does it serve anyone? Or am I doing it out of vanity, fear, habit, or not knowing how to say no?

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary is what your role asks of you; the rest is decoration.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Essentialism Before Essentialism

Today there are whole books on productivity saying the same thing in other words: the 80/20 rule (20 percent of what you do produces 80 percent of the results), minimalism, "no" as a strategic tool. Marcus had it in one line.

The practical reading is this: every "yes" you give to something unnecessary is a "no" you give to something necessary. Say yes to the pointless meeting and you say no to an hour of deep work. Say yes to the social obligation you don't want and you say no to dinner with your partner.

And Marcus adds something almost no productivity book mentions: **unnecessary thoughts**. Recurring worries, imaginary conversations, rehashing what someone said a week ago. They're invisible tasks that fill up your mental calendar.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Who Decides What's Necessary?

The objection is a serious one: what's unnecessary for me may be necessary for someone else. The meeting that seems pointless to me may be important to my boss. The family gathering that bores me may be vital to my mother.

Marcus has an implicit answer in the phrase "what the reason of a social being requires." What's necessary isn't what I feel like doing, but what, thought through calmly, serves the whole. Sometimes going to the family dinner that bores you is necessary, because your role as a son or daughter asks it of you. Sometimes skipping the meeting is what's necessary, because your real work is waiting.

The criterion isn't comfort. It's function. And it takes courage to tell "I don't want to" apart from "it isn't necessary."

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary isn't what you like, but what serves; and sometimes what serves isn't what you like.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: It Isn't That Life Is Short

In *On the Shortness of Life*, Seneca writes a line that seems written for our age: it isn't that we have little time, but that we waste a lot of it. Life is long enough, he says, if it's used well. But when it's spent on luxuries, on carelessness, on busywork that serves no purpose, we realize it's gone without our having lived it.

Seneca describes those he calls the *preoccupied*: people who rush from one obligation to the next, who attend to everyone but themselves, who wait for "retirement" to start living. And he asks, with irony, who guarantees they'll live that long.

### Epictetus: Be Content to Look Foolish

Epictetus offers advice that comes in handy for people who can't say no: if you want to make progress, be content to seem foolish and ignorant about external things. Don't pretend to know everything or be part of everything. If some people think you're important, distrust yourself.

Saying no to what's unnecessary has a cost: sometimes you'll seem rude, uncommitted, or out of the loop. Epictetus asks you to accept that cost. Whoever wants to please everyone ends up with no time for what matters.

### What Science Says Today

Researcher Gloria Mark has spent years studying how we work at screens. One of her most cited findings is that, after an interruption, it can take many people quite a while (in some studies, around twenty minutes or more) to fully return to the original task. Her research has also found that our attention on screens shifts focus faster and faster.

Other research on multitasking suggests that, in most cases, we don't do two things at once; we switch rapidly between them, and every switch has a cost. In Marcus's terms: every unnecessary thought and action not only takes up its own time but contaminates the time of what's necessary.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Is this necessary?" is easy to ask when you don't have a boss. I can't cancel meetings.

**Author:** Not all of them, true. But many people discover they can do more than they thought: ask for an agenda, propose an email instead of a meeting, ask for the notes. And even if you can't cancel anything external, you can always prune the internal: unnecessary thoughts, rehashing yesterday's meeting, imaginary conversations. Marcus insists on that second pruning because it's the one that's always within your power.

**Skeptic:** What's unnecessary for me may be important to someone else. Isn't that selfish?

**Author:** It would be if "necessary" meant "what I like." But Marcus defines it by what your role as a social being requires. Sometimes what's necessary is going to the family dinner that bores you, because your mother needs it. The question doesn't free you from your duties; it frees you from what you do out of inertia, vanity, or fear.

**Skeptic:** If I say no to everything, I'll end up with no friends and no opportunities.

**Author:** Saying no to everything is as thoughtless as saying yes to everything. The question is case by case. And there's something you discover with practice: when you say no to what's unnecessary, your "yes" is worth more. People know that if you accept, it's because you'll really be there.

**Skeptic:** What about downtime? Is watching a show unnecessary?

**Author:** Rest is necessary. Watching a show you enjoy, with your partner, after a hard day, may be exactly what your life needs that night. What isn't necessary is spending three hours switching between streaming apps without watching anything, or watching something you don't even like just to avoid thinking. The difference isn't in the activity, but in whether you chose it or it dragged you along.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Packed Calendar

Lauren and Andrew have a full week: their son's activities, birthday parties, work obligations, dinner with his mother, the gym, groceries. They reach Sunday exhausted, without having had a single conversation longer than ten minutes.

They sit down with the month's calendar and ask, commitment by commitment: is this necessary? They discover they're going to three birthday parties for classmates of their son's whom they barely know, that Andrew plays in a rec basketball league twice a week though he only enjoys one of the games, and that Lauren agreed to organize a work event because she didn't dare say no.

They cut four commitments. With the time they free up, they schedule something that was never on the calendar before: dinner, just the two of them, every other week. Sometimes you have to write down what's necessary, because what's unnecessary shows up on its own.

### At Work: Meetings and Email

Rick does the math: he spends fourteen hours a week in meetings. In half of them, he doesn't speak. In a third of them, nothing is decided.

He applies Marcus's question. He asks for an agenda before accepting any meeting. If there's no agenda, he asks whether it could be handled by email. He stops attending two weekly meetings where he only listens, and asks for the notes instead. He checks his email three times a day rather than every time a notification pops up.

In a month he gets back six hours a week. He uses them for what no one asks for but everyone needs: planning, thinking, mentoring his team.

**For leaders:** the most Stoic meeting is the one that's never called because it wasn't needed.

### In Business: The Client Who Isn't Worth It

Megan realizes that 20 percent of her clients cause 80 percent of her problems: endless revisions, late payments, constant complaints. And they bring in little money.

It's hard, because she's afraid of losing income. But she asks: is this client necessary? She runs the numbers and finds that if she let go of the three worst, she'd lose 10 percent of her revenue but get back 30 percent of her time. With that time she could land a better client.

She raises her rates for those three. Two leave. The one who stays pays what her work is worth.

**For entrepreneurs:** not every client is a good client, not every product deserves to stay in the catalog, and not every opportunity is an opportunity. Saying no is a business decision.

### At School: Studying What Matters

**If you're studying as an adult:** George has little time: he works, he has a family, and he's in school. At first he tried to read everything assigned, underline every page, and write perfect summaries. He wore himself out.

He applies the question: what's necessary to learn this well? He identifies the core concepts of each course, practices with problems instead of rereading, and accepts that he can't read everything with the same depth. His grades improve while he studies fewer hours.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie is signed up for Spanish, swimming, piano, and robotics. She's tired and cranky. Claire and Sophie go over the activities together and ask the girl which ones she truly enjoys. They keep two. Sophie gets her afternoons back for playing, which, at ten, is something necessary.

### In Your Family: The Usual Favor

Rick's family has a habit of asking him to handle every bit of paperwork: his aunt's property tax payment, his cousin's tax return, his brother's driver's license renewal. "Since you know how to do that stuff." Rick never says no, and he spends his weekends doing other people's errands.

He asks himself: is this necessary? Some of it is: his mother really does need help with her paperwork. Some of it isn't: his cousin and his brother can do it themselves. He offers them something different: instead of doing the task for them, he shows them how to do it once. At first they grumble. A year later, they don't need him, and Rick gets his Saturdays back.

### In Money and Health: The Subscriptions

Megan looks at her bank statement and finds eleven subscriptions: three streaming services, two music apps, a meditation app she hasn't opened since March, design software she no longer uses, a digital magazine, an online fitness program, and two services she doesn't even remember signing up for.

She applies Marcus's question to each one: is this necessary? She cancels seven. She saves about $90 a month. But she discovers something else: with fewer platforms, she spends less time choosing what to watch and more time doing other things. Pruning her spending was also pruning her time.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Do the bare minimum."** No. Sometimes what's necessary is a lot. Marcus worked intensely. The point is to remove the superfluous, not to do little.

**"Say no to everything."** No. Saying no to everything is another way of not thinking. The question is case by case.

**"Necessary means what's convenient for me."** No. What's necessary includes what's yours to do for others, even when it isn't convenient.

## This Week's Exercise: The Pruning

1. **Calendar:** go through your commitments for next week. Mark with an X the ones that aren't necessary. Cancel or delegate at least one.
2. **To-do list:** delete every task that's been sitting there for more than a month without any consequence for not doing it. If it was important, it'll come back.
3. **Thoughts:** when you catch yourself rehashing something (a conversation, a fear, an offense), quietly ask: "Is this thought necessary?" If it isn't, return to what you were doing.
4. **The necessary thing that's missing:** write down one necessary thing you've been putting off. Put it on your calendar with a date and time.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** each morning, choose the three necessary things for the day and write them down before opening your email.
- **Intermediate:** practice one kind "no" a week: turn down a commitment, a meeting, or a favor that isn't necessary, without lengthy explanations.
- **Advanced:** audit your time for three days: write down in half-hour blocks what you did. At the end, mark what was necessary and what wasn't. Calculate how many hours a week go to the unnecessary, and decide what you'd do with half of them.

## Key Ideas

- Ask yourself at every moment: is this necessary?
- Every yes to the unnecessary is a no to what matters.
- Prune unnecessary thoughts, too.
- What's necessary is defined by your role toward others, not by your comfort.

## Journaling Question

> **What am I doing out of fear of saying no?**

---

Open your calendar again. The meetings, the emails, the to-dos are still there. But now you have a three-word question that an emperor used to run an empire. You don't need to do less to have peace of mind. You need to do what matters. The rest can wait, or it can disappear.
