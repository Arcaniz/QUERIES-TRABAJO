# Chapter 10. The Future Isn't Yours

> *Don't let the future rattle you. You'll meet it, if you have to, armed with the same reason you're using right now to handle the present.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VII.8 (paraphrase)

> *Don't let the picture of your whole life crush you. Don't start tallying up every hardship that might be waiting for you. Instead, as each one comes, ask: what here is actually unbearable? You'll be embarrassed to answer. Remember that neither the future nor the past weighs on you, only the present, and the present gets smaller once you draw a line around it.*
> — *Meditations*, VIII.36 (paraphrase)

It's 3:14 a.m. and you can't sleep. Your mind is running the numbers: what if I get laid off, what if we can't make tuition, what if the business never gets off the ground, what if this pain in my chest is something serious, what if my partner doesn't love me anymore, what if my kids head down the wrong path. One thought leads to the next. By 4:00 you've lived through ten disasters that haven't happened.

Morning comes, and none of it happened. You're just tired.

Seneca wrote in one of his letters that we suffer more in imagination than in reality. Marcus Aurelius reached the same conclusion on a different sleepless night.

## The Context: Ruling Without Knowing What Came Next

Marcus ruled during one of Rome's most uncertain stretches. The plague came and went. The borders kept shifting. He never knew if the next campaign would be his last, if his health would hold up, or if his son Commodus, his heir, would be ready for the job. History says he wasn't.

Marcus couldn't control that future. What he could do was remember that whenever it arrived, he'd face it with the same tools he had today: reason, character, experience. And that the weight pressing on him at night wasn't coming from the present. It came from adding up every problem that might ever happen.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Trust Your Tools

VII.8 says something simple and deeply reassuring: your future self will have the same tools your present self has. If you could handle what came at you today, you'll be able to handle tomorrow. Not because things are guaranteed to go well, but because you can prepare to meet whatever comes as thoughtfully as you're able.

Think about it this way: you've survived 100 percent of your worst days so far. You didn't handle all of them well, but you made it here. That's a fact, not a motivational poster.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Break Up the Load

VIII.36 hands you a concrete technique: don't look at your whole life at once. When you see every hardship stacked together (the thirty-year mortgage, the kids' college, retirement, your parents' health), the weight is unbearable. But you'll never have to carry all of it at the same time. You only ever carry today's share.

Marcus offers a question: **what is unbearable right now?** Not this year, not this decade. Right now. The answer is almost always: nothing. You're sitting down. You're breathing. You can do something.

It's the same logic behind "one day at a time" in recovery programs, and behind every marathoner's advice: don't think about mile 26; think about the next one.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: So We Shouldn't Plan?

If the future shouldn't bother us, does that mean we don't save, don't make a will, don't plan for retirement? That would be absurd. And Marcus doesn't say that.

The difference is between **planning** and **worrying**. Planning is thinking about the future so you can act today: opening a savings account, getting a checkup, updating your résumé. Worrying is thinking about the future without doing anything: chewing on the same thing at 3 a.m.

The premeditation of adversity from Chapter 2 was useful because it ended in preparation. Worry is useless because it ends in more worry.

Here's a quick test. If, while you're thinking about the future, you can write down something concrete to do today, you're planning. If all you can write is another "what if...?", you're worrying.

**In a nutshell:** planning is thinking ahead so you can act today; worrying is living in a future you never act on.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Hope and Fear Are Handcuffed Together

In one of his letters, Seneca quotes the Stoic Hecato: you'll stop being afraid when you stop hoping. Then he explains the image. Hope and fear walk along together, like a prisoner and the guard he's chained to. Both are looking ahead. Anyone anxiously hoping for something also fears not getting it; anyone afraid of something also hopes it won't come. The root of both, Seneca says, is that we don't settle into the present. We send our thoughts out far ahead of us.

That doesn't mean living without goals. It means not hanging your peace of mind on a future you don't control. You can work toward something with everything you've got without letting your calm today depend on getting it.

In another letter, Seneca writes the line we mentioned at the top of the chapter: more things frighten us than actually crush us, and we suffer more often in imagination than in reality. His advice is not to run out to meet our troubles. Many of the things we dread may never come, and the ones that do don't need to hurt in advance.

### Epictetus: The Singer Who Shakes

Epictetus notices that whenever he sees someone anxious, he asks himself what that person wants. If they weren't wanting something outside their control, they wouldn't be anxious. He uses the example of a musician. Singing alone, he's perfectly relaxed. Walking onstage, he trembles. Why? Because he doesn't just want to sing well, which is up to him. He also wants applause, which isn't.

In this reading, anxiety is almost always the sign of a desire parked in the wrong place.

### What Science Says Today

A 2019 study by researchers Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman asked people with generalized anxiety disorder to write down their worries over a period of days and then check which ones actually came true. The vast majority, about 90 percent, didn't. And in many of the cases where something did happen, it turned out better than they'd feared. It's a small study, but it's a perfect illustration of Seneca's point.

There's also a technique used in some treatments for excessive worry, built on the work of psychologist Thomas Borkovec: set aside a fixed time each day for worrying, and postpone any worry that crops up outside that window. That's the "planning hour" this chapter's exercise suggests.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Not worrying about the future is irresponsible. If I don't worry, I won't save.

**Author:** That's why the chapter separates worrying from planning. Saving *is* planning: a concrete action, today, with the future in mind. Worrying is spinning your wheels about the future without doing anything. In fact, worry often gets in the way of saving, because the fear gets so big that we'd rather not even open the bank statement.

**Skeptic:** But some futures really are scary: getting old, getting sick, the economy.

**Author:** Absolutely. The Stoics never pretended danger doesn't exist. Seneca recommended imagining it on purpose, through the premeditation of adversity. But Stoic imagination has an endpoint: it ends in getting ready. The 3 a.m. version doesn't end in anything. It just circles back on itself. The question is whether your thinking about the future leaves you better prepared or just more exhausted.

**Skeptic:** "You'll meet it with the same tools you have today" isn't much comfort when I feel like I don't have any.

**Author:** That's an honest objection. But think about what you've already come through: losses, crises, changes you were sure would break you. You got through them, not always gracefully, but you made it here. Those are your tools, even if you can't feel them today. And if you truly feel you have none, maybe what you need isn't philosophy but professional help. Asking for it is another way of arming yourself.

**Skeptic:** And hope? Seneca says to stop hoping. That's bleak.

**Author:** He's talking about anxious hope, the kind that hangs your peace on an outcome you can't control. He's not asking you to give up on goals or dreams. He's asking you not to let your calm today hinge on them coming true. You can give something everything you've got and still be at peace if it doesn't happen.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Afraid It Will End

After the crisis in Chapter 4, Lauren and Andrew are still together, and therapy has helped. But Lauren lives with a low hum of fear: what if it happens again? What if one day he decides he's done? Every time Andrew walks in looking serious, her mind jumps to the worst.

Her therapist tells her something very Stoic: "You can't love him well today if you're busy being afraid of losing him tomorrow." Lauren starts practicing the VIII.36 question: what's unbearable right now? Right now, Andrew is in the kitchen making coffee. Right now, the marriage is good.

There are no guarantees in love. But fearing the end doesn't protect a relationship. It wears it down. The only thing that does protect it is how the two of them treat each other today.

### At Work: The Rumor Mill

At Rick's company, there are rumors of layoffs. Nobody knows anything. Everybody speculates in the hallways. Productivity tanks because everyone spends the day running the numbers.

Rick makes two lists. **Planning:** update his résumé, figure out how long his savings would last, call two contacts. He knocks it out in one afternoon. **Worrying:** everything else. He decides to stay out of the hallway rumors.

While his coworkers spend three weeks in agony, Rick works. In the end, the layoffs don't touch him. But if they had, he'd have been ready. Either way, the same strategy covered him.

### In Business: Debt and Scenarios

Megan took out a loan to buy equipment. One slow month and she's picturing repossession, bankruptcy, and the shame of telling her family.

Her accountant shows her a tool Marcus would have loved: three scenarios. She writes down what happens if the year is good, average, or bad. For the bad one, she spells out what she'd do: cut costs, renegotiate the loan, sell a piece of equipment. Once the worst case has a plan, it stops being a monster. It becomes a to-do list.

**For entrepreneurs:** fear grows in the dark. Put numbers on the future, and half the fear disappears. The other half turns into a plan.

### At School: The Capstone and the Kids' Future

**If you're studying as an adult:** the week before his capstone presentation, George can't sleep. He pictures the panel failing him, two years of work down the drain, his family watching him fall short. He applies VIII.36: today there's no presentation. Today he has to review chapter three. So he reviews it. Tomorrow, chapter four. On presentation day, he'll have the same tools he used to prepare every chapter.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire worries about Sophie's future. Will her grades be good enough for middle school? Will she find a job when she grows up? What kind of world is she going to inherit? Claire realizes the worry is turning her into a control freak: she checks homework with a magnifying glass and compares her daughter to other girls. So she decides to do something more useful: give Sophie what she needs today, which is a calm mom, dinner together, and someone who listens. Sophie's future won't be built out of her mother's anxiety. It'll be built out of the days they actually live.

### In Your Family: Aging Parents

Lauren can't stop thinking about what'll happen when her parents can't take care of themselves anymore. Who'll look after them? Will they have enough money? Will she have to quit her job? Every visit is shadowed by that anxiety, and instead of enjoying her parents, she watches them for signs of decline.

She applies the difference between planning and worrying. **Planning:** a conversation with her siblings and her parents about what they want, checking whether they have health insurance and a will, finding out what care options exist nearby. They cover it in two meetings. **Worrying:** everything else.

With a plan in place, Lauren goes back to visiting her parents for what matters: having dinner with them, hearing her dad tell the same stories for the hundredth time, learning her mom's apple pie recipe. The future will come. Today, they're here.

### In Money and Health: Retirement

Andrew is forty-one and hasn't given retirement a thought. When he does, he panics. He hasn't saved enough, Social Security feels shaky, and he's heard stories about older people with nothing left. The anxiety is so big that he'd rather not think about it at all, and since he doesn't think about it, he doesn't do anything.

It's a textbook case of worry causing paralysis. Andrew decides to turn the fear into a plan. He checks his 401(k) balance, books an appointment with a financial adviser, and commits to putting in a small but fixed amount every month. He doesn't solve the future in one go. But for the first time, he's facing it with the tools he has today.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Don't think about the future."** No. Think about it so you can prepare. What doesn't help is living it before it gets here.

**"Everything will be fine."** No. Marcus never promises that. He promises you'll have what you need to face it, however it turns out.

**"Philosophy can cure anxiety."** Not always. If worry has been keeping you up for weeks, or it's affecting your work or your health, get professional help. Stoicism can go hand in hand with therapy. It can't replace it.

## This Week's Exercise: The 3 A.M. Page

Keep a notebook and a pen next to your bed. When worry wakes you up:

1. Write the fear down in one sentence: "I'm afraid that..."
2. Underneath, write: **What can I do about this tomorrow?** If there's something, write it down. Now it's a plan.
3. If there's nothing you can do, write: **"This isn't getting solved tonight."**
4. Ask yourself: **what is unbearable right now?** Take three breaths and go back to bed.

During the day, set aside fifteen fixed minutes for "planning the future": money, health, work. Outside those fifteen minutes, when a worry shows up, tell it: "I'll see you at planning hour."

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** keep a notebook by your bed and use the 3 a.m. page whenever you need it.
- **Intermediate:** for two weeks, write down your main worries with the date. At the end, check which ones happened and which didn't, like the participants in the LaFreniere and Newman study.
- **Advanced:** put Epictetus's question to your biggest anxiety: what do I want here that isn't within my power? Then write down what it would look like to go after the part that *is* within your power without letting your peace depend on the part that isn't.

## Key Ideas

- You'll face the future with the same tools you have today.
- Planning ends in action; worrying ends in more worry.
- Break up the load: what is unbearable right now?
- Most of what we dread never happens.
