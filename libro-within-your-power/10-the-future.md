# Chapter 10. The Future Is Not Yours

> *Don't let the future trouble you. You will meet it, if you have to, with the same reason you use today to face the present.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VII.8 (paraphrase)

> *Don't let the picture of your whole life crush you. Don't start imagining all the hardships that may be waiting. Ask yourself, as each one arrives: what is unbearable here? You'll be ashamed to answer. Remember that neither the future nor the past weighs on you, only the present, and the present shrinks if you mark its limits.*
> — *Meditations*, VIII.36 (paraphrase)

It's 3:14 a.m. You can't sleep. Your mind is doing math: what if I get laid off, what if we can't cover tuition, what if the business doesn't take off, what if that pain in my chest is something serious, what if my partner doesn't love me anymore, what if my kids go down the wrong path. Each thought leads to the next. By 4:00 you've lived through ten disasters that haven't happened.

In the morning, none of it happened. You're just tired.

Seneca wrote in one of his letters that we suffer more in imagination than in reality. Marcus Aurelius reached the same conclusion from a different sleepless night.

## The Context: Governing Without Knowing What Was Coming

Marcus ruled during one of Rome's most uncertain periods. The plague came and went. The frontiers shifted. He never knew whether the next campaign would be his last, whether his health would hold, whether his son Commodus, his heir, would be up to the task. History says he wasn't.

Marcus couldn't control that future. What he could do was remember that, when it came, he would face it with the same tools he had today: reason, character, experience. And that the weight he felt at night didn't come from the present, but from the imaginary sum of every possible problem.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Trust Your Weapons

VII.8 says something very simple and very comforting: your future self will have the same weapons as your present self. If you've been able to face what came your way today, you'll be able to tomorrow. Not because things will turn out well, but because you will be there, with your reason.

Think of it this way: you've survived 100 percent of your worst days. You didn't handle all of them well, but you made it here. That's not a motivational slogan; it's a fact.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Divide the Weight

VIII.36 offers a concrete technique: don't look at your whole life at once. When you see every difficulty together (the thirty-year mortgage, your kids' education, retirement, your parents' health), the weight is unbearable. But you'll never have to carry all of it at the same time. You only carry today's.

Marcus proposes a question: **what is unbearable in this moment?** Not this year, not this decade. This moment. Almost always the answer is: nothing. You're sitting down, you're breathing, you can do something.

It's the same logic used by addiction recovery programs ("just for today") and by marathon runners (don't think about mile 26; think about the next one).

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Shouldn't We Plan?

If the future shouldn't trouble us, does that mean we don't save, don't make a will, don't plan for retirement? That would be absurd. And Marcus doesn't say it.

The difference is between **planning** and **worrying**. Planning is thinking about the future in order to act today: opening a savings account, getting a checkup, updating your résumé. Worrying is thinking about the future without acting: turning the same thing over at 3 a.m.

The premeditation of adversity from Chapter 2 was useful because it ended in preparation. Worry is useless because it ends in more worry.

A practical test: if, while thinking about the future, you can write down a concrete action for today, you're planning. If all you can write is another "what if...?", you're worrying.

**In a nutshell:** planning is thinking about the future so you can act today; worrying is living in the future without ever acting.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Hope and Fear Are Chained Together

In one of his letters, Seneca quotes the Stoic Hecato: you will stop fearing if you stop hoping. And he explains the image: hope and fear march together, like a prisoner and a guard bound by the same chain. Both look toward the future. Whoever anxiously hopes for something also fears not getting it; whoever fears something also hopes it won't happen. The cause of both, Seneca says, is that we don't adapt ourselves to the present and we send our thoughts far ahead.

That doesn't mean living without goals. It means not hanging your peace on a future you don't control. You can work for something with all your strength without your calm today depending on getting it.

In another letter, Seneca writes the line we mentioned at the start of this chapter: more things frighten us than crush us, and we suffer more often in imagination than in reality. And he advises not to run out to meet our troubles: many of the things we fear may never come, and the ones that do needn't hurt ahead of time.

### Epictetus: The Musician Who Trembles

Epictetus observes that when he sees someone anxious, he asks himself what that person wants. If they didn't want something outside their power, they wouldn't be anxious. He gives the example of a musician: when he plays alone, he sings calmly; when he steps onto the stage, he trembles. Why? Because he doesn't just want to sing well, which is up to him, but also to be applauded, which isn't.

Anxiety, in this reading, is almost always the symptom of a desire placed in the wrong spot.

### What Science Says Today

A 2019 study by researchers Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman asked people with generalized anxiety disorder to write down their worries over several days and then check which ones came true. The vast majority, around 90 percent, did not. And in many of the cases where something did happen, the outcome was better than they'd feared. It's a small study, but it illustrates Seneca's line beautifully.

A technique used in some treatments for excessive worry, developed from the work of psychologist Thomas Borkovec, involves setting aside a fixed time each day for worrying and postponing any worries that come up outside that window. It's the "planning hour" idea proposed in this chapter's exercise.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Not worrying about the future is irresponsible. If I don't worry, I don't save.

**Author:** That's why the chapter separates worrying from planning. Saving is planning: a concrete action, today, with the future in mind. Worrying is spinning on the future without doing anything. In fact, worry often prevents saving, because the fear is so big we'd rather not open the bank statement.

**Skeptic:** But some futures are frightening for good reason: old age, illness, the economy.

**Author:** Of course. The Stoics didn't deny that dangers exist. Seneca recommended imagining them on purpose, in the premeditation of adversity. But Stoic imagination has an endpoint: it ends in preparation. The 3 a.m. anxiety doesn't end in anything; it circles back on itself. The question is whether your thinking about the future leaves you better prepared or just more tired.

**Skeptic:** "You'll meet it with the same weapons as today" isn't comforting if today I feel unarmed.

**Author:** That's an honest objection. But think of what you've already been through: losses, crises, changes you thought you couldn't survive. You survived them, not always well, but you made it here. Those are your weapons, even if you don't feel them today. And if you truly feel you have none, maybe you don't need philosophy but professional help. Asking for it is also a way to arm yourself.

**Skeptic:** What about hope? Seneca says to stop hoping. That sounds depressing.

**Author:** Seneca is talking about anxious hope, the kind that hangs your peace on an outcome you don't control. He isn't asking you to give up goals or dreams. He's asking that your calm today not depend on them coming true. You can work with everything you have toward something and, at the same time, be at peace if it doesn't arrive.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Fear That It Will End

After the crisis in Chapter 4, Lauren and Andrew are still together, and therapy has helped. But Lauren lives in fear: what if it happens again? What if one day he decides to leave? Every time Andrew comes home looking serious, she imagines the worst.

Her therapist tells her something very Stoic: "You can't love him well today if you're busy being afraid of losing him tomorrow." Lauren starts practicing the VIII.36 question: what is unbearable right now? Right now, Andrew is in the kitchen making coffee. Right now, the relationship is good.

There are no guarantees in love. But fear of the ending doesn't protect the relationship; it wears it down. The only thing that does protect it is how they treat each other today.

### At Work: Uncertainty

At Rick's company, there are rumors of layoffs. No one knows anything. Everyone speculates in the hallways. Productivity drops because everyone spends the day running numbers.

Rick makes two lists. **Planning:** update his résumé, figure out how long he could live on his savings, call two contacts. He does it in one afternoon. **Worrying:** everything else. He decides to stop taking part in the hallway rumors.

While his coworkers spend three weeks in anguish, he works. In the end, the layoffs don't touch him. But if they had, he was ready. Both outcomes were possible with the same strategy.

### In Business: Debt and Scenarios

Megan took out a loan to buy equipment. A slow month has her imagining repossession, bankruptcy, and shame in front of her family.

Her accountant teaches her a tool Marcus would have loved: three scenarios. She writes down what happens if the year is good, average, or bad. For the bad scenario, she defines what she'd do: cut expenses, renegotiate the loan, sell a piece of equipment. Once the worst case has a plan, it stops being a monster and becomes a task.

**For entrepreneurs:** fear grows in the dark. Put numbers on the future and half the fear disappears. The other half turns into a plan.

### At School: The Final Presentation and the Kids' Future

**If you're studying as an adult:** the week before his capstone presentation, George can't sleep. He imagines the panel failing him, losing two years of effort, his family watching him fail. He applies VIII.36: today there's no presentation; today he has to review chapter three. He reviews it. Tomorrow, chapter four. On presentation day he'll have the same weapons he used to prepare each chapter.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire worries about Sophie's future: will her grades be good enough for middle school? Will she find a job when she grows up? What kind of world will she inherit? She realizes this worry makes her controlling: she checks homework with a magnifying glass and compares her daughter with other girls. She decides to do something more useful: take care of what Sophie needs today, which is a calm mom, dinner together, and someone who listens. Sophie's future isn't built on her mother's anxiety; it's built on what they live every day.

### In Your Family: Aging Parents

Lauren can't stop thinking about what will happen when her parents can no longer take care of themselves. Who'll look after them? Will they have enough money? Will she have to stop working? Every visit with her parents is tinged with that anxiety, and instead of enjoying them, she watches them for signs of decline.

She applies the difference between planning and worrying. **Planning:** a conversation with her siblings and her parents about what they want, checking whether they have health insurance and a will, finding out what care options exist nearby. They do it in two meetings. **Worrying:** everything else.

With the plan in place, Lauren goes back to visiting her parents for what matters: having dinner with them, hearing her dad's stories for the hundredth time, learning her mom's apple pie recipe. The future will come. Today, they're here.

### In Money and Health: Retirement

Andrew is forty-one and hasn't thought about retirement. When he does, he panics: he hasn't saved enough, Social Security feels uncertain, he's heard stories of older people with no money. The anxiety is so big he'd rather not think about it. And since he doesn't think about it, he doesn't act.

It's a perfect example of how worry paralyzes. Andrew decides to turn fear into a plan: he checks his 401(k) balance, makes an appointment with a financial adviser, and decides to contribute a small but fixed amount every month. He doesn't solve the future in one go. But for the first time, he faces it with the weapons he has today.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Don't think about the future."** No. Think about it to prepare. What doesn't help is living it before it arrives.

**"Everything will be fine."** No. Marcus doesn't promise that. He promises you'll have what you need to face it, however it turns out.

**"Anxiety can be cured with philosophy."** Not always. If worry keeps you from sleeping for weeks, affects your work, or affects your health, seek professional help. Stoicism can go hand in hand with therapy; it can't replace it.

## This Week's Exercise: The 3 A.M. Page

Keep a notebook and a pen next to your bed. When worry wakes you up:

1. Write the fear in one sentence: "I'm afraid that..."
2. Below it, write: **What can I do about this tomorrow?** If there's an action, write it down. It's now a plan.
3. If there's no possible action, write: **"This won't be solved tonight."**
4. Ask yourself: **what is unbearable in this moment?** Take three breaths and go back to bed.

During the day, set aside a fixed fifteen minutes for "planning the future": finances, health, work. Outside those fifteen minutes, when worry shows up, tell it: "I'll see you during planning hour."

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** keep a notebook by your bed and use the 3 a.m. page whenever you need it.
- **Intermediate:** for two weeks, write down your main worries with the date. At the end, check which ones happened and which didn't, as in the LaFreniere and Newman study.
- **Advanced:** apply Epictetus's question to your biggest anxiety: what do I want here that isn't within my power? Write down what it would look like to pursue the part that is within your power without letting your peace depend on the part that isn't.

## Key Ideas

- You'll face the future with the same weapons you have today.
- Planning ends in an action; worrying ends in more worry.
- Divide the weight: what is unbearable in this moment?
- Most of what we fear never happens.
