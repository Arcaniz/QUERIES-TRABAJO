# Chapter 11. What Isn't Good for the Hive

> *What isn't good for the hive isn't good for the bee either.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.54 (paraphrase)

> *What doesn't harm the city doesn't harm the citizen.*
> — *Meditations*, V.22 (paraphrase)

This is the shortest line in the book, and perhaps the most demanding. A bee can't survive alone. Its life depends on the hive, and the hive depends on what each bee does. If a bee devotes itself only to itself, it dies, and the whole is weakened.

Now think about your life. The partner who "wins" the argument but loses the relationship. The employee who takes credit and destroys the team's trust. The business that squeezes its customers and loses its reputation. The student who cheats and doesn't learn. In every case, the bee thought it was winning. The hive lost. And in the end, so did the bee.

## The Context: Citizen of the World

The Stoics embraced a word attributed to the Cynic Diogenes, *cosmopolitan*: citizen of the world. They believed all human beings share reason, and that this makes us a single community, beyond borders, classes, and languages. Marcus, who ruled an empire of dozens of different peoples, wrote it many times: we are parts of a whole, like the limbs of a body.

A Stoic named Hierocles described the idea with an image: each person stands at the center of a series of circles. The first is oneself; then family; then friends and neighbors; then the city; then the country; then all of humanity. The moral task, he said, is to draw the outer circles in toward the center: to treat distant people a little more like those close to us.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Self-Interest Rightly Understood

For Marcus, there's no contradiction between one's own good and the common good. What harms the whole ends up harming you; what benefits it ends up benefiting you. Not by some magic law of karma, but because we're social beings: we live on trust, cooperation, and reputation.

From this angle, VI.54 is a decision rule: before you do something, ask whether it's good for the hive: your family, your team, your company, your community. If it isn't good for the hive, it probably isn't good for you either, even if it seems that way in the short run.

**In a nutshell:** whoever wins at the group's expense loses in the long run.

### Reading 2. The Practical: The Couple and the Team as "Us"

There's a very useful reading for relationships: stop thinking "me against you" and start thinking "us against the problem." In a couple, if one person wins the argument and the other loses, the relationship loses. On a team, if one department wins the budget and another is left without resources, the company loses.

The hive isn't the sum of the bees: it's something new that exists between them. A couple is more than two people: it's a relationship that has to be cared for like a third party. A team is more than its members: it's a culture.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What If the Hive Is Wrong?

There's a danger here. "What isn't good for the hive isn't good for the bee" can be used to crush the individual: "sacrifice yourself for the company," "put up with it for the family," "don't report it, so you don't hurt the institution's image." Many abuses have hidden behind "the good of the group."

But that use betrays the meditation. For Marcus, the true hive is the human community guided by reason and justice, not any particular group. A company that exploits its employees isn't a healthy hive; it's one that's getting sick. A family that covers up violence isn't protecting the hive; it's destroying it. Exposing injustice, in those cases, is what's good for the hive. And if violence is happening inside your own home, protecting yourself and seeking help (in the U.S., the National Domestic Violence Hotline: 1-800-799-7233, or 911 in an emergency) doesn't betray your family: it's the first step toward stopping the harm.

What's more, a hive that mistreats its bees doesn't survive. The good of the group includes the good of each member.

**In a nutshell:** serving the hive isn't obeying the group; it's caring for what makes it good.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: We Are Limbs of One Great Body

In one of his longest letters, Seneca sums up the Stoic ethics of living together in an image: we are limbs of one great body. Nature made us kin by forming us from the same material and for the same ends. And he quotes a line from the Roman playwright Terence that became famous: "I am human; nothing human is foreign to me."

Seneca compares society to a stone arch: it would collapse if the stones didn't hold each other up, and that's precisely why it stands.

### Musonius Rufus: Marriage as a Partnership in Life

Musonius Rufus, the teacher of Epictetus, was one of the few ancient philosophers who wrote in detail about marriage, and he did it in a way that was surprisingly modern for his time. For him, marriage wasn't just a way to have children, but a partnership in life in which the two care for each other, in sickness and in health, and in which each tries to outdo the other in attention and care. Musonius also argued, unusually for his era, that women should study philosophy just as men did.

If a couple is a hive of two, Musonius would be its best beekeeper: it isn't about who wins, but about who best cares for what they share.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologist John Gottman has spent decades studying couples in his lab. One of his best-known observations is that certain behaviors during conflict, such as criticizing the person rather than the behavior, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling, are warning signs for a relationship. What tends to distinguish stable couples isn't that they don't argue, but how they argue and how they repair afterward.

In the workplace, an internal research project at Google, known as Project Aristotle, looked for what set its most effective teams apart. One of its most publicized conclusions was the importance of *psychological safety*: team members feeling they can speak up, ask questions, or make mistakes without being humiliated, a concept researcher Amy Edmondson had studied earlier. In Marcus's terms, it's a healthy hive.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "What isn't good for the hive isn't good for the bee" is exactly what companies say when they ask you to sacrifice yourself.

**Author:** They say it, and they often use it to exploit people. But that betrays the line. A hive that mistreats its bees dies. For Marcus, the true hive is the community guided by justice, not any group that demands your obedience. If your company is squeezing you dry, the line doesn't oblige you to endure it; it gives you an argument for pointing out that the company is harming itself.

**Skeptic:** In a relationship, thinking about "us" can make one person disappear.

**Author:** That's a real risk, especially for someone who has learned always to give in. That's why Musonius talked about marriage as a partnership where both care for each other, not where one sacrifices for the other. A hive of two doesn't work if one bee does all the work. Thinking about "us" includes thinking about what you need, because you're part of the "us" too.

**Skeptic:** What about competition? In business, if you don't compete, you get eaten alive.

**Author:** Competing isn't wrong. What does damage is winning at the expense of trust: lying to customers, squeezing suppliers, stealing a colleague's idea. That may give you an edge today, but it destroys what holds your business up tomorrow. There's a way of competing that makes everyone better: doing your work better. That's the competition that's good for the hive.

**Skeptic:** Hierocles's circles sound nice, but I can't love all of humanity.

**Author:** Hierocles doesn't ask you to. He asks you to draw the circles in a little: treat a stranger a bit more like a neighbor, and a neighbor a bit more like family. It isn't an abstract universal love; it's greeting the security guard by name. It's small, and that's why it's possible.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Winning the Argument

Lauren and Andrew have a recurring fight about vacations: she wants to go to the beach with her family; he wants to go to the mountains, just the two of them. Every year someone "wins" and the other spends the vacation resentful. Neither enjoys it.

In therapy they learn to ask the hive question: what's good for *us*? They discover it isn't really about the beach or the mountains, but something deeper: Lauren needs to feel her family is welcome; Andrew needs time alone with her. They find a solution: a long weekend with her family and four days alone somewhere else.

**A tool:** in your next argument, switch seats and sit on the same side of the table, with the problem written on a sheet of paper in front of you both. It sounds like a game. It changes the conversation.

### At Work: Credit and Information

On Rick's team, one coworker regularly presents other people's ideas as his own. It works: he gets promoted. But within a year the team stops sharing ideas in meetings. Everyone holds back their best to present it alone. Innovation grinds to a halt.

When Rick becomes team lead (Chapter 8), he changes one rule: every presentation credits whoever came up with the idea. It seems minor. Within three months, the meetings are full of proposals again.

**For leaders:** the team is the hive. If you reward people who win at others' expense, you teach everyone to compete against their colleagues instead of against the problem.

### In Business: Clients, Suppliers, and Competitors

Megan is offered a big job on one condition: she has to cut her price and, to do so, pay less to the freelance illustrators she works with. She'd earn more; they'd lose.

Megan asks what's good for her hive: her network of collaborators, which took her years to build and has bailed her out more than once. If she pays them less, the best will leave. She turns the job down on those terms and proposes a different scope. The client accepts a smaller version.

A few months later, a competitor asks for her help with a project she can't finish. Megan could take advantage. Instead, she collaborates and they split the fee. The next year, that competitor refers two clients to her.

**For entrepreneurs:** your business lives in a hive of customers, suppliers, employees, competitors, and community. Squeezing any of them is squeezing your own future.

### At School: Cheating and Sharing

**If you're studying as an adult:** in George's MBA program, a classmate offers him the answers to an exam he got from a previous semester. George is tired and could use the help. But he asks: what's good for the hive? If everyone cheats, the degree is worth less for everyone. And he won't learn what he came to learn. He says no. Instead, he organizes a study group and shares his notes with anyone who needs them. Sharing isn't the same as cheating: the first makes the hive stronger; the second weakens it.

**If you're supporting your kids:** there's a new girl in Sophie's class whom no one invites to play. Sophie doesn't either, because she's afraid her friends will leave her out. Claire tells her about Hierocles's circles: "Right now that girl is in your outer circle. What would happen if you brought her in a little?" The next day, Sophie invites her to eat lunch with them. Nothing bad happens. The hive gets a little bigger.

### In Your Family: The Inheritance

When Andrew's grandmother dies, his aunts and uncles start fighting over the house. Each has an argument: who took care of her most, who put money into the remodel, who lives closest. Within two months, siblings who loved each other stop speaking.

Andrew, the oldest grandchild, suggests they ask the hive question: what's good for the family? He reminds them that his grandmother spent her life gathering them in that house. Selling it and dividing the money may be fair, but losing the family over it would betray what the house meant. They suggest a mediator. It takes almost a year, but they reach an agreement. Not everyone is happy. But they sit down together again at Christmas.

### In Money and Health: Money as a Couple

Lauren and Andrew always handled their money separately: each paid their own expenses and they split household costs down the middle. It worked, until Andrew went through a period of lower income after a job change, and half the household costs became a burden for him.

They sit down to think like a hive. They decide to contribute to a joint account in proportion to what each earns, keep a separate amount for each person's personal spending, and make big decisions together. It isn't the only possible model, and every couple has to find its own. But the change of question, from "what's fair for me?" to "what's good for both of us?", took away a source of resentment they'd been carrying for years.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Always sacrifice yourself for the group."** No. A healthy group needs healthy members. Taking care of yourself is also taking care of the hive.

**"Whatever the majority says is right."** No. The Stoic hive is guided by reason and justice, not by majority opinion.

**"Never compete."** No. Honest competition can make everyone better. What does damage is winning at the expense of trust, justice, or other people's well-being.

## This Week's Exercise: The Circles

1. On a sheet of paper, draw five concentric circles. Write your name in the center. Then, moving outward: close family, friends and colleagues, community (neighbors, clients, school), strangers.
2. Write one specific person in each circle.
3. During the week, do one small thing to bring someone from an outer circle a little closer: greet the security guard by name, help a coworker who isn't your friend, pay your smallest supplier on time, write to a relative you rarely talk to.
4. Before every important decision this week, ask yourself: **is this good for the hive?**

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** before each important decision this week, ask yourself out loud: "Is this good for the hive?"
- **Intermediate:** in your next argument with your partner or a colleague, sit on the same side of the table and write the problem on a sheet of paper in front of you.
- **Advanced:** identify a situation where you're "winning" at the expense of someone in your hive: a colleague, a supplier, a relative. Look for a way for both of you to win, even if you win a little less.

## Key Ideas

- What harms the "us" ends up harming you.
- Care for the relationship as if it were a third person at the table.
- Serving the hive isn't obeying the group: it's caring for what makes it just.
- Draw the outer circles a little closer to the center.

## Journaling Question

> **In what situation am I winning while the "us" is losing?**

---

The bee doesn't ask whether its work is worth it. It goes out, searches, comes back, and leaves what it brought. It doesn't do it out of sacrifice, but because it knows, in its own way, that its life and the hive's are one. You can know that more clearly than the bee does. And you have something the bee doesn't: the freedom to choose it.
