# Chapter 12. What I Owe to Others

> *From my grandfather Verus: good character and an even temper. From my mother: devotion, generosity, keeping clear not only of doing wrong but even of thinking it, and living simply, far from the habits of the rich. From Rusticus: being introduced to the teachings of Epictetus. From Alexander the grammarian: not correcting people with reproach, but offering the right phrase tactfully. From Fronto: seeing up close what a tyrant's envy, cunning, and hypocrisy look like.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book I (selections, paraphrased)

Who taught you to drive? Who gave you your first job? Who explained something nobody else had the patience to explain? Who told you something twenty years ago that you still remember when you're about to give up?

Chances are you never thanked them the way they deserved. Hardly anyone does. Marcus Aurelius, the most powerful man in the world, devoted the entire first book of his *Meditations* to doing exactly that.

## The Context: A Book of Thank-Yous

Book I of the *Meditations* is unlike the rest. No reflections on death, anger, or the universe. It's a list. Seventeen short sections, nearly all starting the same way: "From my grandfather...," "From my mother...," "From my teacher..." Marcus goes through each person who shaped him and writes down what he learned from them.

What's remarkable is the detail. He doesn't write "my mother was a good woman." He writes that she taught him to live simply. He doesn't write "my teacher was wise." He writes that Alexander taught him how to correct people without humiliating them. And the portrait he paints of his adoptive father, Antoninus, is so detailed it reads like a manual for being a good person with power.

He even learns from the negative. From Fronto, his rhetoric teacher, he says he learned what envy and hypocrisy look like in tyrants, and that the people we call aristocrats often lack real warmth. Not everything we learn comes from good examples.

The book ends with thanks to the gods for everything he had, and even for the things that never happened to him.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Nobody Is Self-Made

To the Stoics, human beings are social by nature. Much of who we are, we owe to others: the people who raised us, taught us, corrected us, gave us a shot. For a Stoic, the "self-made man" is a myth.

Book I is an antidote to arrogance. An emperor who reminds himself every day "I got this from my grandfather, this from my mother, this from my teacher" is going to have a hard time believing he's better than anyone else. It's the vaccine against the "turning into Caesar" problem from Chapter 8.

### Reading 2. The Psychological: Gratitude as a Practice

Modern psychology has studied gratitude extensively. A number of studies suggest that regularly writing down what you're grateful for goes along with greater well-being, better sleep, and stronger relationships. But according to that research, the key is the same one Marcus models: be specific. "I'm grateful for my family" does less than "I'm grateful my sister called when I was sick."

Marcus doesn't write generic thank-you notes. He writes down specific behaviors he picked up. That turns gratitude into more than a feeling. It becomes a way of remembering who you want to be.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What If My Family Hurt Me?

Not everyone had an even-tempered grandfather and a generous mother. Some people grew up with violence, with neglect, or with people who taught them the worst of everything. Are they supposed to be grateful?

Marcus gives us a clue with Fronto: we also learn from what we don't want to repeat. Gratitude doesn't mean excusing harm. It means recognizing what you took away, even from a bad experience: "From my father, I learned how I *don't* want to treat my kids." That isn't gratitude toward the father. It's gratitude for your own ability to learn.

Besides, Book I isn't only about family. It includes teachers, friends, a brother, colleagues. If your family let you down, maybe there were others: a teacher, a neighbor, a coach, a boss, a friend. Marcus's list is an invitation to go looking for those people.

**In a nutshell:** you also learn from the people who showed you what you don't want to become.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Art of Receiving

Seneca wrote seven books *On Benefits*, a whole treatise on how to give, receive, and be grateful. One of his finest insights is that gratitude is also a skill, and many people never learn it: they accept help with indifference, forget it quickly, or feel humiliated about owing anybody anything. For Seneca, receiving a kindness with real gratitude is already the first payment on the debt.

He also warns that the most common kind of ingratitude is simply forgetting. That's why, for the Stoics, gratitude isn't only a feeling. It's an exercise in memory.

### Epictetus: Thankful for What You Bring to the Fight

Epictetus devotes one of his lessons to being grateful for something we almost never think about: our own abilities. If we had any sense, he says, we'd do nothing but praise and give thanks, because we've been given the faculties we need to bear whatever happens to us: patience, courage, reason. Instead, he complains, most people skip the gratitude and just lament what happens to them.

It's a different kind of gratitude from Marcus's: not toward other people, but toward what you carry inside you to face life. The two go well together.

### What Science Says Today

In a widely cited 2003 study, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked one group of participants to write down things they were grateful for each week, a second group to write down hassles, and a third simply to list events. On average, the gratitude group reported feeling better about their lives and more optimistic. Later research has generally pointed the same way, though the effects are modest, and it's no miracle cure.

What's interesting is that several studies line up with Marcus's method: gratitude seems to work best when it's specific, and when it's expressed to the person rather than just felt privately.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Gratitude is nice and all, but sometimes it's just a way of settling. "Be grateful for what you have and don't ask for more."

**Author:** That use of it exists, and it's a con. But Marcus was grateful and, at the same time, worked tirelessly to improve both his empire and himself. Being grateful for what you've received doesn't stop you from fighting for what you're missing or calling out what's wrong. If anything, people who recognize what others have done for them tend to want to do more for others.

**Skeptic:** And if my parents hurt me? Do I have to thank them, too?

**Author:** No. You can acknowledge what you learned from a painful experience without thanking the person who caused it. Marcus learned from Fronto what a tyrant's hypocrisy looks like. He wasn't thanking him for being a hypocrite; he was acknowledging a lesson. Your Book I can include what you don't want to repeat. That shaped you, too.

**Skeptic:** Gratitude lists feel corny.

**Author:** They can be, if you just write "thanks for everything" every night. What Marcus does is different. He writes down concrete behaviors he learned from concrete people. It's almost an inventory of his character. It isn't an exercise in warm feelings; it's an exercise in memory. Knowing where your strengths came from helps you take care of them, and keeps you from believing you invented them all on your own.

**Skeptic:** Why tell the other person? It's enough that I know.

**Author:** Maybe it's enough for you. But think about them: a teacher, a former boss, a friend who has no idea they changed your life. Telling them costs you almost nothing and could mean a great deal to them. And there's one more reason, a very Stoic one: you won't always have time to say it.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: What I Learned from You

Lauren and Andrew have been together for ten years. Lately they've learned to fight better (Chapter 6) and to think in terms of "us" (Chapter 11). But it's been a long time since they told each other what they've gotten from one another.

For their anniversary, their therapist suggests an exercise: each of them writes a "Book I" about the other. Andrew writes: "From Lauren I learned to say what I feel instead of shutting down. To get organized. To apologize without making excuses." Lauren writes: "From Andrew I learned to laugh at myself. Not to take everything so seriously. To rest without feeling guilty."

They read them aloud. Both of them cry. After ten years, they realize, they've been shaping each other all along, and neither of them had noticed.

### At Work: Mentors

Rick, now a team lead, starts thinking about what kind of boss he wants to be. He writes his own professional Book I. From his first boss, he learned to walk into meetings prepared. From a coworker, he learned to negotiate without fear. From the director who used to humiliate him, he learned how not to treat his team. From last year's intern, he learned to try new tools without being embarrassed to ask questions.

Then he does something harder. He sends a message to his first boss, whom he hasn't seen in fifteen years: "I lead a team now, and every week I use something I learned from you. Thank you." The boss, long since retired, replies with a photo of the two of them at the office back in the day.

**For leaders:** gratitude flows downhill too. Telling your team what you've learned from them is worth more than any bonus.

### In Business: The People Who Hold You Up

Megan spends an evening looking back over the history of her studio. She realizes it wouldn't exist without a handful of people: the first client who trusted her before she had a portfolio; the accountant who taught her to keep her personal and business finances separate; the illustrator who gave up a whole weekend to save a deadline; her sister, who lent her the money for her first computer.

She decides to do something with that list. In December, instead of sending generic corporate gifts, she writes each of those people a handwritten letter telling them what they did for her business. It takes her an afternoon. Several of them write back that it's the best thing they've ever received from a vendor.

**For entrepreneurs:** your business has a Book I. Writing it reminds you that you didn't do it alone, and it reminds them why they're glad they stuck with you.

### At School: Teachers

**If you're studying as an adult:** as he wraps up his MBA, George remembers the elementary school teacher who told him he was a good writer, and the tutor who patiently walked him through statistics. In his capstone report, he writes an acknowledgments page that's more than a formality: he names each person and what he learned from them. A classmate tells him he's never read acknowledgments like that.

**If you're supporting your kids:** at the end of the school year, Claire suggests Sophie write a letter to her teacher. Not "thanks for everything," but three specific things she learned that year. Sophie writes: "You taught me not to be scared of getting things wrong when I go up to the board." The teacher keeps the letter. Without ever saying so, Claire has taught her daughter the exercise that opens the *Meditations*.

### In Your Family: Grandparents

Claire never had a good relationship with her father. He was distant, sometimes harsh. When he gets sick, she visits out of obligation. One day her therapist suggests she write a Book I about him, including both the good and what she doesn't want to repeat.

Claire writes: "From my father I learned to work hard and keep my word. I also learned how I don't want to talk to my daughter." Reading it over, she realizes both things are true, and that she can be grateful for the first without excusing the second. On her next visit, she tells her father only the first part. He doesn't say much. But that afternoon, he asks her to bring his granddaughter next time.

### In Money and Health: The Family Doctor

George has had the same family doctor since he was a kid. When he was diagnosed with a thyroid problem years ago, the doctor didn't just write a prescription. He patiently explained what was going on and talked straight with him about his habits. George never thanked him beyond the "thanks, Doc" at the end of every appointment.

In his Book I, George writes: "From Dr. Miller I learned that taking care of yourself is a responsibility to your family, not vanity." He brings the page to his next appointment. The doctor, who's about to retire, tucks it into his desk drawer. George realizes some people hold up our lives for decades without our ever telling them.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"You have to thank whoever hurt you."** No. You can acknowledge what you learned from a bad experience without thanking whoever caused it.

**"Gratitude means settling."** No. Being grateful for what you have doesn't stop you from wanting more or fighting to fix what's wrong. Marcus was grateful and worked relentlessly.

**"Feeling it is enough."** No. Marcus's gratitude is written down and specific. Gratitude that never gets expressed or remembered just fades.

## This Week's Exercise: Your Book I

1. List ten people who shaped you: family, teachers, bosses, friends, past partners, even difficult people.
2. Next to each name, write a sentence in Marcus's style: **"From ___, I learned ___."** Be specific: behaviors, not adjectives.
3. Include at least one person who taught you what you *don't* want to be.
4. Pick one living person on the list and let them know, by text, letter, or in person, what you wrote about them.
5. Keep the list. Reread it when you feel alone, or when you start thinking you did it all yourself.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** every night, write down one specific thing you're grateful for. Not "my family," but "that my brother called to check on me."
- **Intermediate:** write your full Book I, with at least ten people, including one who taught you what you don't want to be.
- **Advanced:** try what's known as a *gratitude visit*: write a one-page letter to someone who changed your life, and read it to them in person or over video.

## Key Ideas

- Nobody is self-made: every strength you have bears someone's fingerprints.
- Gratitude works when it's specific and spoken out loud.
- You also learn from the people who showed you what you don't want to become.
- Being grateful isn't settling.
