# Chapter 12. What I Owe to Others

> *From my grandfather Verus: good character and an even temper. From my mother: piety, generosity, refraining not only from doing wrong but even from thinking it, and simplicity in the way I live, far from the habits of the rich. From Rusticus: being introduced to the teachings of Epictetus. From Alexander the grammarian: not correcting people with reproach, but offering the right expression tactfully. From Fronto: having seen what the envy, cunning, and hypocrisy of a tyrant look like.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book I (selection and paraphrase)

Who taught you to drive? Who gave you your first job? Who explained what no one else had the patience to explain? Who said something to you, twenty years ago, that you still remember when you're about to give up?

You probably never thanked them the way they deserved. Hardly anyone does. Marcus Aurelius, the most powerful man in the world, devoted the entire first book of his *Meditations* to doing exactly that.

## The Context: A Book of Thanks

Book I of the *Meditations* is different from all the others. It has no reflections on death, anger, or the universe. It's a list. Seventeen paragraphs that almost all begin the same way: "From my grandfather...," "From my mother...," "From my teacher..." Marcus goes through every person who shaped him and notes what he learned from them.

What's extraordinary isn't the list but the detail. He doesn't write "my mother was good." He writes that she taught him to live simply. He doesn't write "my teacher was wise." He writes that from Alexander he learned to correct others without humiliating them. And of his adoptive father, Antoninus, he writes a portrait so detailed it reads like a guide to being a good person with power.

He even learns from the negative: from Fronto, his rhetoric teacher, he says he learned what the envy and hypocrisy of tyrants look like, and that those we call aristocrats often lack real affection. Not everything we learn comes from good examples.

The book ends with thanks to the gods for everything he had, and even for what didn't happen to him.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: No One Is Self-Made

For the Stoics, human beings are social by nature. Much of who we are we owe to others: to those who raised us, taught us, corrected us, and gave us opportunities. The myth of the "self-made man" is false, to a Stoic.

Book I is an antidote to arrogance. An emperor who reminds himself every day "I learned this from my grandfather, this from my mother, this from my teacher" will have a hard time believing he's better than others. It's the vaccine against "turning into Caesar" from Chapter 8.

### Reading 2. The Psychological: Gratitude as a Practice

Contemporary psychology has studied gratitude extensively. Several studies suggest that regularly writing down what we're grateful for is associated with greater well-being, better sleep, and stronger relationships. But the key, according to that research, is the same one Marcus shows: be specific. "I'm grateful for my family" works less well than "I'm grateful my sister called when I was sick."

Marcus doesn't write a generic thank-you. He writes down specific behaviors he learned. That turns gratitude into more than a feeling: into a way of remembering who you want to be.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What If My Family Hurt Me?

Not everyone had an even-tempered grandfather and a generous mother. Some people grew up with violence, with neglect, or with people who taught them the worst. Should that person be grateful?

Marcus gives us a clue with Fronto: we also learn from what we don't want to repeat. Gratitude doesn't mean justifying harm. It means acknowledging what you took away, even from a bad experience: "from my father I learned how I don't want to treat my children." That isn't gratitude toward the father; it's gratitude for your own capacity to learn.

Besides, Book I isn't addressed only to family. There are teachers, friends, a brother, colleagues. If your family failed you, perhaps there were others: a teacher, a neighbor, a coach, a boss, a friend. Marcus's list invites you to look for those people.

**In a nutshell:** you also learn from the people who taught you what you don't want to be.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Art of Receiving

Seneca wrote seven books *On Benefits*, an entire treatise on how to give, receive, and be grateful. One of his finest ideas is that gratitude is also an art, and many people don't know how to do it: they receive with indifference, forget quickly, or feel humiliated at owing something. For Seneca, whoever receives a favor with genuine gratitude has already paid the first installment of the debt.

Seneca also warns against the most common kind of ingratitude: not that of someone who denies having received something, but that of someone who simply forgets. That's why, for the Stoics, gratitude isn't only a feeling but an exercise of memory.

### Epictetus: Gratitude for What We Have to Face Life

Epictetus devotes one of his lessons to giving thanks for something we almost never think to be grateful for: our own abilities. He says that if we had any sense, we would do nothing but praise and give thanks, because we've received the faculties we need to bear everything that happens to us: patience, courage, reason. He complains that most people, instead of being grateful for those abilities, lament what happens to them.

It's a different gratitude from Marcus's: not toward other people, but toward what you carry inside to face life. The two complement each other.

### What Science Says Today

In an influential 2003 study, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked one group of participants to write down each week things they were grateful for, another to write down hassles, and a third simply to list events. The gratitude group reported, on average, feeling better about their lives and more optimistic. Later research has found results in the same direction, though with moderate effects, and not as a miracle cure.

What's interesting is that several studies agree with Marcus's method: gratitude seems to work best when it's specific and when it's expressed to the person, not just felt in private.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Gratitude is all well and good, but sometimes it's a way of settling. "Be grateful for what you have and don't ask for more."

**Author:** That use exists, and it's a trick. But Marcus was grateful and, at the same time, worked tirelessly to improve his empire and himself. Being grateful for what you received doesn't stop you from fighting for what you lack or calling out what's wrong. In fact, people who recognize what others did for them tend to want to do more for others.

**Skeptic:** And if my parents hurt me? Do I have to thank them too?

**Author:** No. You can acknowledge what you learned from a painful experience without thanking the person who caused it. Marcus learned from Fronto what a tyrant's hypocrisy looks like. He wasn't thanking him for being a hypocrite; he was acknowledging a lesson. Your Book I can include what you don't want to repeat. That shaped you, too.

**Skeptic:** Writing gratitude lists seems corny.

**Author:** It can be, if you write "thanks for everything" every night. What Marcus does is something else: he writes down concrete behaviors he learned from concrete people. It's almost an inventory of his character. It isn't an exercise in pretty feelings, but in memory. Knowing where your virtues came from helps you take care of them, and keeps you from believing you invented them all by yourself.

**Skeptic:** Why tell the other person? It's enough that I know.

**Author:** Maybe for you. But think of the other person: a teacher, a former boss, a friend who never knew they changed your life. Telling them costs very little and can mean a great deal to them. And there's one more reason, a very Stoic one: the time to say it isn't infinite.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: What I Learned from You

Lauren and Andrew have been together ten years. Lately they've learned to argue better (Chapter 6) and to think in terms of "us" (Chapter 11). But it's been a long time since they told each other what the other has given them.

For their anniversary, their therapist suggests an exercise: each of them writes their own "Book I" about the other. Andrew writes: "From Lauren I learned to say what I feel instead of shutting down. To get organized. To apologize without making excuses." Lauren writes: "From Andrew I learned to laugh at myself. Not to take everything so seriously. To rest without guilt."

They read them out loud. Both cry. They discover that, after ten years, they've been shaping each other, and they had never noticed.

### At Work: Mentors

Rick, now a team lead, wonders what kind of boss he wants to be. He writes his own professional Book I: from his first boss he learned to come to meetings prepared; from a coworker, to negotiate without fear; from the director who used to humiliate him, how not to treat his team; from the intern who joined last year, to use new tools without being embarrassed to ask.

Then he does something harder: he sends a message to his first boss, whom he hasn't seen in fifteen years: "Today I lead a team, and every week I use something I learned from you. Thank you." The boss, now retired, replies with a photo of the two of them in the office back then.

**For leaders:** gratitude also flows downward. Telling your team what you've learned from them is worth more than any bonus.

### In Business: The Network That Holds You Up

Megan spends an evening looking back at the history of her business. She realizes it wouldn't exist without a group of people: the first client who trusted her without a portfolio; the accountant who taught her to separate her personal and business accounts; the illustrator who worked an entire weekend to save a deadline; her sister, who lent her money for her first computer.

She decides to do something with that list: in December, instead of sending generic corporate gifts, she writes a handwritten letter to each of those people telling them what they did for her business. It takes her an afternoon. Several of them reply that it's the best gift they've ever received from a vendor.

**For entrepreneurs:** your business has a Book I. Writing it reminds you that you didn't do it alone, and it reminds them why it's worth staying with you.

### At School: Teachers

**If you're studying as an adult:** as he finishes his MBA, George remembers the elementary school teacher who told him he was good at writing, and the tutor who patiently explained statistics to him. For his capstone report he writes an acknowledgments page that isn't a formality: he names each person and what he learned from them. A classmate tells him he's never read acknowledgments like that.

**If you're supporting your kids:** at the end of the school year, Claire suggests Sophie write a letter to her teacher. Not "thanks for everything," but three specific things she learned with her. Sophie writes: "You taught me not to be afraid of making mistakes when I go up to the board." The teacher keeps the letter. Without saying so, Claire has taught her daughter the exercise that opens the *Meditations*.

### In Your Family: Grandparents

Claire never had a good relationship with her father. He was distant, sometimes harsh. When he falls ill, Claire visits him out of obligation. One day her therapist suggests she write her Book I about him, including both the good and what she doesn't want to repeat.

Claire writes: "From my father I learned to work hard and keep my word. I also learned how I don't want to speak to my daughter." Reading it, she realizes both things are true and that she can be grateful for the first without justifying the second. On her next visit she tells her father only the first part. He doesn't say much, but that afternoon he asks her to bring his granddaughter.

### In Money and Health: The Family Doctor

George has a family doctor who has treated him since he was a child. When he was diagnosed with a thyroid problem years ago, the doctor didn't just hand him a prescription: he patiently explained what was going on and talked straight with him about his habits. George never thanked him beyond the "thanks, Doc" at the end of each visit.

In his Book I, George writes: "From Dr. Miller I learned that taking care of yourself is a responsibility to your family, not vanity." He brings him the page at his next appointment. The doctor, who is about to retire, keeps it in his desk drawer. George realizes there are people who hold our lives up for decades without our ever telling them.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"You must be grateful to whoever hurt you."** No. You can acknowledge what you learned from a bad experience without thanking whoever caused it.

**"Gratitude is settling."** No. Being grateful for what you have doesn't stop you from wanting more or fighting to change what's wrong. Marcus was grateful and worked relentlessly.

**"Feeling it is enough."** No. Marcus's gratitude is written and specific. Gratitude that's never expressed or remembered gets lost.

## This Week's Exercise: Your Book I

1. Make a list of ten people who shaped you: family, teachers, bosses, friends, past partners, even difficult people.
2. Next to each name, write a sentence in Marcus's style: **"From ___ I learned ___."** Be specific: behaviors, not adjectives.
3. Include at least one person from whom you learned what you **don't** want to be.
4. Choose one living person from the list and let them know, by message, letter, or in person, what you wrote about them.
5. Keep the list. Reread it when you feel alone, or when you feel you did it all yourself.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** every night, write one specific thing you're grateful for: not "my family," but "that my brother called to ask how I was doing."
- **Intermediate:** write your complete Book I, with at least ten people, including one from whom you learned what you don't want to be.
- **Advanced:** do what's known as a *gratitude visit*: write a one-page letter to someone who influenced your life and read it to them in person or by video call.

## Key Ideas

- No one is self-made: every virtue bears someone's fingerprint.
- Gratitude works when it's specific and expressed.
- You also learn from the people who taught you what you don't want to be.
- Being grateful isn't settling.
