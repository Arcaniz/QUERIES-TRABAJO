# Chapter 13. Remember That You Will Die

> *You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do, say, and think.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, II.11 (paraphrase)

> *Don't act as if you had ten thousand years to live. Death hangs over you. While you live, while you can, be good.*
> — *Meditations*, IV.17 (paraphrase)

There's a story told about Rome's triumphal parades: as the victorious general rode through the streets amid the cheers, a slave behind him whispered in his ear to remember that he was mortal. *Memento mori*: remember that you will die.

We don't know for certain whether the scene happened exactly that way. But the idea has survived two thousand years because it says something we all know and almost never think about: time runs out. And what we do with it depends, to a large extent, on remembering that.

## The Context: A Man Surrounded by Death

Marcus Aurelius didn't need to imagine death. It was all around him. The plague killed millions in his empire. He watched soldiers die at the front. Several of his children died young. His wife, Faustina, died. He himself was in fragile health and died at fifty-eight, on campaign, far from Rome.

The *Meditations* are full of reminders of death. Marcus writes that Alexander the Great and his mule driver ended up the same in death. That of the powerful emperors before him, almost no one remembers anything. That life is an instant between two eternities. He doesn't write this to depress himself, but to get clear: if time is short, don't spend it on what doesn't matter.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Death as the Measure of What Matters

For the Stoics, death isn't an evil. It's a natural process, like being born or growing up. What is an evil is living badly: with fear, with hatred, with vanity, without doing what you should.

Death works as a measuring stick. If tomorrow were your last day, would you still be angry with your brother over the inheritance? Would you spend the night scrolling? Would you put off the call to your father? The question in II.11 isn't "What would you do if you had one day left?", because that invites you to blow everything on pleasure. It's "What would you do, say, and think if you knew this could end at any moment?" The answer is almost always: the same as usual, but with more care, more honesty, and more love.

**In a nutshell:** death doesn't take meaning away from life; it gives it a deadline.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Urgent Versus Important

There's a productivity tool attributed to President Dwight Eisenhower: dividing tasks into urgent and important. The urgent shouts: the email, the call, the notification. The important whispers: your health, your relationship with your kids, the project that gives you meaning.

*Memento mori* works as an amplifier for the whisper. When you remember that your time is limited, what's important gets louder. Many people who go through a serious illness or a close loss say the same thing: suddenly they knew what mattered to them. Stoicism suggests not waiting for the illness to find out.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Isn't This Morbid?

For many people, thinking about death is depressing or distressing. Is it healthy to do it every day?

The Stoic answer is that what's morbid isn't thinking about death, but avoiding it. Our culture hides it: in hospitals, in quick funerals, in phrases like "we lost him." And by hiding it, we live as if we had ten thousand years, just as Marcus warns: postponing, fighting over trivia, holding back what we should say.

That said, care is needed. If you're going through a recent loss, depression, or thoughts of harming yourself, this exercise isn't for you right now. Please seek professional help. *Memento mori* is a practice for valuing life, not for turning away from it. In the United States, you can call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) at any time, free and confidential. Outside the U.S., contact your local crisis line or emergency number.

**In a nutshell:** remembering death isn't loving death, but learning to love life before it ends.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: "I Have Lived"

Seneca wrote a great deal about death, and almost always in order to talk about life. In one letter he proposes an exercise for every night: go to sleep cheerfully and say, "I have lived; I have run the course fortune gave me." If tomorrow is given to us, he adds, let us receive it as a gain. Whoever can say "I have lived" every night wakes up every morning to a bonus day.

In another letter, he says we die every day: every day we lose a part of our life, and death isn't a distant event but something already under way. He doesn't say this to depress himself, but to value the hours we do have.

Seneca himself had to face death brutally: Nero ordered him to take his own life. According to the historian Tacitus, he faced it calmly, consoling his friends and reminding them of what he had tried to teach them.

### Epictetus: Don't Say "I Lost It"

Epictetus gives advice that may seem harsh but that many people in grief have found comforting over time. Never say of anything, "I lost it," but "I gave it back." Did your child die? They were given back. Did your wife die? She was given back. As long as it's entrusted to you, he says, take care of it as something that isn't yours, the way travelers take care of an inn they're staying in.

This needs to be read with tenderness. Epictetus isn't telling us not to cry. He's asking us to remember that the people we love were never ours: they were a gift. And the pain of losing them is, in a way, the measure of what they gave us.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec studied in the 1990s what people regret. They found that in the short term we tend to regret what we did wrong more, but in the long term, looking back over life, we regret more what we didn't do: the opportunities not taken, the words not spoken, the paths not explored.

It's a modern confirmation of this chapter's question: what am I putting off as if I had ten thousand years? The things we don't do because we think there will be time are the ones that weigh most when time runs out.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Thinking about death every day seems morbid to me.

**Author:** Every day, for hours, it would be. But *memento mori* is a brief reminder, not an obsession. It works like the "low battery" warning on your phone: it doesn't say the phone will explode; it says use well what's left. What's actually morbid is living as if death didn't exist and discovering it all at once in a hospital room.

**Skeptic:** If I remember I'm going to die, why not spend everything and do whatever I want?

**Author:** Because Marcus doesn't say "live as if you'll die tomorrow"; he says "while you live, while you can, be good." The conclusion isn't unbridled pleasure but seriousness. If time is short, don't waste it on grudges, on pointless busywork, or on what doesn't matter. People who've been close to death rarely say "I should have spent more." They tend to say "I should have said more, hugged more, forgiven more."

**Skeptic:** That line from Epictetus, "don't say I lost it, say I gave it back," seems cold in the face of a loved one's death.

**Author:** I understand why it seems that way, especially early in grief. It isn't something to say to someone at a funeral. It's an idea that, over time, has helped many people transform the anger of loss into gratitude for what they had. The person we love was never ours; they were a gift. The pain is enormous precisely because the gift was.

**Skeptic:** And if I don't believe in anything after death?

**Author:** Marcus wasn't sure either. In several passages he considers both possibilities: that there's something afterward, or that we simply dissolve back into nature. And he reaches the same conclusion in both cases: there's nothing to fear, and the only thing that matters is how we live while we're here. It's one of the most consoling ideas in the book, and it works equally well for believers and nonbelievers.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: What Goes Unsaid

One ordinary Tuesday, Andrew's father has a heart attack. He survives, but spends a week in intensive care. At the hospital, Andrew realizes it's been years since he told his father he loves him. He tells him. His father, who never talks about these things, squeezes his hand.

That night, Andrew and Lauren talk until late. They ask each other what they'd say if they knew one of them wouldn't come back tomorrow. They discover things they'd gone years without saying: thanks, overdue apologies, dreams they'd tucked away. They decide not to wait for the next hospital.

**A small habit:** they never say goodbye angry. They can pick the argument up later, but when one of them leaves the house, the other says something kind. Just in case.

### At Work: Career and Life

Rick goes to the funeral of a former coworker who died suddenly at fifty-two. At the service, no one talks about his professional achievements. They talk about what a good friend he was, how he always had a joke, how he took his kids to soccer every Saturday. One of his sons says: "What I miss most is that he waited up for me."

Rick goes home and looks at his week. He works late almost every day. He answers emails on Sundays. He missed his son's last game for a meeting.

He doesn't quit his job. But he changes a few things: he leaves at six two days a week, keeps his phone out of the bedroom at night, and goes back to the games. The company doesn't fall apart. No one at his funeral will talk about the quarterly report.

### In Business: Legacy

Megan wonders what she wants to remain of her business when she's gone, or when she stops working. A company that carries on? Clients who remember she was honest? Designers who learned from her?

The question changes some of her decisions. She starts documenting her processes so the business doesn't depend on her alone. She writes a will and a plan in case she falls ill. And she spends more time training the young designers, because she realizes that's what she'd most like to leave behind.

**For entrepreneurs:** thinking about death is also thinking about continuity. A business that dies with you may not have been a business but self-employment. And that's fine, if you chose it. But it's worth deciding, not discovering too late.

### At School: Learning for Its Own Sake

**If you're studying as an adult:** as he finishes his MBA, George wonders whether he'll keep studying. He's thirty-nine. Before, he would have thought it was too late. Now he thinks the opposite: precisely because time isn't infinite, he doesn't want to stop learning what interests him. He signs up for guitar lessons, something he's wanted since he was fifteen.

A late Roman historian, Ammianus Marcellinus, tells a story about Socrates: in his last days, while awaiting execution, he heard someone singing a poem and asked to be taught it. They asked him why, if he was going to die. He answered, more or less: "So I'll know one more thing before I go." It may not have happened that way, but the idea is worth keeping.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie's grandmother dies. It's the first close death in the girl's life. Claire doesn't tell her that grandma "went on a trip" or "is sleeping." She explains, in simple words, that grandma died, that it's natural and sad, and that they can remember her. Together they make a list of the things they learned from her: a little Book I. Sophie learns that death can be named, cried over, and turned into gratitude.

### In Your Family: The Overdue Conversation

Rick and his brother, the one who lives near their mother and hardly ever visits, had a falling-out a year ago. Their last argument was a shouting match in their mother's kitchen, and since then they speak only when they have to. Both believe they're right.

When an uncle, their father's brother, is diagnosed with a serious illness, Rick catches himself thinking something he didn't want to think: someday, one of the two brothers will bury the other, and the fight would still be there, unresolved.

He sends a short message: "I don't know if I'm right or not. But I don't want this to be the last thing between us." His brother takes two days to answer. They meet at a bar near their mother's house. They don't resolve everything. But the following Sunday, for the first time in a year, they have lunch together with her.

### In Money and Health: The Postponed Checkup

Andrew hasn't had a checkup in four years. There's always something more urgent: work, errands, the hassle of going to the clinic. After his father's heart attack, described at the start of this chapter, he remembers Marcus's line: don't act as if you had ten thousand years to live.

That same week he makes an appointment. The tests come back mostly fine: slightly high cholesterol, nothing serious. The doctor tells him there's still time. Andrew leaves the clinic thinking that taking care of his health isn't just a personal matter: it's a way of taking care of Lauren and their son, who are counting on him to be there.

## Author's Note

From the moment my father got sick, the problems at work began to change size. The urgent report, the tense meeting, the email that would once have kept me up at night: it all grew smaller. And when he died, I felt the weight of many things I had believed were important lift off my shoulders. I'm not writing this to say grief has a bright side; it doesn't. I'm writing it because it left me with a question I still use: will this matter a year from now? The answer is almost always no. And when the answer is yes, it's almost always about a person, not a task.

## What This Chapter Does Not Say

**"Live every day as if it were your last."** Not in the sense of "spend everything, go wild." Marcus suggests living each day with the seriousness and care of someone who knows time is limited, not with the desperation of someone who believes it ends tomorrow.

**"You shouldn't fear death."** Fear is natural. What Stoicism proposes is that fear not stop you from living.

**"You should think about death all the time."** No. A brief reminder, now and then, is enough to change your priorities. Thinking about it all day isn't Stoic; it's anxiety.

## This Week's Exercise: The Last Time

1. Think of an everyday activity with someone you love: breakfast with your partner, taking your kid to school, a phone call with your mother.
2. Ask yourself calmly: **what if this were the last time?** Not to distress yourself, but to pay attention.
3. Do it this week with that awareness: no phone, looking, listening.
4. Write a letter to someone important telling them what you'd want them to know if you weren't here tomorrow. You don't have to give it to them. But if you decide to, all the better.
5. Look at your calendar and ask: **what here would I do if I knew my time was limited?** Because it is.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** tonight, before you sleep, say as Seneca did: "I have lived." Think of one thing from the day that was worth it.
- **Intermediate:** write a list of the three conversations, visits, or actions you've been putting off and would regret not having done. Choose one and do it this month.
- **Advanced:** write your own obituary, not the one you'd have if you died today, but the one you'd like someone to write many years from now. Then compare it with your calendar for this week. The distance between the two is your unfinished work.

## Key Ideas

- Remembering death doesn't take meaning from life: it gives it a deadline.
- The urgent shouts; the important whispers.
- In the long run, we regret most what we didn't do.
- While you live, while you can, be good.
