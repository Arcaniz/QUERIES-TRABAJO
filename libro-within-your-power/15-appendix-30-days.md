# Appendix: A 30-Day Stoic Routine

This routine brings together the exercises in the book into a one-month program. You don't need more than fifteen minutes a day: five in the morning, five at some point during the day, and five at night. You can start on any day.

## The Daily Structure

### In the Morning (5 minutes)

Before checking your phone:

1. **The blanket sentence** (Chapter 3): read the sentence that answers "why do I get up?"
2. **The day's rehearsal** (Chapter 2): think of the most difficult person or situation today. What's likely to happen? How will you respond?
3. **What's necessary** (Chapter 9): choose the three necessary things for the day.

### During the Day (5 minutes, spread out)

- When you feel anger: **the card with the nine reminders** (Chapter 6).
- When you feel anxious: **what is unbearable in this moment?** (Chapter 10).
- When you want to buy something or envy someone: **the stripped-bare definition** (Chapter 7).
- Before an important decision: **is this good for the hive?** (Chapter 11).

### At Night (5 minutes)

Seneca tells us that the philosopher Sextius, every night before sleeping, asked himself: What bad habit did I cure today? What fault did I resist? In what way am I better? Seneca adopted the habit. You can do the same with three questions:

1. What did I do well today?
2. Where did I go wrong?
3. What would I do differently tomorrow?

And add one line of specific gratitude (Chapter 12): "Today I'm grateful that ___."

## The 30-Day Program

Each week focuses on a group of chapters. In addition to the daily routine, do the day's special exercise.

### Week 1. What Is Within Your Power (Chapters 1, 2, and 3)

| Day | Special exercise |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do the two columns (Chapter 1) for the situation that worries you most. |
| 2 | Write your blanket sentence (Chapter 3) and put it on your phone. |
| 3 | Identify the most difficult person in your week and write one sentence of understanding about them. |
| 4 | Find a judgment you're treating as a fact. Write the bare fact and the interpretation separately. |
| 5 | Get up within the first two minutes of the alarm. No negotiating. |
| 6 | Prepare in writing for a difficult conversation you've been putting off: what the other person will say, what you'll answer. |
| 7 | Review the week: which column did you spend more energy on? |

### Week 2. Mind and Emotions (Chapters 4, 5, and 6)

| Day | Special exercise |
|---|---|
| 8 | Do the third column (Chapter 4): what does this obstacle allow me to do? |
| 9 | Audit your dye (Chapter 5): write down everything your mind consumes today. |
| 10 | Cut one source of bad dye in half. |
| 11 | Write the card with the nine reminders against anger and carry it with you. |
| 12 | Apply the 24-hour rule to any message written while angry. |
| 13 | Correct someone with firm kindness: say what you think without trying to hurt. |
| 14 | Review the week: what color was your mind? |

### Week 3. The Outside World (Chapters 7, 8, and 9)

| Day | Special exercise |
|---|---|
| 15 | Write the stripped-bare definition of something you want to buy. |
| 16 | Describe a recent offense stripped bare, without interpretation. |
| 17 | Write your Antoninus portrait: five behaviors of the best person with power you've known. |
| 18 | Give special attention to someone who can't do anything for you. |
| 19 | Prune your calendar: cancel or delegate one unnecessary commitment. |
| 20 | Delete from your list the to-dos older than a month that have had no consequences. |
| 21 | Review the week: what story are you buying? Where has power gone to your head? |

### Week 4. Meaning (Chapters 10 to 13)

| Day | Special exercise |
|---|---|
| 22 | Put a notebook by your bed for the 3 a.m. page (Chapter 10). |
| 23 | Write your three scenarios (good, average, bad) for your biggest financial or work worry. |
| 24 | Draw Hierocles's circles (Chapter 11) and bring someone from an outer circle a little closer. |
| 25 | In your next argument, sit on the same side as the other person. |
| 26 | Write your Book I (Chapter 12): ten people and what you learned from each. |
| 27 | Send a message of specific gratitude to one person on that list. |
| 28 | Do an everyday activity with someone you love as if it were the last time (Chapter 13). |
| 29 | Write the letter of what you'd want that person to know. |
| 30 | Reread your notes from the month. Write in one sentence what changed, and choose the chapter you'll keep practicing. |

## Your Own Notebook

Marcus Aurelius didn't have this book. He had a notebook. I suggest you keep one too, paper or digital, just for this. Write in it the journaling questions from each chapter, your answers, and what you discover along the way. Over time, that notebook will be more yours and more useful than any book, including this one.

### The Thirteen Journaling Questions

1. What judgment am I treating as if it were a fact?
2. What am I silently demanding from someone who never promised to give it to me?
3. What do I do in the world that, if I didn't do it, no one else would do in quite the same way?
4. If this obstacle were a teacher, what would it be forcing me to learn?
5. If someone read my thoughts from this week, what color would they say my mind is?
6. In what recent situation did I become something like the person who offended me?
7. What in my life am I paying more for the story than for what it is?
8. Whom do I treat differently because they can't do anything for me?
9. What am I doing out of fear of saying no?
10. What disaster have I already suffered many times in my imagination without it ever happening?
11. In what situation am I winning while the "us" is losing?
12. What do I do today, without realizing it, that I learned from someone I never thanked?
13. What am I putting off as if I had ten thousand years?
