# Prologue

## What an Emperor Taught Me Without Knowing It

I met Marcus Aurelius in a university classroom, in a philosophy course that, to be honest, felt far removed from anything I needed at the time. We read a few passages from the *Meditations*, and something caught me off guard: this man, who ruled the largest empire of his age, didn't write about his power. He wrote about his doubts. He scolded himself, encouraged himself, reminded himself of what mattered. He sounded like someone trying hard not to lose his head.

Back then I read him the way you read a text for an exam. Years later I read him again, the way you read a letter that arrives exactly when you need it. This book was born from that second reading, and from three moments in my life when those ancient notes helped me more than any modern advice.

### More Responsibility, Same Paycheck

The first was a promotion. I was given a role with more responsibility, more people depending on my decisions, and more pressure, but with the same salary. At first I experienced it as an injustice, and in part it was. I came home turning the same things over and over: what they were asking of me, what they weren't paying me, what no one acknowledged.

That's when I remembered the simplest idea in Stoicism: some things are within my power and some are not. My salary was not within my power; the conversation to ask for a raise was. The pressure of new challenges was not within my power; how I organized my day and how I treated the people now reporting to me was. Marcus warned himself not to be "dyed purple," not to let power make him worse. I didn't have an empire, but I had a team and a new title, and I discovered that the real test of a promotion wasn't just whether I could handle the work, but who I would become while doing it.

### Losing My Father

The second moment was the hardest of my life: my father's illness and death.

I won't say philosophy took away the pain. It didn't, and I don't think it should. But something happened that I didn't expect. From the moment my father got sick, everyday problems began to matter less. The email that had made me angry, the meeting that had gone badly, the deadline that kept me up at night, suddenly appeared at their true size. Small.

And when he died, I felt the weight of everything else lift off my shoulders, everything I had believed was important and wasn't. I was left with only what was essential.

Marcus wrote that we should not act as if we had ten thousand years to live. I had read that line many times. I only understood it when my father was gone. Since then, whenever something at work weighs on me too much, I try to ask myself one question: will this matter a year from now? The answer is almost never yes. What does matter is people, the time we have with them, and what we say to them while we still can.

### Studying, Working, and Coming Home Exhausted

The third moment wasn't an event but a season: working and studying at the same time. I went straight from the office to class, or from class to work, and came home so tired that all I wanted was not to think. There were nights I wondered why I had signed up for any of it.

There is a passage in the *Meditations* where Marcus argues with himself about getting out of bed. He tells himself he was born to do the work of a human being, not to stay under the blankets. It made me smile to know that even the emperor struggled. And it helped me with something else: understanding that my exhaustion was real, but it didn't get to make my decisions for me. There was a reason to keep going, and that reason was mine. I also learned, the hard way, that rest is part of the work, and that not everything I demanded of myself was necessary.

### Why I Wrote This Book

I'm not a professional philosopher, and I don't pretend to be. I'm someone who works, who studied while exhausted, who was handed more responsibility than he was paid for, and who lost his father. Someone who gets angry, makes mistakes, and starts again. What you'll find here isn't a theory. It's what I wish I had read in each of those moments: the ideas of Marcus Aurelius explained in several ways, with examples from relationships, work, business, and school, and with exercises to put them into practice.

Marcus wrote for himself and never imagined anyone would read his notes. I wrote this thinking of you: the person who comes home drained, the person carrying more than their share, the person grieving who doesn't know how to go back to work as if nothing happened. I hope you find in these pages what I found in his: not final answers, but a calmer way of asking the questions.

In memory of my father.

**Francisco Javier Ballesteros**
# Introduction

## An Emperor's Secret Notebook

It's night in an army camp on the banks of the Danube. Outside, it's cold. There are wounded men, and the enemy is out there somewhere, unseen but close. Inside a tent, a tired man in his fifties, in poor health, is writing by lamplight. He rules the largest empire on earth. He could be writing about his own glory. Instead, he's giving himself a talking-to.

He reminds himself that tomorrow he'll deal with nosy, ungrateful, arrogant people, and that he mustn't get angry at them. He tells himself that getting out of bed isn't a punishment; it's the work he was born to do. He warns himself not to let power go to his head. And again and again, he reminds himself that he is going to die.

That man was Marcus Aurelius. His notebook was never meant for us. That's exactly why it works.

The *Meditations* aren't really a book. They're the private notes of a man trying to hold himself together through war, plague, betrayal, and a difficult family. He wasn't trying to persuade anybody. He was trying to persuade himself. And when you read those notes today, on the train, in a waiting room, or lying awake at two in the morning, something strange happens: they sound like they were written about your week.

Because your problems aren't so different from his. You don't command legions, but you have a boss who reshuffles the priorities every Monday. You don't negotiate with Germanic tribes, but you do negotiate with a client who hasn't paid you in three months. You don't have a Senate plotting against you, but you do have a family group chat. And like him, you've got exactly one mind to handle all of it.

### What This Book Is, and What It Isn't

Stoicism has gotten trendy, and like every trend, it's been bent out of shape. On social media it shows up as a philosophy for tough guys who never cry, for founders who get up at four a.m., for people who "don't feel anything." None of that is in Marcus Aurelius. The emperor cried, doubted himself, made mistakes, and wrote them down. His Stoicism wasn't armor against emotion. It was a craft for not being ruled by it.

This book won't promise to make you happy, rich, or unshakable. What it offers is more modest and more useful: learning to tell what's within your power from what isn't, and putting your energy where it can actually do some good. Practiced consistently, that changes a marriage, a career, a business, and the way you learn.

### How to Read It

Each chapter starts with one of Marcus's meditations. You won't find a single "correct" reading; you'll find several, because a text that has survived two thousand years can be read more than one way, and because the reading that helps you will depend on where you are in life. Next you'll hear what two other great Stoics, Seneca and Epictetus, had to say on the same subject, along with what some current research suggests. Then you'll listen in on a short conversation with a skeptic who pushes back on the idea. After that come six scenes from adult life: relationships, work, business, school (whether you're working on a degree, heading back to school at forty, or helping your kids with theirs), family, and money and health. Every chapter closes with an exercise, offered at three levels, a summary of key ideas, and a journaling question. Do them. Marcus didn't read philosophy; he practiced it in a notebook. You can too.

Along the way you'll meet a few people you might recognize: Lauren and Andrew, ten years into their marriage; Rick, who's spent more than a decade at the same company; Megan, who runs a small design studio; George, thirty-eight, working on a weekend MBA; and Claire, mom to ten-year-old Sophie. They aren't real, but they look a lot like people you know. Maybe a lot like you.

You can read the book straight through, or open it wherever it hurts. There are chapters for the morning you can't face getting up, for the night after a fight, and for the week when everything falls apart. At the end you'll find a thirty-day routine and a quiz to see how much of this has stuck.

### A Note on the Quotations

Marcus Aurelius wrote in Greek. The lines that open each chapter are paraphrases that stay true to the meaning of the original, not quotations from any particular translation. The book and section numbers are given in parentheses, so you can look up each passage in whatever edition you like and compare.

### One Last Warning

Marcus told himself to stop arguing about what a good man is and just be one. It's the best warning any reader of philosophy could get, including the person who wrote these pages. This book isn't for underlining, or for quoting in meetings. It's for Tuesday afternoon, when someone gets under your skin and you remember, just in time, that you have a choice.

So let's start there: with what's within your power.
# Before We Begin: A Brief History of Stoicism

## A Shipwrecked Merchant in Athens

The story of Stoicism begins with a shipwreck. Around 300 BCE, a Phoenician merchant named Zeno, from Citium on the island of Cyprus, lost his cargo at sea and arrived in Athens with nothing. According to Diogenes Laertius, an ancient biographer, Zeno walked into a bookshop, began reading about Socrates, and asked where he could find men like that. The bookseller pointed to Crates, a Cynic philosopher who happened to be walking by. Zeno followed him.

Years later, Zeno began teaching on his own. He didn't do it in a closed school but in a public spot in the Athenian marketplace: a colonnade decorated with paintings, called the *Stoa Poikile*, the "Painted Porch." That's where the name comes from: Stoics, "the people of the porch." It was a philosophy born in the street, open to anyone who walked by.

Zeno is credited with a line about his own story: "I made a prosperous voyage when I was shipwrecked." It may be the first Stoic lesson: what stands in the way becomes the way.

## What They Taught

The early Stoics divided philosophy into three parts and compared it to an orchard: logic was the fence, physics was the tree, and ethics was the fruit. Logic was for thinking clearly and not being fooled by appearances. Physics studied nature and the universe, which the Stoics saw as an ordered whole governed by reason, which they called the *logos*. Ethics, the fruit, taught how to live well.

To live well, they said, you must live in agreement with nature. And since human nature is rational and social, living well means using reason and living for the good of the community. They called this *virtue*, and divided it into four: **wisdom** (knowing what is good, what is bad, and what is neither), **justice** (giving each person their due and treating others well), **courage** (facing fear and pain), and **self-control** (mastering desire and keeping measure in all things).

Everything else (health, wealth, fame, even life itself) was, for them, *indifferent*. Not in the sense that it didn't matter, but in the sense that it is neither good nor bad in itself: it depends on how it is used. A millionaire can be generous or cruel; a sick person can be brave or bitter. What makes a life good isn't what happens to you, but how you respond.

## What About the Gods?

There's something many modern books on Stoicism prefer not to mention, and it's worth saying up front: the ancient Stoics were deeply religious, in their own way. They believed the universe was ordered by a divine reason, the *logos*, which they sometimes called Zeus, nature, or providence. For them, everything that happens is part of that order, so accepting what is not within our power wasn't surrender to chaos but cooperation with a whole that makes sense.

Marcus Aurelius shared that belief. Book I of the *Meditations* ends with thanks to the gods, and many passages speak of "the nature of the whole" and of welcoming what life brings. But he was also honest about doubt. Several times he poses a dilemma: either the universe is governed by providence, or it is just a jumble of atoms coming together and drifting apart by chance (see, for example, IV.3). And he reaches a remarkable conclusion: in either case, the right way to live is the same. If there is providence, live in harmony with it. If there isn't, all the more reason to govern yourself by your own reason and not be swept along.

This book offers a practical reading of Stoicism that can serve believers of any faith and nonbelievers alike. That's not because Stoic theology doesn't matter, but because, as Marcus himself sensed, the part of his philosophy that touches our daily life works either way. Still, you should know that the emperor was not a modern secular thinker: he prayed, he gave thanks, and he believed the universe had an order.

## From Athens to Rome

After Zeno, the school had other leaders in Athens, such as Cleanthes, a former boxer who carried water at night to pay for his studies, and Chrysippus, a thinker so prolific that it was said that without him there would have been no Stoa. Over time Stoicism traveled to Rome, where it found fertile ground. The Romans, a practical people, cared less about logic and physics and more about ethics: how to govern, how to face adversity, how to die with dignity.

That's how the three great Roman Stoics emerged, whose writings we still have nearly complete and who appear throughout this book:

**Seneca** (c. 4 BCE–65 CE) was a writer, a politician, one of the richest men in Rome, and tutor to the emperor Nero. He wrote letters, essays, and tragedies. His prose is the warmest and most elegant of the three. His life was full of contradictions: he preached simplicity surrounded by luxury and advised an emperor who became a tyrant. Nero ordered him to take his own life, and Seneca, according to the historian Tacitus, did so calmly.

**Epictetus** (c. 50–135 CE) was born a slave in Phrygia, in what is now Turkey. He studied with the Stoic philosopher Musonius Rufus and, once freed, founded a school in Nicopolis, in Greece. He wrote nothing himself; his lessons were recorded by a student, Arrian, in two works: the *Discourses* and a short summary, the *Handbook* (or *Enchiridion*). His style is direct, sometimes blunt, always practical.

**Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE) was emperor of Rome for nearly twenty years. Adopted by his uncle Antoninus Pius at the request of the emperor Hadrian, he was trained in philosophy from a young age. He ruled in times of war and plague, and wrote, in Greek and for himself alone, the notebooks we now know as the *Meditations*. He probably would have titled them differently; one name found in the manuscripts is *To Himself*.

## How the Meditations Reached Us

Marcus never meant to publish his notes. We don't know exactly how they were preserved after his death. For centuries they are barely mentioned; a few Byzantine writers quote them. The first printed edition appeared in Zurich around 1558–1559, and they have been read ever since. They have sat on the nightstands of kings, presidents, soldiers, prisoners, and ordinary people looking, like Marcus, for a way not to lose their heads.

## Stoicism Today

In the twentieth century, Stoicism had an unexpected second life through psychology. Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, founders of the cognitive therapies, acknowledged the influence of Epictetus's idea that we are disturbed not by things but by our opinions about them. Viktor Frankl, the psychiatrist who survived the Nazi concentration camps, wrote that everything can be taken from a person but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude in any set of circumstances. He didn't call himself a Stoic, but the line could have come from Epictetus.

In recent decades, philosophers such as Pierre Hadot, Martha Nussbaum, Massimo Pigliucci, and William B. Irvine have helped recover Stoicism as a way of life rather than just an academic subject. And on social media it has become a trend, with all the good and bad that implies: more people know about it, but many know a distorted version.

This book tries to go back to the source. Not to the Stoicism of motivational quotes, but to that of a tired man in a tent, trying to be a little better each day.

## Where This Book Fits

Each chapter that follows takes a passage from the *Meditations* and sets it in conversation with Seneca, with Epictetus, and with some current research. But Marcus is always at the center, because he is the only one of the three who wrote for himself, and that makes him the most like us: someone with responsibilities, with fatigue, with difficult people around him, who didn't have all the answers and who still, every night, opened his notebook again.
# Chapter 1. What Is Within Your Power

> *If something outside you is troubling you, it isn't the thing itself that disturbs you. It's your judgment about it. And you can wipe that judgment out right now.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VIII.47 (paraphrase)

> *Drop the opinion, and "I've been wronged" disappears. Drop "I've been wronged," and the wrong disappears.*
> — *Meditations*, IV.7 (paraphrase)

It's 8:47 a.m. You've been crawling through traffic for twenty minutes, and you're going to be late for a meeting you never wanted in the first place. The light turns green. The car in front of you doesn't move. You feel the heat climb up the back of your neck. You lean on the horn. You smack the steering wheel. In your head, you're already telling that driver exactly what you think of him.

Now consider this: the light, the traffic, the distracted driver, and the meeting time are all out of your hands. The only thing you control in that moment is what you do with all of it on the inside. And that, the one thing you actually control, is exactly where you're losing.

That's the heart of Stoicism. Everything else in this book grows out of it.

## The Context: An Emperor Who Controlled Almost Nothing

We like to imagine that a Roman emperor could do whatever he pleased. Marcus Aurelius knew better. During his reign (161–180 CE), an epidemic, probably smallpox, killed millions across the empire. The northern frontier was at war almost without pause. One of his most trusted generals, Avidius Cassius, declared himself emperor after hearing a false rumor that Marcus was dead. Several of Marcus's children died young.

He held more power than anyone alive, and he still couldn't stop a disease, guarantee anyone's loyalty, or keep the people he loved from dying.

The idea he keeps circling back to in his notebooks wasn't his own. He got it from a former slave named Epictetus, whose teachings reached him through his tutor Junius Rusticus, a debt Marcus gratefully records in Book I of the *Meditations*. Epictetus had boiled it down in his *Handbook*: some things are within our power (our judgments, our intentions, our actions), and some things aren't (our bodies, our reputations, our money, what other people do).

Think about that for a second. The most powerful man of his age learned his most important lesson from a man who was born a slave. They arrived at the same conclusion from opposite ends of the social ladder: freedom isn't out there.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: The Harm Lives in the Judgment

The Stoics noticed something most of us miss: between what happens and how we feel about it, there's a middle step. They called it **judgment**. Something happens (your partner doesn't answer a text). An impression shows up ("they're ignoring me"). Then you either *accept* that impression as true or you don't. The Stoics called that move *assent*.

What hurts, Marcus says, isn't the unanswered text. It's the story you signed off on: "I don't matter to them." "They're probably with someone else." "They always do this."

That doesn't mean nothing bad ever happens. It means there's a gap between the blow and the wound, and that gap belongs to you.

**In a nutshell:** you don't control what happens; you control what you tell yourself about it.

### Reading 2. The Psychological: Stoicism as Therapy's Ancestor

In the twentieth century, psychologists Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck developed approaches that are now used around the world to treat anxiety and depression: rational emotive behavior therapy and cognitive therapy. Ellis openly credited Epictetus as an inspiration. The core idea is nearly identical: events don't upset us; our beliefs about events do. Change the belief, and the feeling changes with it.

Read this way, VIII.47 becomes a technique:

1. Name the emotion ("I'm furious").
2. Find the judgment underneath it ("my boss humiliated me on purpose").
3. Ask whether that judgment is a fact or an interpretation.
4. Try on another interpretation ("my boss is under pressure and snapped at everybody").

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Isn't This Just a Recipe for Putting Up with Things?

This idea has a dangerous reading, and it deserves an honest look.

If all the harm lives "in my judgment," should someone being abused by their partner simply "change their perspective"? Should a worker who isn't being paid for overtime just make peace with it? Is Stoicism a philosophy designed to keep people at the bottom quiet?

It's the strongest objection there is, and it deserves a real answer. The answer is **no**, for three reasons.

- **First:** saying your judgment is within your power doesn't mean you have to judge that everything is fine. You can see, with total clarity, that something is unjust. What Stoicism asks is that you not pile a second, useless layer of suffering on top of the injustice: the replaying, the self-pity, the resentment that eats at you instead of the person who wronged you.
- **Second:** your *actions* are within your power. Reporting it, quitting, setting a boundary, ending a relationship, negotiating, demanding what you're owed: all of that is in your hands. Stoics don't sit still. They act with a cool head instead of a hot one.
- **Third:** Marcus wrote again and again that we exist for one another, and justice is one of the four core Stoic virtues. A Stoic who shrugs at injustice done to others has missed the point entirely.

> **Important.** If you're experiencing physical, emotional, sexual, or financial abuse in your relationship, the Stoic response isn't to "change your judgment" or tough it out. It's to protect yourself and get help. Reaching out is within your power. In the United States, you can contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788. In an emergency, call 911. Outside the U.S., contact your local emergency number or support services.

The contemporary philosopher William B. Irvine suggests a useful refinement: there aren't two categories, there are three. What you control completely (your judgments, your values), what you don't control at all (the weather, the past), and **what you control in part** (winning a lawsuit, landing a promotion, saving a marriage). For that middle zone, the Stoics had a lovely image, which Cicero puts in the mouth of a Stoic speaker: the archer. The archer controls his training, his stance, the draw of the bow, and the moment he lets go. He doesn't control the wind, or whether the target moves. His goal isn't to *hit the target*. It's to *shoot well*. If he shoots well and misses, he hasn't failed.

**In a nutshell:** accepting what's outside your power isn't giving up; it's quitting your fight with the wind so you can focus on your shot.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: It's Not the Things, It's the Opinions

Epictetus put it in one line that Marcus echoed, in his own words, again and again: people aren't disturbed by things, but by their opinions about things. His example is death. Death isn't terrible, he says; if it were, Socrates would have thought so, and Socrates met it calmly. What's terrible is the opinion that death is terrible.

Epictetus wasn't theorizing from an armchair. He was born a slave, walked with a limp, and spent much of his life without even owning his own body. An old tradition, which comes down to us through the theologian Origen, who got it from a writer named Celsus, says his master once twisted his leg for fun. Epictetus calmly warned him, "You're going to break it." When the leg snapped, he said only, "Didn't I tell you?" Maybe it didn't happen quite like that. But it explains why his students believed him: he wasn't teaching from a place of comfort.

In the *Handbook*, Epictetus sorts the world into two piles. Within our power: opinion, impulse, desire, aversion; in short, everything that's our own doing. Not within our power: the body, possessions, reputation, public office; everything that isn't our own doing. And he warns: treat what belongs to others as if it were yours, and you'll spend your life complaining and blaming gods and people alike. Treat as yours only what really is yours, and no one will ever be able to force you or stand in your way.

### Seneca: Fortune Can't Take What It Never Gave

Seneca, who was spectacularly rich and later lost nearly everything, comes at the same principle from the side of money and luck. In one of his letters, he writes that fortune can't take away what it never gave. Health, possessions, status, and fame are on loan from fortune, and she can call them in at any time. Character isn't a loan.

Seneca didn't ask anyone to sneer at what's borrowed. He asked us to use it knowing it's borrowed. You can tell the difference when it's gone: if you knew it was a loan, you hand it back with sadness; if you thought it was yours, you hand it back with despair.

### What Science Says Today

Psychology has been studying something close to the Stoic distinction for decades. In the 1960s, psychologist Julian Rotter introduced the idea of *locus of control*. Some people tend to credit what happens to them to outside forces (luck, other people, the system); others tend to credit their own choices. Broadly speaking, a more internal locus of control has been linked to more initiative and better coping with stress, though too much of it brings its own risk: blaming yourself for things that were never within your power.

Research on emotion regulation, including work by psychologist James Gross, distinguishes between *suppressing* an emotion (keeping a lid on it) and *reappraising* the situation (changing how you interpret it). In many studies, reappraisal is linked to better outcomes than suppression. That's almost exactly what Marcus was after: not pretending you don't feel anything, but examining the judgment that's producing the feeling.

Science doesn't "prove" Stoicism, and you should be suspicious of anyone who claims it does. But it does suggest this ancient instinct is pointing in a sensible direction.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** This all sounds great, but it feels like a trap. If the problem is my judgment, then the problem is me.

**Author:** Part of the problem is your judgment. Not all of it. If you get fired unfairly, the firing is real and so is the unfairness. What Stoicism points out is that you can strap a second weight onto the first: the story that you're worthless, that it's all over, that no one will ever hire you again. That second weight is yours, which means you can set it down.

**Skeptic:** And if my judgment is right? If I think my boss is unfair and he actually is, why should I change my mind?

**Author:** You shouldn't. A true judgment isn't the problem. The trouble starts when you tack on false ones: "this is unfair" turns into "so my life is ruined," or "so I'm entitled to get even." Marcus isn't asking you to think everything's fine. He's asking you to think accurately.

**Skeptic:** But nobody chooses their emotions. You don't decide to be scared.

**Author:** Agreed, and the Stoics would agree too. You don't choose the first jolt. But you do choose what happens next. Do you feed it for hours? Do you turn it into a snap decision? That's where your responsibility starts. Not before.

**Skeptic:** So this is basically a technique for not suffering?

**Author:** It's a technique for not suffering more than you have to. Some pain you can't avoid, and nobody's promising otherwise. What you can avoid is the suffering you manufacture on top of the pain. And honestly, for most adults, the manufactured kind is the bigger share.

**Skeptic:** Who decides what's within my power, though? A lot of things depend on me a little.

**Author:** That's exactly why Irvine talks about three zones instead of two. In the middle zone, the archer's rule applies: do your part well and let go of the result. If you get the promotion, great. If you don't, you still did your part. The alternative is to hang your peace of mind on something you'll never fully control. Call that what it really is: gambling your sanity on luck.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Left on Read

At 2 p.m., Lauren texts Andrew: "Can you pick up your mom's prescription?" The message shows as read. Two hours pass. Nothing.

By 4:00, Lauren has the whole story written: he never takes her seriously, he leaves everything to her, he always answers his mother right away. When he walks in at 7 with the prescription in hand (he picked it up; he just forgot to reply), she's already spent five hours arguing with an imaginary Andrew. The fight they have that night isn't about the prescription.

**What wasn't within Lauren's power:** when Andrew would reply, or why he didn't.
**What was within her power:** the story she chose to believe and what she did with it.

The Stoic move isn't pretending it didn't bother her. It's separating the fact ("he didn't answer for two hours") from the interpretation ("I don't matter to him"), and then acting: calling him, or telling him calmly that evening, "When you don't reply, I'm left hanging. Could you just send a quick 'ok'?" That's a boundary. The other thing is a novel.

**For long-term couples:** a lot of years-long fights are really fights with a judgment on repeat: "always," "never," "again." Ask yourself whether you're reacting to what your partner did today, or to who you've decided they are.

### At Work: The Reorg

Rick has been with the company for eleven years. One Friday, an email goes out announcing a reorganization: his department is merging with another one, and he'll be reporting to a new boss eight years younger than he is.

Rick spends the weekend stewing: they're trying to push him out, they don't value his experience, it's not fair.

**What isn't within his power:** the company's decision, who his boss will be, management's real motives.
**What's partly within his power:** his future at the company.
**What's fully within his power:** how he shows up Monday morning, the quality of his work, whether he updates his résumé, whether he asks his new boss for a straight conversation, and how much of his time he hands over to stewing.

The Stoic archer would do three things: get information instead of guessing ("What do you need from me in the new structure?"), do his best work yet (not for the company, but for his own character), and quietly line up a plan B. If they let him go anyway, he'll have shot well.

### In Business: The Client Who Won't Pay

Megan runs a small design studio. Her biggest client owes her three invoices, close to 40 percent of her revenue for the quarter. He answers every email with "definitely this week," and that week never comes.

Megan can't sleep. She checks her bank account five times a day. She pictures the studio going under.

The meditation doesn't ask Megan to stop caring. Her anxiety makes perfect sense. It asks for something more practical: **pull your energy out of the places where it does nothing and put it where it counts.**

| Not within Megan's power | Within Megan's power |
|---|---|
| Whether the client pays this week | Sending a formal statement with a firm deadline |
| The client's cash flow | Pausing new work until the account is current |
| Whether the economy picks up | Landing two more clients so she isn't dependent on one |
| What the client thinks of her | Looking into legal or collections options |
| | Rewriting her contracts: deposits and milestone payments |

Checking her balance five times a day belongs in the left column, dressed up as the right one: it looks like action, but it's really anxiety. Every hour Megan spends imagining bankruptcy is an hour she isn't spending finding new clients.

**The lesson for entrepreneurs:** your business depends on plenty of things you don't control (the market, the competition, interest rates). That's exactly why your edge lies in being relentless about what you do control: your processes, your contracts, your service, your financial discipline.

### At School: The Midterm and the Playground

**If you're studying as an adult:** George is thirty-eight and working on a weekend MBA on top of a full-time job. He fails his first statistics midterm. His instant verdict: "I'm too old for this." "The professor has it out for me." "I'm just not a numbers guy."

All three are judgments, not facts. The fact is: "I failed one exam." Nothing about that makes George incapable, over the hill, or the professor's victim. Within his power: how many hours he studies, whether he goes to office hours, whether he starts a study group, whether he goes back over what he got wrong. Not within his power: the professor's teaching style, how hard the exam was, his age.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire gets a message from the teacher: her ten-year-old, Sophie, got into a fight at recess. Claire feels a mix of embarrassment ("What are the other parents going to think of me?") and anger ("I bet the other girl started it").

Here the meditation cuts both ways. First, for Claire: what the other parents think isn't within her power; how she listens to her daughter is. Second, it's a chance to teach Sophie the most useful idea in this book: *you can't control what the other girl said; you can control what you did next*. Very few adults learned that as kids. Imagine what changes when you learn it at ten.

### In Your Family: The Brother Who Doesn't Show Up

Rick's mother is seventy-eight and needs more help every month. Rick has two brothers. One lives out of state; the other lives fifteen minutes away and almost never comes by. Rick drives her to the doctor, picks up her prescriptions, fixes the leaky faucet. Every time, he feels a low, steady anger toward his brother.

He does the two-column exercise. **Not within his power:** whether his brother changes, his mother getting older, what his brother thinks his role should be. **Within his power:** taking care of his mother the way he wants to, asking his brother for something specific instead of something vague ("Can you take her to the doctor the last Tuesday of every month?"), and deciding how much he can give without wrecking his own health or his family.

Rick makes the specific ask. His brother meets him halfway: he shows up every other time. It isn't fair. But Rick finds that his anger shrinks once he stops fighting with the brother he wishes he had and starts negotiating with the one he's got.

### In Money and Health: The Diagnosis

Megan goes in for a checkup and walks out with a prediabetes diagnosis. That night she googles everything and ends up imagining insulin shots and amputations. She doesn't sleep.

Meditation VIII.47 hits with full force here. **The fact:** her blood sugar is a little high, and her doctor says there's still plenty of time. **The judgment:** "I'm going to end up sick like my aunt." There's a canyon between those two, and Megan crossed it alone in three hours.

**Within her power:** what she eats, how much she walks, whether she goes to her follow-up, how much she sleeps. **Not within her power:** her genes, her family history, exactly what her numbers will be in six months.

Megan tapes a two-column list to her fridge. She follows her doctor's instructions and keeps every follow-up appointment. Six months later, her blood sugar is back in the normal range. Not because philosophy cures anything, but because she stopped spending her energy in the wrong column.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"You shouldn't feel anything."** Wrong. The Stoics accepted our first, involuntary reactions: the startle, the knot in your stomach, the flush in your face. Seneca called them "first movements" and said not even the wisest person is free of them. What you train is what you do *after* that first movement. And the Stoics weren't aiming for coldness, either. They described healthy emotions, which they called *eupatheiai*: calm joy, sensible caution, goodwill toward others. Stoics feel; what they avoid is being swept away by runaway passions.

**"Everything is your fault, because it all depends on how you look at it."** Wrong. Your judgments are within your power, but what other people do to you isn't. Owning your response isn't the same as blaming yourself for the blow.

**"If it's not within my power, I shouldn't care about it."** Wrong. You can care deeply that your father is sick. His diagnosis isn't within your power. Being there for him is. Stoicism doesn't ask for indifference; it asks you to point your love toward whatever action is actually possible.

**"This is just positive thinking: think happy thoughts and it'll all work out."** Wrong. Stoicism doesn't promise that things will turn out well. It promises that however they turn out, you can meet them with dignity. It's more realistic than optimistic.

## This Week's Exercise: The Two Columns

Every night this week, before bed:

1. Write down the situation that weighed on you most today.
2. Split a page into two columns: **"Within my power"** and **"Not within my power."**
3. Sort every piece of the situation into its column. Be strict: "my boss appreciating me" goes on the right; "getting the report in on time" goes on the left.
4. Mentally let go of the right-hand column. Not because it doesn't matter, but because there's nothing you can do about it tonight.
5. Pick **one single action** from the left-hand column for tomorrow.

At the end of the week, look back over your pages. You'll probably notice two things: you spend more energy on the right-hand column than you realized, and the left-hand column always had something in it you could do.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** do the two columns for just one situation a week, whichever one is weighing on you most.
- **Intermediate:** add a third column for what's *partly* within your power, as Irvine suggests, and note exactly which part is yours.
- **Advanced:** for one whole day, every time you complain, out loud or in your head, stop and ask: "Is this in my column or the other one?" Keep a tally. At the end of the day, count how many of your complaints were about things that were never within your power.

## Key Ideas

- Between what happens and what you feel, there's a judgment, and that judgment is yours.
- Always separate the fact from the interpretation.
- There are three zones: what you control, what you don't, and what you partly control. In the third, shoot well and let go of the result.
- Accepting what's outside your power isn't resignation: your actions are always within your power.
- Stoics feel; what they avoid is being swept away by their passions.

# Chapter 2. At Dawn, Brace Yourself for Difficult People

> *When the day begins, tell yourself: today I'll run into people who are nosy, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and rude. They're like this because they can't tell good from bad. The alarm goes off at 6:30. Before you've even opened your eyes, you know how the day is going to go: the meeting with the director who talks over everyone, the supplier who'll swear the order "already shipped," the parents' group chat fighting about the class holiday party, your mother-in-law coming over for Sunday dinner. You pull the pillow over your face.

Marcus Aurelius knew that feeling. That's why he opened one of his notebooks with an exercise that sounds gloomy and works more like a vaccine.

## The Context: Writing in Enemy Territory

An old note in the manuscripts says these entries were written "among the Quadi, on the river Granua," which is to say in the middle of a military campaign against Germanic tribes, a long way from Rome. Marcus spent much of the last decade of his life at the front. Yet the people who worried him most weren't the barbarians. They were his own: ambitious generals, flattering senators, officials who said one thing and did another.

He didn't write "today everything will go well." He wrote the opposite: today you're going to deal with difficult people. Then he reminded himself of three things. They act this way out of ignorance. They share his nature. And we were all made to work together.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Rehearsing the Bad Stuff

The Stoics practiced *premeditatio malorum*, the premeditation of adversity. Seneca recommended it constantly: picture ahead of time what could go wrong, so that if it happens, it doesn't blindside you. The point isn't to invite trouble or live in fear. A blow you saw coming simply hurts less than one you didn't.

When Marcus tells himself "today I'll meet ungrateful people," he isn't being bitter. He's clearing out false expectations. Most of our anger at other people comes from a quiet demand we never say out loud: "people ought to be reasonable." They aren't always. Knowing that at 6:30 in the morning spares you the outrage at 11.

### Reading 2. The Socratic: Nobody Does Wrong on Purpose

Marcus says difficult people behave the way they do because they "can't tell good from bad." That idea goes back to Socrates: nobody picks what's wrong knowing it's wrong; they pick it because they've convinced themselves it's good for them. The coworker who steals your idea thinks it's protecting his job. The client who haggles you down to an insult thinks he's being shrewd.

Seeing it this way changes the emotion underneath. If the other person is acting out of a mistake, the fitting response looks less like fury and more like the patience you'd have with someone who took a wrong turn. It doesn't let them off the hook. It explains them.

Psychologists today have a name for something similar: the *fundamental attribution error*. We chalk up our own slipups to circumstances ("I was late because of traffic") and other people's to their character ("he was late because he's irresponsible"). Marcus is asking you to give others the same benefit of the doubt you give yourself.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: So I'm Supposed to Put Up with Everything?

The objection writes itself. If everyone is "family" and "can't hurt me," am I supposed to tolerate the abusive boss, the partner who belittles me, the business partner who's skimming off the top?

No. Read Marcus closely. The other person *can't hurt you* in the Stoic sense: they can't make you a worse person. Your character stays yours. That doesn't stop you from protecting yourself, keeping your distance, reporting someone, or walking away from a relationship. Feet and hands cooperate, sure. But if one hand got infected, the body would treat it. It wouldn't pretend the hand was fine.

The meditation is a rule for keeping resentment out of your system. It's no agreement to be a doormat. In practice, the difference looks like this: you can walk away from someone without hating them, and you can demand something without humiliating anybody.

**In a nutshell:** understanding a difficult person doesn't mean you have to stay close to them.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: "That's How It Looked to Him"

Epictetus offers advice almost identical to Marcus's for moments when someone mistreats us or talks behind our back: remember that they're doing what they believe is right. They can't follow what seems right to you, only what seems right to them. If they've got it wrong, they're the ones who pay, because they're the ones deceived. Start from there, he says, and you'll be gentle with whoever insults you. Each time, you'll simply say, "That's how it looked to him."

That little phrase is a tool you can carry in your pocket. It doesn't say the other person is right. It says they acted on what they understood, and that your job isn't to fix their thinking from a place of outrage. Your job is to decide what *you* will do.

### Seneca: Don't Get Mad at Someone Stumbling in the Dark

In his treatise *On Anger*, Seneca gives us a useful picture: a wise person doesn't get angry at people for making mistakes, any more than he'd get angry at someone tripping in the dark, or at a sick person for groaning. Human errors are so many and so common that getting angry at each one would mean living in a permanent state of anger.

Then he adds something refreshingly realistic: stop expecting people to be different from how they are. Walk out the door expecting everyone to be kind, and you'll spend the day offended. Walk out knowing you'll meet every kind of person, and you'll be able to enjoy the good ones and put up with the rest.

### What Science Says Today

In 1977, psychologist Lee Ross put a name to a bias we all fall into: the *fundamental attribution error*. We tend to explain other people's behavior by their character ("he's rude") and our own by our situation ("I was exhausted"). Knowing about the bias won't make it go away, but it helps you catch yourself, which is exactly what Marcus is doing when he reminds himself that people act out of ignorance, not malice.

Psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, meanwhile, has spent years studying what she calls *mental contrasting*: picturing not just the goal you want but the specific obstacles likely to get in the way. Her research suggests that people who imagine the obstacles and plan their response tend to make more progress than people who only picture success. It's a modern take on *premeditatio malorum*. Preparing for trouble isn't pessimism. It's strategy.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Making a list of difficult people every morning sounds like a great way to start the day in a bad mood.

**Author:** It would be, if you did it with a grudge. Do it the way a pilot checks the weather before takeoff, and it's a different thing. The pilot isn't rooting for a storm; he just doesn't want to be surprised by one. Seeing difficult people coming won't ruin your mood. Getting caught flat-footed will.

**Skeptic:** Isn't it unfair to think badly of someone before you've even seen them?

**Author:** You're not thinking badly of anyone in particular. You're accepting something close to a statistic: on any given day, someone is going to be rude, late, or unfair. Could be your boss, could be a stranger, could be you. Marcus doesn't write "my colleague is an idiot." He writes "I'll run into people like this." That's humility about how the world works, not a verdict on any one person.

**Skeptic:** "They act out of ignorance" sounds naive. Some people know exactly what they're doing.

**Author:** That's the strongest objection, and you're partly right. Some people know perfectly well they're hurting you. But the Socratic point is subtler: that person believes hurting you is good *for them*. They're wrong about what's good for them, even if they're right about the effect on you. That doesn't make them innocent. It just helps keep their mistake from becoming your obsession.

**Skeptic:** And what if the difficult person is my partner? Or my mom?

**Author:** Then the exercise matters even more, because you can't just walk away from them. Getting ready for the hard side of someone you love isn't loving them less. It's loving them with your eyes open.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Sunday Dinner

Lauren knows that on Sunday they're having dinner at Andrew's mother's house, and that, like every month, her mother-in-law will comment on the house, on Lauren's job, or on how "it's about time" they had another baby. Lauren usually shows up tense, sits through it in silence, and then unloads on Andrew in the car on the way home.

**The Stoic version of Sunday morning:** Lauren tells herself, "Today my mother-in-law is going to make some comment. She does it because she thinks she's looking out for her son and their family, even if her delivery is terrible. She can't make me rude, and she can't ruin my day."

Then something practical: she talks to Andrew ahead of time. "If your mom brings up the baby thing again, can you change the subject?" Preparing is a mental exercise, sure, but it's also planning.

**The flip side:** it works in both directions. Andrew can brace himself for the evening Lauren comes home wiped out from work and snaps at him. Getting ready for the hard side of someone you love is its own kind of love.

### At Work: The Director Who Talks Over Everyone

Every week, Rick has a meeting with a director who interrupts, jumps from topic to topic, and shoots down ideas before he's heard them out. Rick walks out of every one of these meetings with a headache.

At 7 a.m. on Monday, Rick tries meditation II.1. He writes on an index card: "The director is going to interrupt me today. It's not personal; he does it to everyone. My job is to make my proposal clear, not to change him."

With that expectation set, he changes his approach. He brings his proposal on a single page, leads with the bottom line, and sends a follow-up email afterward summarizing what was agreed. The meeting is the same as ever. Rick isn't.

### In Business: The Client Who Haggles

Megan has a prospect who always wants a discount, compares her quote with his nephew who "also does design," and needs everything yesterday. Before every call, she can feel her blood pressure climbing.

The Stoic version: before the call, Megan writes down three things the client is *likely* to say ("that's too expensive," "my nephew will do it for less," "I need it by Friday") and drafts an answer to each one. She also sets her floor: the lowest price she'd accept, and the point at which she'll politely say no.

That's what a Stoic business looks like. It doesn't expect perfect clients; it builds its process around real ones. And it understands something else: the client who haggles isn't insulting her. He's doing what he thinks serves him. Sometimes the right move is to negotiate. Sometimes it's to let him go, no hard feelings.

### At School: The Group Project and the Parents' Chat

**If you're studying as an adult:** George has a group project with three classmates in his MBA program. One never answers, one insists on doing everything his way, and one turns things in late. George, who works all week, feels like he's dragging the whole team.

Before the first meeting, George tells himself: "Somebody on this team won't pull their weight. That's how group projects go." Then he proposes something concrete: a shared document listing tasks, owners, and deadlines. If someone drops the ball, everyone can see it, and nobody has to pick a fight.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire is in the parents' group chat for Sophie's class. Every week there's a blowup: the money for the holiday party, too much homework, the dad who lectures the teacher in all caps. Claire decides to run through the meditation before she opens the chat: "Someone in here is going to be angry. I don't have to respond to everything." She mutes the chat and checks it once a day. She answers only what actually involves her. She's gotten back two hours a week and a little peace of mind.

### In Your Family: The Holiday Dinner

Every December, Lauren's family gets together at her mother's house. And every December, her Uncle Ernie brings up politics, mocks anyone who disagrees, and ends up in a fight with somebody. Lauren walks into dinner with a knot in her stomach.

This year she starts on meditation II.1 first thing in the morning: "My uncle is going to talk politics and try to get a rise out of people. He thinks that's how you stand up for what you believe. He can't make me argue." She prepares three things: a friendly line to sidestep the topic ("Uncle Ernie, today I'm not debating, I'm eating"), an ally (her cousin, who hates fights just as much), and an exit (helping with the dishes when things heat up).

Her uncle talks politics. Lauren smiles, delivers her line, and heads to the kitchen. For the first time in years, she gets home without a headache.

The next December, though, the plan doesn't hold up as well. Her uncle skips the general politics and goes straight after Lauren's job, in front of everyone. She uses her line; he keeps pushing. Lauren ends up snapping back harder than she meant to and leaves before dessert. In the car, Andrew asks whether the Stoic thing "failed." Lauren thinks about it and says not completely. This time she got angry, but she didn't spend three days replaying the fight, and the next morning she texted her uncle and calmly asked him not to make comments like that again. The practice didn't make her bulletproof. It helped her bounce back faster.

### In Money and Health: The Call to the Bank

Rick needs to call his bank about a charge on his credit card that shouldn't be there. He knows how it'll go: twenty minutes on hold, three transfers, and a rep who says there's nothing they can do. Last time, he ended up yelling and hanging up with nothing fixed.

This time he prepares the way Marcus would. He writes down the details first: card number, date and amount of the charge, the reference number from his earlier claim. He lists what they'll probably tell him ("you'll need to wait 45 days," "we don't see that in our system") and his answer to each. He decides not to yell, because the rep isn't the bank, and yelling at him won't get the money back.

It takes forty minutes and two transfers. But he hangs up with a case number and a resolution date. The call was just as annoying as last time. He wasn't.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Assume the worst about everyone."** No. Bracing for difficult people simply means you won't be shocked when someone turns out to be one. Most people, most days, are reasonable.

**"If people act out of ignorance, they aren't responsible."** No. Marcus explains other people's behavior so that he won't hate them, not so they can skip the consequences. An employee who steals still gets fired, however well you understand him.

**"You have to suffer in silence."** No. Often the Stoic move is to speak up, set a boundary, or ask for something to change. What you're avoiding is the knee-jerk reaction, not action itself.

Here's a scene almost nobody owns up to: the most powerful man on earth, losing a wrestling match with his blankets. The emperor of Rome, sun coming up, bargaining with himself for five more minutes. This meditation is proof that Marcus Aurelius was human. It's also proof that procrastination is not a modern invention.

## The Context: Duty as Identity

Marcus never asked to be emperor. He was adopted as a young man by Antoninus Pius at the request of the emperor Hadrian, and from that point on his life was mapped out for him. By some accounts, he'd have preferred the life of a philosopher. What he got instead was government, war, and the courts.

So this meditation goes deeper than motivation, down to the question of what a person is *for*. To the Stoics, every living thing has a job that fits its nature. The bee's is making honey; the spider's is spinning webs. A human being's job is to act with reason and for the good of others. Getting up isn't a chore imposed from outside. It's being what you are.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: You're Built to Act

Marcus compares us to the plants and animals that do their jobs without complaint. The idea underneath is both Aristotelian and Stoic: what's good for anything is doing its job well. A knife is good if it cuts. A human being is good if they think clearly, choose well, and contribute to the people around them.

From that angle, the morning question isn't "Do I feel like it?" It's "What's mine to do today, as a person?" Feelings come and go. The job stays.

**In a nutshell:** you don't get up because you feel like it; you get up because it's your job.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Identity Beats Motivation

We know by now that motivation is a lousy strategy for consistency. Waiting until you "feel like" going to the gym, studying, or making that hard phone call means waiting for something that almost never shows up on time. What works better is identity and habit: "I'm someone who works out." "I'm someone who follows through."

Marcus doesn't give himself a pep talk. He doesn't say, "Come on, you've got this." He says, "This is who I am." Same strategy. He doesn't argue with being tired; he puts it in its place. The tiredness is real. It just doesn't get a vote.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Burnout?

Skim this meditation too fast and it sounds like hustle culture: up at five, never rest, sleep is for the weak. That reading betrays it.

A few lines after the passage above, Marcus admits that nature sets limits on eating and drinking, and that we need rest. What he's going after is lounging in comfort while cutting corners on effort, not sleep itself. His real question is: do you rest so you can do your work, or do you live for the rest?

There's a difference between laziness and exhaustion. Laziness is cured by starting. Exhaustion is cured by stopping. If you haven't been able to get out of bed for months, with no energy for anything, that may not be a discipline problem at all. It may be depression or burnout, and the Stoic response, the one reason would pick, is to get help. Marcus himself, in Book I, praises the way his adoptive father took care of his body in moderation, and gives thanks for the remedies he received when he was sick.

**In a nutshell:** get up and do your job, and remember that rest is part of the job.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Take Your Time Back

The very first letter Seneca writes to his friend Lucilius opens with an order: claim yourself back. Reclaim the time that's been taken from you, stolen from you, or simply allowed to slip away. Some of our time gets taken, Seneca says, some gets stolen, and some just leaks out, and the most embarrassing loss of all is the one that happens through carelessness.

For Seneca, getting up to do what you should is about more than meeting other people's expectations: it's how you take your own life back. Every morning you put off what matters, someone or something else decides where your day goes.

### Epictetus: Nothing Great Happens Overnight

When an impatient student wanted to transform his character all at once, Epictetus answered with an image from the farm. Nothing great comes into being suddenly, he said, not even a bunch of grapes or a fig. Tell him you want a fig right now, and he'll tell you to wait: let it blossom first, then bear fruit, then ripen.

The discipline of getting up works the same way. Nobody becomes consistent in a week. The person who becomes consistent is the one who gets up today, and tomorrow, and the day after that, even if they miss a few along the way.

### What Science Says Today

A widely cited 2009 study led by researcher Phillippa Lally followed people trying to build a new habit. On average, it took about 66 days for the behavior to become automatic, though the range was enormous: anywhere from 18 days to more than 250. Missing a day here and there didn't derail the process. The takeaway is pure Stoicism: consistency matters more than perfection.

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer has studied what he calls *implementation intentions*, plans in the form "when X happens, I'll do Y" ("when the alarm goes off, I'll put my feet on the floor before I reach for my phone"). Across many studies, a concrete plan like that makes people more likely to follow through than good intentions alone.

One caution. Since 2019, the World Health Organization has listed burnout in its international classification as an occupational phenomenon, not a failure of willpower. If you've been running on empty for months, more discipline isn't what you need. You need to take a hard look at your circumstances, and maybe ask for help.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Get up and do the work of a human being" is easy to say when you own slaves and don't have rent due.

**Author:** That's a fair hit, and we shouldn't forget it. Marcus lived in a slave-owning society, with privileges you and I will never have. But notice that Epictetus, who *was* a slave, says almost exactly the same thing. The idea doesn't depend on social status. It depends on something we all share: the pull to stay comfortable when we know something is ours to do.

**Skeptic:** But laziness isn't today's problem. A lot of people work way too much.

**Author:** No argument there. That's why the chapter separates laziness from burnout. If you're working twelve-hour days, V.1 isn't telling you to get up and work more. It's asking why you work, and whether what you're doing is actually your job or just somebody else's demand. Sometimes the Stoic answer is to quit, renegotiate your hours, or rest.

**Skeptic:** And if someone finds no meaning in their job? Should they just grit their teeth?

**Author:** They should look for meaning, and if they can't find it, change whatever they can. In the meantime, though, a person's job in the human sense is bigger than their job title. You can be a good coworker, a good parent, the one honest person in a place where nobody else is. That counts as human work, and it doesn't require your job to be fascinating.

**Skeptic:** It still sounds like boot camp to me.

**Author:** Marcus literally commanded armies, so there's a bit of that. But the discipline he's talking about doesn't come from a drill sergeant. It comes from you. The message isn't "obey." It's "remember who you want to be." That difference changes everything.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Invisible Work

In Lauren and Andrew's house there's a to-do list nobody ever wrote down: pay the gas bill, book the pediatrician, remember the nephews' birthdays, buy toilet paper. For years that list lived in Lauren's head. She woke up tired every morning before the day had even started.

Meditation V.1 helps here in two ways. For Andrew: "the work of a human being," in a marriage, includes his half of running the household, not "helping out." One morning he gets up and says, "From now on, doctor's appointments and the gas bill are mine." It's not a favor. It's his job.

For Lauren: doing her part doesn't mean doing all of it. Her job also includes asking for what belongs to the other person instead of carrying it silently and resenting it.

### At Work: The Monday That Feels Pointless

For weeks, Rick has felt that his work doesn't matter. He fills out reports nobody reads. Every Monday it's harder to get going.

He can't switch jobs tomorrow. But he can ask Marcus's question: what's my real job here, beyond the title? He comes up with three answers. Training the two new analysts who depend on him. Providing for his family. Doing things right even when nobody's watching, because that says something about who he is.

The report is still boring. But now he's getting up for something else. And on the side, he starts looking seriously for a role where his work is more visible. He can do both.

### In Business: The Entrepreneur with No Boss

Megan doesn't have a boss making her show up. What felt like freedom turned into a trap: some days she starts working at eleven, after a long scroll through social media, and wraps up at midnight, feeling guilty.

She applies the meditation with one simple rule. At 8:30, she sits down at her computer whether she feels like it or not, and spends an hour on the most important task of the day before she opens her email. She doesn't wait for inspiration. She does her job. The rest of the day falls into place.

Not every week. Sometimes an urgent client blows up her routine on Monday and Megan slides right back into old habits: working in scattered bursts until midnight and beating herself up about it. The difference is that now she knows the way back. By Thursday she's sitting down at 8:30 again. Marcus never wrote that he must never stay in bed. He wrote down what to do every time he did.

**For entrepreneurs:** when you're your own boss, your identity has to do the job the time clock used to do.

### At School: Studying on Empty

**If you're studying as an adult:** at 6:30 on Saturday morning, George has to drive an hour to his MBA class. He's worked all week. Every Saturday he asks himself why he signed up for this.

The meditation gives him an answer that doesn't depend on feeling like it: learning is part of the work of a human being, and he decided this stretch of his life would be about growing. On the first page of his notebook he writes down why he started the program: "so my kids can watch me finish something hard." When the alarm goes off, he doesn't argue with the blanket. He reads that sentence.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie doesn't want to get up for school. Claire could yell, beg, or bribe. She tries something else. At bedtime, she asks her daughter what she has to do tomorrow that only she can do (help her friend with the presentation, take care of the class plant). Without ever naming it, Claire is teaching her that getting up has a purpose.

### In Your Family: Caring for an Aging Parent

On Saturdays, Rick takes his mother to the farmers' market and then out to lunch. Some Saturdays he doesn't feel like it. He's tired; he wants to sleep in; he wants to watch the game. One morning, thumb on the snooze button, he remembers Marcus's question: were you born to be comfortable, or to act?

He doesn't treat it as a punishment. At this point in his life, his job as a son includes this. And when he does it from that place instead of from guilt, he notices something: those mornings with his mom are some of the only hours in his week when nobody is asking him for a report. What felt like a burden slowly turns into a refuge.

### In Money and Health: The Gym Membership

In January, George paid for a year's gym membership up front. In February he went three times. In March, zero. Every month that goes by, he feels like he's flushing money away, and the guilt makes him go even less.

He applies the blanket sentence to his health and writes down why he wants to exercise. Not "to lose weight," but "to play soccer with my kids without getting winded." Then he changes the plan. Instead of an hour five days a week, he goes for twenty minutes three days a week, always at the same time, right after school drop-off. He packs his gym bag the night before.

He didn't turn into an athlete. But by June he'd gone twelve weeks without missing a session, and to George that mattered more than any number on the scale.

## Author's Note

For a stretch of my life, I worked and studied at the same time. Some nights I came home so drained I couldn't tell whether I was feeling lazy or genuinely exhausted. Over time I learned to ask myself this chapter's question: is this cured by starting, or by stopping? Some nights the answer was to open my notebook for twenty minutes even though I didn't feel like it. Other nights it was to shut everything down, eat dinner, and go to sleep. Both were part of my job. What never helped was getting stuck halfway: not studying, not resting, just sitting in front of a screen feeling guilty.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Getting up early makes you a better person."** No. Marcus is talking about getting up to do your job, not about any particular hour. Some people's work starts at night. What matters is not running from what's yours to do.

**"Rest is laziness."** No. Rest is part of being human. Laziness is using rest as a way to dodge what you should be doing.

**"If you don't feel like it, you're weak."** No. To a Stoic, whether you feel like it is beside the point. Your feelings aren't within your power. Your decision to act is.

- Feelings don't get the deciding vote; your job does.
- Laziness is cured by starting; exhaustion is cured by stopping.

# Chapter 4. What Stands in the Way Becomes the Way

> *Other people can block some of my actions. They can't block my intentions or my attitude, because those can adapt and change course. The mind takes whatever obstructs its action and turns it into a means of acting. What blocks the action moves the action forward. What stands in the way becomes the way.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, V.20 (paraphrase)

> *A strong fire takes whatever you throw on it, burns it up, and grows bigger.*
> — *Meditations*, IV.1 (paraphrase)

Some days go sideways from the first minute. The email that opens with "We regret to inform you." The test result you weren't braced for. Your partner in the kitchen, voice low: "We need to talk." The bank turning down the loan.

On days like that, the mind does what the body does when it trips: it freezes. "This wrecks everything." "There's no way forward." Marcus Aurelius suggests something that sounds impossible: that the obstacle isn't the end of the road. It *is* the road.

## The Context: A Plan That Never Held

Marcus's reign was one interrupted plan after another. He wanted to govern in peace and got war. He wanted reform and got plague. He wanted rest and got a rebellion. His life was a chain of obstacles.

In the passage this meditation comes from, Marcus is thinking about the people who get in his way. He admits that someone can stop what he's trying to do. Then he draws a line: they can stop his action, but not his intention. The Stoics had a tool for this, which they called the *reserve clause*: always act with an unspoken condition attached, "I'll do this, if nothing prevents it." If something does, the intention hasn't failed. It changes shape. If I can't help someone one way, I help them another. If I can't move forward, I learn to wait. If I can't win, I learn to lose with dignity.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Obstacles as Raw Material for Character

For the Stoics, the only truly good thing is virtue: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control. And virtue needs something to work on. You can't be brave without danger, patient without waiting, or fair without the temptation to cheat.

Seen this way, an obstacle doesn't block your most important goal, which is being a good person. If anything, it hands you the chance to practice it. Losing your job is a chance for courage. Illness is a chance for self-control and calm. A disloyal partner is a chance for justice.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Redraw the Map

There's a more down-to-earth reading, too. When one road closes, others open, but only for people who stop staring at the closed door. The reserve clause works like good planning: keep the destination fixed and the route flexible.

If Megan's goal is "a business that can sustain itself," losing a client is a problem with the route, not the destination. If Rick's goal is "supporting my family with work I can be proud of," losing his job is a detour, not the end of the trip.

The question V.20 hands you is: **what does this let me do that I couldn't do before?** Sometimes the answer is learn something. Sometimes it's find out who's really in your corner. Sometimes it's just: have some time.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Not Every Obstacle Is a Gift

There's a syrupy version of this idea floating around: "everything happens for a reason," "every crisis is an opportunity," "be grateful for your cancer." Marcus never says that. He doesn't call the obstacle good or tell you to be glad about it. He says the mind *can turn it* into material for action.

Some losses don't turn into opportunities: losing a child, a brutal injustice, an illness with no cure. In the face of something like that, making the obstacle the way doesn't mean finding the silver lining. It means something humbler and more dignified: staying the person you want to be, in spite of it. Marcus lost several of his children. He never wrote that it was a blessing. He wrote that it had to be borne without giving up on being just and good.

**In a nutshell:** not every obstacle is an opportunity, but every obstacle is a chance to decide who you are.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Fire Tests Gold

In his essay *On Providence*, Seneca takes on a question we've all asked: why do bad things happen to good people? His answer is tough and consoling at the same time. Nobody seems more unfortunate to him, he says, than a person who's never faced hardship, because that person has never had the chance to find out what they can handle. He sums it up in one image: fire tests gold; misfortune tests the brave.

Seneca doesn't claim misfortune is pleasant or that we should go looking for it. His point is that when it arrives, it gives us something comfort never can: knowing what we're made of.

### Epictetus: The Sparring Partner

Epictetus reached for a sports image. Hard times, he said, are what show people for who they are. When one comes your way, remember that God, like a coach, has paired you with a young, tough sparring partner. Why? So you can become a champion, and nobody becomes a champion without sweating.

The metaphor changes the question. It's no longer "Why me?" It's "What is this training me for?" A wrestler who complains that his opponent is strong has forgotten why he came to the gym.

### What Science Says Today

In the 1990s, psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun coined the term *post-traumatic growth* to describe something they saw in many people after very hard experiences: a deeper appreciation for life, closer relationships, new priorities, a sense of personal strength. Read this carefully, though. Not everyone grows after trauma, growth doesn't erase pain, and nobody should feel pressured to "make the most of" their suffering.

What the research does suggest is that the meaning we give to what happens to us shapes how we get through it. That's a modern version of Marcus's instinct: the obstacle doesn't get the final word on its own. The mind facing it has a say.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "The obstacle is the way" sounds like something on a coffee mug.

**Author:** It's definitely become a slogan. But the original is less cheesy than it sounds. Marcus doesn't say the obstacle is good, or that everything works out. He says the mind can use the obstacle as material for action. That's a claim about what *you* do, not about what the universe hands you.

**Skeptic:** Some obstacles don't turn into anything. A terminal diagnosis isn't a "way."

**Author:** Not a way to get well, no. But even there, a path remains: how to live the time that's left, how to say goodbye, how to treat the people standing beside you. Many people who've been through it say it was when they saw most clearly what mattered. Nobody would wish for that clarity at that price. But when the obstacle can't be removed, the only freedom left is the freedom of your response, and that freedom is no small thing.

**Skeptic:** Isn't it dangerous to tell somebody their tragedy is an "opportunity"?

**Author:** Extremely, especially when you're saying it to someone else. This chapter is for you to apply to yourself, not to prescribe to a grieving friend. When someone is suffering, you sit with them; you don't lecture them. Maybe, much later, they'll discover on their own what that experience made possible. That discovery has to be theirs.

**Skeptic:** And the "reserve clause"? If I'm always saying "if nothing prevents it," aren't I just giving myself an out?

**Author:** It's the other way around. The reserve clause lets you commit completely to your part, precisely because you're not frozen by fear that something outside your control will wreck it. The person who says "I'll do it, if nothing prevents it" gives it everything. The person who says "I'll do it no matter what" often falls apart the moment something unexpected shows up.

### In Your Relationship: The Crisis

One night, Andrew tells Lauren he isn't happy. There's no one else. There's exhaustion, routine, distance. Lauren feels the floor drop out from under her.

Her first instinct is to decide the marriage is over, or that it was all a lie. The meditation offers a different question: what does this conversation make possible that wasn't possible before?

It makes possible something they haven't done in years: talk honestly. Lauren and Andrew decide to start couples therapy. They don't know if they'll stay together. But the obstacle became the road to an honest conversation, and that's worth something however it ends.

**Another angle:** if the relationship does end, the road looks different. It runs through learning to be alone, noticing what keeps repeating in your relationships, rebuilding friendships you let slide. The obstacle doesn't pick your destination. It picks your next task.

### At Work: The Layoff

Three months after the reorg, Rick is laid off. They offer him a severance package and a letter of recommendation. At forty-four, he feels it's too late to start over.

### In Business: The Client Who Walked

The client who wasn't paying (Chapter 1) finally pays half and takes his business to a competitor. Overnight, Megan loses 40 percent of her revenue.

It's serious. But a fire, Marcus says, grows on whatever you throw at it. Megan uses the crisis to make decisions she's been putting off for years: she raises her rates for new clients, requires a 50 percent deposit, builds a monthly retainer package, and starts posting case studies online. A year later she has eight smaller clients instead of one big one. It's less comfortable. It's also safer.

**For entrepreneurs:** plenty of companies are born or reborn in a crisis. Not because crises are good, but because they force you to do what comfort let you put off.

### At School: Failing a Class and Not Making the Team

**If you're studying as an adult:** George fails his statistics final and has to retake the course. He'll graduate a semester late.

He could drop out. Instead he asks what this lets him do. He realizes he never really understood the fundamentals, and that if he'd just scraped by, it would have come back to bite him in his capstone. Retaking the class means he can actually learn it. He finds a tutor, works problems every night, and on the second try ends up being the one explaining it to everyone else.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie doesn't make the school basketball team. She cries all afternoon. Claire resists the urge to call the coach, or to tell her it's no big deal. Instead she says: "It *is* a big deal, and it's okay that it hurts. What do you want to do now?" Sophie decides to practice on weekends and try out again next year. At ten, she learns that a "no" can be where the work starts, not where the dream ends.

### In Your Family: The Forced Move

George's parents get hit with a rent increase on the house they've lived in for thirty years and have to move into a small apartment. His father takes it as a defeat; his mother cries while she packs.

George can't stop the move. But he helps his parents ask this chapter's question: what does this make possible? They find several things. The apartment is ten minutes from George's place, so they'll see their grandkids more. It has an elevator, which matters for his father's knees. And it forces them to do something they've been avoiding for years: go through three decades of stuff, give away what they don't use, and keep what they truly love.

The move was still sad. But a year later, George's father admits he sees his grandkids three times as often as he used to.

### In Money and Health: The Injury

Andrew blows out his knee in a pickup basketball game, and his doctor bans running for six months. Andrew, who ran to clear his head after work, sinks into a funk. He feels he's lost the one thing keeping him balanced.

He applies the reserve clause. His goal was never running; it was taking care of his body and his head. Running was just the route. With his physical therapist's help, he finds others: swimming, the stationary bike, long evening walks with Lauren. The walks start as plan B and end up being the part of the day when they talk the most.

When he's finally cleared to run again, Andrew runs less. Not because he can't, but because he doesn't want to give up the walks.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Everything happens for a reason."** Stoicism doesn't promise a hidden purpose behind every misfortune. It promises that you can give your *response* a purpose.

**"You should be grateful for your problems."** No need. You can hate what happened and still respond well.

**"If you don't turn the crisis into a success, you failed."** No. Sometimes the road an obstacle opens is simply getting through it with your dignity intact. That counts.

# Chapter 5. Your Mind Takes On the Color of Your Thoughts

> *Your mind will become like the thoughts you think most often, because the soul is dyed by its thoughts. So dye it with thoughts like this one: wherever it's possible to live, it's possible to live well.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, V.16 (paraphrase)

Try something. Pick up your phone and check how much time you spent on social media yesterday. Now think about what you actually saw: crime stories, political fights, other people's flawless-looking lives, fifteen-second videos stacked end to end. Then ask yourself how you felt when you finally closed the app.

Marcus Aurelius never saw a smartphone, but he understood the mechanism perfectly. The mind isn't a neutral container. It's fabric, and it soaks up the color of whatever you dip it into every day.

## The Context: The Roman Dyer

Marcus didn't pick that image at random. In Rome, cloth dyeing was a trade you saw everywhere, and the famous imperial purple came from a sea snail and cost a fortune. Dip a piece of cloth once and it picks up a faint tint. Dip it a hundred times and it forgets what color it started out.

Thoughts work the same way, Marcus says. A passing idea doesn't change anyone. What you think every day, what you repeat, what you watch and listen to, eventually becomes your character. You aren't what you think once. You're what you think all the time.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Character Is Built

The Stoics believed virtue is a habit, not a gift. Aristotle had already made the point: we become fair by doing fair things and brave by doing brave things. Marcus applies it to the mind. We become calm by thinking calmly, and bitter by thinking bitterly.

That's why Stoicism is a daily practice rather than a theory. Its exercises (the journal, the evening review, the morning prep) are deliberate trips to the dye vat. You choose the color instead of letting your surroundings choose it for you.

**In a nutshell:** you don't get a character all at once; you dye it, one day at a time.

### Reading 2. The Modern: You Are What You Consume

We know now that attention is a limited resource, and that entire industries exist to capture it. News that stokes fear and outrage gets shared more; content built to keep you watching makes money every minute you stay. It isn't a conspiracy. It's a business model.

Read this way, V.16 is a warning about your mental diet. Start every morning with fifteen minutes of alarming headlines, end every night with half an hour of other people's highlight reels, and your mind takes on the colors of alarm and comparison. Not because you're weak, but because that's how fabric works.

The practical question: **who's picking the colors in my head?**

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: "Wherever It's Possible to Live, It's Possible to Live Well"

The second half of the meditation is the most provocative. Marcus adds that wherever it's possible to live, it's possible to live well, and he uses a palace as his example: if you can live in a palace, you can live well in one. Surrounded by luxury, intrigue, and flatterers, he kept reminding himself that the problem wasn't the palace. It was how he lived in it.

That runs straight into a modern belief: "Once I change jobs, cities, or partners, I'll be happy." Sometimes moving on is exactly right. But if your mind is still dyed the same color, it moves with you. Plenty of people change jobs and find the same boss waiting for them, or change partners and have the same fight.

The serious objection is that some places really are unlivable: a toxic workplace, a violent relationship. True, and Marcus says "wherever it's possible to live." If a place is destroying you, leaving is the sensible call. But before you go, it's worth asking how much of the problem is coming along for the ride.

**In a nutshell:** before you change your surroundings, check the color of the lens you're seeing them through.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: Count Your Days Without Anger

Epictetus explained habits with very simple logic: every habit gets stronger through the actions that match it. Walking makes you a better walker; running, a better runner. The mind is no different. Every time you lose your temper, you're not just feeling anger; you're strengthening the habit of anger. It's like throwing wood on a fire.

His advice is practical and a little funny. If you don't want to be a hothead, stop feeding the habit. Count the days you haven't gotten angry. "I used to lose it every day. Then every other day. Then every third day, every fourth." If you make it to thirty days, he says, make an offering to God, because the habit is starting to lose its grip.

### Seneca: Watch Out for the Crowd

In one of his letters, Seneca admits to Lucilius that he never comes home from a crowd with the same character he left with. He returns, he says, greedier, more ambitious, more pleasure-seeking, even crueler, just from being around people. He isn't being a snob. He's being realistic: our surroundings dye us. So he recommends choosing carefully who we spend our time with, and seeking out people who can make us better, and people we can help become better.

If an afternoon at the arena changed Seneca, imagine what an afternoon on social media does to us.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologists talk about a *negativity bias*: we tend to notice, and remember, bad information more than good. That was probably useful for survival. In a world full of news and content engineered to grab our attention, though, it can tilt us toward a darker view than reality warrants.

Researcher Susan Nolen-Hoeksema spent years studying *rumination*, going over the same negative thoughts again and again. Her work linked rumination to a higher risk of depression and anxiety. The connection between social media and well-being, on the other hand, is still hotly debated: results vary from study to study, and *how* people use it seems to matter at least as much as *how much*.

The cautious takeaway is Marcus's: pay attention to what each thing you consume leaves behind, because nobody else is going to look after your mind for you.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Watch your thoughts" sounds like positive thinking, and positive thinking doesn't work.

**Author:** They're different things. Positive thinking asks you to believe everything will turn out fine. Marcus asks for something more modest: notice what you're soaking your mind in every day. You can be realistic, even pessimistic about certain facts, and still not spend six hours a day on stuff that leaves you scared or envious.

**Skeptic:** And if the world really is falling apart? Tuning out the news would be irresponsible.

**Author:** Staying informed is part of being a citizen, and Marcus ran an empire; he didn't tune out the world. The difference is between getting informed and getting soaked. You can catch up on the news that matters in twenty minutes and act on it: vote, help out, organize. What helps nobody is spending the evening watching disaster clips. That doesn't make you more responsible. It makes you more anxious.

**Skeptic:** "Wherever it's possible to live, it's possible to live well" sounds like an excuse to stay somewhere bad.

**Author:** That's exactly why Marcus says "wherever it's possible to live." Some places, it isn't: a job that's making you sick, a violent relationship. You need to get out of those. But a lot of the time we blame the place for something we're carrying, and we move with the problem packed in our suitcase. The meditation asks you to figure out which one it is before you decide.

**Skeptic:** How am I supposed to tell?

**Author:** Here's a clue. If the same problem has followed you through several places (several jobs, several partners, several cities), part of it is probably traveling with you. If it showed up only here and nowhere else, it's probably the place.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Grievance Ledger

Lauren realizes she's been keeping a running tally of everything Andrew does wrong: the plate left in the sink, the towel on the bed, the anniversary he forgot three years ago. The list grows on its own. When they fight, she reads him the whole thing.

What she never writes down is what Andrew does right, because that doesn't hurt, and the mind doesn't file it. After years of this, their marriage has taken on a color: the color of complaint.

Three weeks in, Lauren notices she's watching for things during the day so she'll have something to say that night. Her mind has started taking on a new color. The plates still end up in the sink sometimes. They're just not the only thing she sees anymore.

### At Work: The Complaint Club

In Rick's department, lunch is a daily complaint session: the director, the pay, the client, the system that never works. At first Rick joined in to fit in. Over time, he noticed he was coming back from lunch angrier than when he left.

He doesn't need to cut himself off or pretend everything's fine. He decides on two things: stop eating with the same group every day, and, when the complaining starts, ask, "Okay, so what can we actually do about it?" Sometimes the conversation shifts. Sometimes it doesn't. Either way, he's no longer soaking in that dye every single day.

**For leaders:** the mood of a team is everybody's dye. Whatever the boss keeps repeating in meetings (fear, urgency, blame, or clarity and trust) eventually becomes the team's character.

### In Business: The Comparison Trap

Megan follows other design studios online. She sees their offices, their big-name clients, their awards. Every time she closes the app, she feels like her studio is tiny and she's falling behind.

She realizes she's been dyeing her mind with comparison. She makes three changes. She unfollows the accounts that only make her jealous. She follows people who teach something useful about the craft. And every Friday, she writes down three wins her own business had that week. Her studio didn't grow because of it. But she stopped feeling like a failure while it grew.

**For entrepreneurs:** competitors are useful as information, not as a mirror. Study what they do; don't measure your worth by what they post.

### At School: The Voice in Your Head

**If you're studying as an adult:** every time George gets something wrong in class, a voice in his head says, "You're an idiot. You're too old for this." That voice has been around so long he thinks it's telling the truth.

George would never tell a classmate who made a mistake that he's an idiot. So he starts changing the line: "I got it wrong. What didn't I understand?" The fabric changes slowly, but it changes.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire notices Sophie repeating lines she hears around the house: "Ugh, this is so boring." "I can't." "I'm going to fail." Then she realizes a lot of them are *hers*. Meditation V.16 works across generations: kids take on the color of the thoughts they hear from their parents. Claire starts with herself.

### In Your Family: The Family Group Chat

Andrew's family group chat is a daily flood of chain messages, alarming headlines, miracle cures, and political arguments. Andrew checks it before bed and goes to sleep angry or scared.

He doesn't want to leave the chat, because it's how his dad stays in touch with everyone. So he does a dye audit. He mutes the chat, checks it once a day at lunchtime, and never before bed. When someone shares something fake, he doesn't argue in the group; he messages that person privately, and kindly.

### In Money and Health: The Money Script

Megan grew up hearing her mother say "money is dirty" and "rich people are bad." As an adult, she struggles to charge what her work is worth, and feels guilty every time she raises her rates.

She realizes her mind has been dyed with sentences she never chose. So she starts changing the dye. She reads up on personal finance, talks about money with a friend who manages hers well, and writes new sentences in her notebook: "Charging a fair price lets me do my work well." It doesn't happen overnight. But the next year, she raises her prices for the first time without feeling like a thief.

**"Think positive and things will change."** No. Thinking differently doesn't change reality. It changes your character in the face of reality, and that changes what you do.

**"Never watch the news."** No. Staying informed is part of being a citizen. But checking reliable sources once a day is one thing; marinating in alarm all day is another.

# Chapter 6. Kindness Is Invincible

> *Kindness is invincible, as long as it's real and not a fake smile or a mask. What can the most insolent person do to you if you stay kind to him, if, when the moment comes, you gently set him straight and, right as he's trying to hurt you, calmly tell him: "No, son. We weren't made for this"?*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, XI.18 (paraphrase)

> *The best revenge is not to be like the person who wronged you.*
> — *Meditations*, VI.6 (paraphrase)

The driver who cuts you off on the freeway. Your boss's 11 p.m. message with "URGENT" in all caps. Your partner, mid-argument, dragging out the one thing they swore they'd never bring up again. The customer who leaves you a one-star review full of lies.

In every one of those moments there's an instant, maybe half a second long, when anger rises like a wave. And almost everything that happens next gets decided in that half second.

## The Context: An Emperor with a Temper

Marcus wasn't naturally even-keeled. In Book I, he thanks his grandfather for teaching him good character and how to keep his temper in check, which suggests he needed the lesson. Ancient sources describe him as serious, sometimes severe. He wrote about anger so often that his notes read like those of someone fighting it every single day.

In meditation XI.18, he lists nine reminders for when someone offends him. We were all born for one another. The other person is acting on their own beliefs. If they're right, there's nothing to complain about, and if they're wrong, it's out of ignorance. He's made the same kinds of mistakes himself. He can't always be sure the other person actually did wrong. Life is short. What upsets him isn't what the other person did but his judgment about it. The fallout from anger is usually worse than whatever caused it. And finally: kindness is invincible. (Some readers count a tenth reminder, which Marcus presents as a gift from Apollo: expecting bad people not to do bad things is madness.)

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Anger as Temporary Insanity

Seneca wrote a whole treatise, *On Anger*, in which he calls it a brief madness. To the Stoics, anger is the worst state to be in, because it disguises itself as justice: the angry person is always sure they're right. And while they're angry, they do things they regret. They say things they can't unsay. They make decisions they'd never make with a clear head.

Marcus puts it almost like a math problem: the consequences of anger are usually far worse than its cause. The driver cut you off and cost you two seconds; chase him down and it could cost you your life. Your partner said something hurtful; answer with something worse and it could cost you years.

### Reading 2. The Strategic: Kindness as Strength

"Kindness is invincible" can sound naive. But Marcus isn't talking about being a pushover. He's talking about kindness that corrects: "No, son. We weren't made for this." It's firm, and it's calm.

In an argument, the other person's anger needs yours to keep growing. It's a fire looking for fuel. Answer calmly, and their anger runs out of fuel. You haven't caved; you've just refused to play their game. Negotiators know a version of this: whoever loses their temper loses the negotiation.

VI.6 pushes the idea further: the best revenge is not to be like the person who wronged you. Answer rudeness with rudeness, and the other person wins twice. They insulted you, and they turned you into someone just like them.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Righteous Anger?

This debate is ancient. Aristotle believed in a just anger: being angry at the right person, at the right time, for the right reason, and to the right degree. Without anger, he argued, nobody would stand up for the weak. Plenty of social movements were born out of anger at injustice.

The Stoics answer that you can fight injustice without anger, and that you fight it better that way. A judge who hands down sentences in a rage is dangerous; one who sentences with justice is someone you can trust. Indignation may be the spark, but just action needs a cool head.

The honest position probably sits somewhere in between. That first flash of anger at injustice is human, even healthy, because it tells you something's wrong. What doesn't help is letting it take the wheel.

**In a nutshell:** let anger sound the alarm; let reason decide what to do.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Best Cure Is to Wait

Seneca devoted three full books to anger, and he keeps coming back to one idea: the greatest remedy for anger is delay. The first wave hits hardest. Let it pass, and anger loses its force on its own.

He tells a story about Plato. One day, furious with a slave, Plato raised his hand to strike him. Then he realized how angry he was and froze, hand in midair. A friend walked in and asked what on earth he was doing. "Punishing an angry man," Plato said. He was punishing himself for almost acting in anger. Seneca adds that Plato then decided not to discipline the slave himself, because he didn't trust his own judgment while he was angry.

### Epictetus: Buy Yourself Time

Epictetus gives similar advice in the *Handbook*. When someone offends you, remember that it isn't the person insulting or hitting you who does you harm. It's your belief that you've been harmed. Then he adds a very concrete instruction: don't let the first impression carry you off. Buy yourself some time, take a breath, and you'll find it much easier to get a grip.

"Buy yourself time" may be the most useful anti-anger advice ever written. Counting to ten, going for a walk, waiting until morning to answer that email: they're all ways of buying time.

### What Science Says Today

For a long time, people believed that "venting" by hitting something or yelling helped get anger out of your system. In 2002, psychologist Brad Bushman published an experiment that's been cited ever since: people who punched a bag while thinking about someone who'd made them angry came out, on average, *more* aggressive than people who did nothing at all. Venting that way seemed to feed anger rather than drain it, just as Epictetus would have predicted with his idea of habits.

What does seem to help, according to several lines of research, is getting some distance: calming your body, waiting, and then reinterpreting what happened. In other words, exactly what Seneca and Epictetus recommended two thousand years ago.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Being nice to someone who treats you badly is for suckers.

**Author:** Being kind and letting yourself get walked on are two different things. Look at Marcus's line: "No, son. We weren't made for this." That's a correction. It's firm. Stoic kindness isn't smiling while someone humiliates you. It's telling the truth without humiliating anyone back. And in practice, that's a lot harder than yelling.

**Skeptic:** Sometimes getting angry is the only way people take you seriously.

**Author:** Sometimes, in the short run, sure. But think about the people you respect most at work or in your family. Are they the ones who yell, or the ones who make the whole room go quiet when they speak calmly? Anger buys you power for about five minutes. Calm firmness buys you authority for years.

**Skeptic:** What about Aristotle? He said righteous anger exists.

**Author:** It's a real disagreement between the schools, and there's no reason to hide it. Aristotle thought anger, in the right amount, is a virtue. The Stoics thought you can fight injustice without anger, with reason. My own sense is that everyday experience gives each of them a point. That first flash of indignation tells you something is wrong. But the decisions you make in the middle of a rage are rarely your best ones.

**Skeptic:** If I bottle up my anger, it's just going to blow up later.

**Author:** If you bottle it up, yes. That's why the Stoics don't talk about suppressing it. They talk about waiting and examining the judgment behind it. You're not stuffing anger into a pressure cooker; you're turning down the flame while you think. You can feel the difference physically: suppression leaves you tense, and waiting leaves you calmer.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Argument That Spirals

Lauren and Andrew are fighting about money. She says he spends without checking with her. He says she does too. She brings up the trip from two years ago. He brings up her mother. Five minutes in, they've stopped talking about money. They're arguing about who's the worse person.

In therapy, they learn to use meditation XI.18 as an emergency brake. They agree on a word ("pause") that either of them can say when the argument starts getting away from them. When someone says it, they separate for twenty minutes. Nobody's running away; they're letting the "brief madness" pass. Then they come back with one rule: talk about the problem, not about the person.

The first time they try it, it doesn't go well. Lauren says "pause," and Andrew hears a door slamming in his face: "You always leave when it suits you." The pause turns into a whole new fight. It takes them a few weeks to figure out that the word only works if both of them promise to come back to the topic, and that whoever calls the pause is responsible for going and finding the other person afterward. Stoic tools don't work like a light switch. You learn them by using them, and by getting it wrong.

Invincible kindness in a marriage doesn't mean staying quiet. It means telling the truth without trying to wound. "It worries me that we spend without talking about it" is firm. "You're just like your father" is gasoline.

### At Work: The Flaming Email

The director emails Rick, with the whole team copied, saying his report "is completely useless." Rick feels the blood rush to his face. He starts typing a reply-all.

Then he remembers Seneca's rule: don't act while you're hot. He saves the draft and goes for a walk. An hour later he writes a different email, this time only to the director: "I'd like to understand what the report was missing so I can fix it. Do you have fifteen minutes tomorrow?" In the meeting, the director tones it down. It turns out corporate had been leaning on him.

Rick never conceded that the director was right. But he didn't give him the satisfaction of watching him lose it in front of everyone, either. That's the revenge VI.6 has in mind.

### In Business: The Unfair Review

A customer leaves a one-star review on Megan's business page. He says the work was bad and she was rude. None of it is true: he asked for changes that weren't in the contract and got angry when she quoted him for the extra work.

The temptation is to fire back and expose him. But Megan knows prospective clients won't just read the review; they'll read her response. She writes: "I'm sorry you weren't satisfied. We delivered the work we agreed on and offered to quote the additional changes. We're happy to talk it through." Firm, short, kind.

Three weeks later, a new client tells her, "I hired you because of the way you answered that review." Invincible kindness turns out to be good for business, too.

### At School: The Unfair Professor and the Kid Who Shoves

**If you're studying as an adult:** George's professor mocks him in front of the class over a question. George wants to fire back. Instead he waits until class is over and tells the professor privately: "That comment made me uncomfortable. My question was sincere." The professor half-apologizes. It's not a total win. But George walked out of that room being the person he wanted to be.

**If you're supporting your kids:** a boy shoves Sophie in line. She comes home furious: "Tomorrow I'm going to push him back." Claire doesn't tell her not to be angry. She says: "You have every right to be mad. Do you want to be someone who pushes people?" Together they figure out what to do: tell the boy firmly, and tell the teacher if it happens again. Sophie learns she can stand up for herself without turning into the kid who hurt her.

### In Your Family: The Teenage Son

George's oldest is fifteen and talks back almost every day. George comes home tired from work and feels his blood boil. More than once they've ended up yelling at each other and slamming doors.

George tries Seneca's rule. When his son mouths off, he doesn't respond right then. He says: "I'm not going to talk to you like this right now. We'll talk at dinner." At dinner, cooled off, he tells his son what bothered him, then listens to what's going on in the boy's life. It turns out he's having trouble with a group of kids at school and has been bringing it home with him.

The back talk didn't stop. The door slamming did. And George discovered that behind a lot of his son's attitude was something the boy hadn't been telling him.

### In Money and Health: Traffic and Blood Pressure

At a checkup, Rick finds out his blood pressure is high. The doctor asks about stress. Rick realizes he gets angry in traffic every day, twice a day, for more than an hour total.

He can't change the traffic. He can change what he does in it. He starts leaving ten minutes early so he isn't rushing, listens to audiobooks, and whenever he feels the anger rising, he mutters to himself: "Buy yourself time." It feels ridiculous at first. Three months later, his blood pressure is down. His doctor credits part of it to diet and exercise. Rick knows another part came from quitting his daily fight with the freeway.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Be nice to everyone, even when they mistreat you."** No. Stoic kindness includes correcting people, setting boundaries, and walking away. Tolerating abuse is no part of it.

**"Kindness means pretending you're not upset."** No. Marcus insists that kindness be genuine, not a fake smile. If you're smiling on the outside and boiling on the inside, you're not being Stoic. You're bottling it up, and sooner or later the bottle blows.

**"You shouldn't feel anger."** No. The first flash is involuntary. The point is not to obey it blindly.

# Chapter 7. See Things Stripped Bare

> *When you're served meat or fish, think: this is the dead body of a fish, a bird, a pig. The finest wine is grape juice. The purple robe is sheep's wool dyed in shellfish blood. Thoughts like these cut to the core of things and show them for what they are, and we should think this way all through life: whenever something looks impressive, strip it bare, see how little it's worth, and peel off the story it's puffing itself up with.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.13 (paraphrase)

In a department store window sits a $2,500 handbag. Next to it is one that looks nearly identical for $120. The difference is a logo about an inch wide. Some people spend a year paying off that logo.

At a high-end restaurant, the server spends a full minute describing the dish: "a reduction of," "an emulsion of," "on a bed of." What arrives is an enormous plate with three bites in the middle.

On LinkedIn, someone calls himself a "visionary, disruptor, and transformation leader." He's a sales manager.

Marcus Aurelius had an exercise for all of this: strip things bare. Take away the name, the packaging, and the story, and look at what's left.

## The Context: The Man in the Purple Robe

It's no accident that Marcus writes about the purple robe. Purple was the color of imperial power. Only the emperor could wear it head to toe. It was the ultimate status symbol of the Roman world. And Marcus, who wore it, reminded himself in private that it was sheep's wool stained with snail blood.

In the same passage he turns the exercise on sex, describing it with almost clinical bluntness as friction between bodies. He isn't sneering at pleasure. He's making sure he doesn't become its slave. His method never changes: strip away the emotion, the fame, the decoration, and look at the bare fact. Scholars sometimes call this "physical definition," describing something exactly as it is, minus the value-loaded adjectives we pile on.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Value Lives in Character, Not in Things

For the Stoics, wealth, fame, pleasure, and status are "indifferents." They aren't bad; many of them are "preferred" (being healthy beats being sick, and having money beats not having it). But they aren't good in themselves, because they can be used well or badly. The only true good is virtue.

Stripping things bare is a way of keeping that ranking straight. If the purple robe is dyed wool, the person wearing it isn't worth more for wearing it. If "director" is a chair and a word on a business card, the person who holds the title isn't a better human being for holding it.

**In a nutshell:** things are worth what they are, not what they look like.

### Reading 2. The Practical: An Antidote to Marketing

These days we're surrounded by machinery built to do the exact opposite of what Marcus recommended: dress things up. Advertising doesn't sell you a car; it sells you freedom. It doesn't sell perfume; it sells seduction. It doesn't sell a phone; it sells belonging.

VI.13 makes a great filter for spending decisions. Before you buy something expensive, describe it stripped bare: "a piece of leather with a logo on it." "A laptop that does what mine already does, with a better camera." "An SUV I'll drive to work, alone, every day." If you still want it after that, buy it and don't feel guilty. Often, though, you'll find it was the story you wanted, not the thing.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Beauty?

This exercise comes with a risk: cynicism. If everything is "just" something, then a symphony is "just" vibrating air, a kiss is "just" skin touching skin, and a child is "just" a bundle of cells. Follow that road far enough and nothing is worth anything.

Marcus isn't headed there. Elsewhere he marvels at the beauty of ordinary natural things: bread splitting open as it bakes, ears of wheat bending under their own weight, the foam at a wild boar's mouth. The exercise isn't meant to make you despise everything. It's meant to let the air out of what's inflated, above all the things that stir up excessive desire, envy, or vanity.

A good rule of thumb: strip bare whatever enslaves you; leave clothed whatever makes you better.

**In a nutshell:** stripping things bare isn't looking down on them; it's breaking their spell.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: Remember What It Is You Love

Epictetus suggests a similar exercise, aimed at the things we're attached to. With anything you enjoy or find useful, he says, remind yourself what it actually is, starting with the smallest things. If you're fond of a jug, say: "I'm fond of a jug." Then if it breaks, you won't be shattered along with it.

The example sounds trivial, but the method is powerful. Describing something as it actually is takes away its power to overwhelm you. A new car is a car. A phone is a phone. You can enjoy them both, and neither will wreck you when it gets scratched or lost.

### Seneca: Poor Isn't Having Too Little

Seneca wrote a line people have been quoting for two thousand years: it isn't the person with too little who's poor, but the one who always wants more. For him, wealth isn't measured by what you own. It's measured by the gap between what you have and what you want. Someone whose wants have no limit will always be poor, even with millions.

Seneca, it should be said, was one of the richest men in Rome, and his critics never let him forget it. His answer was that a wise man can own riches as long as he doesn't keep them in his heart. It's a debatable answer, and it's worth reading him with that tension in mind: a rich philosopher preaching simplicity is a reminder that this idea is easier to write than to live.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologists call it *hedonic adaptation*: our tendency to get used to good things. The new car, the raise, the bigger house give us a lift that, over time, settles back to roughly where we started. A classic 1978 study by Philip Brickman and colleagues compared lottery winners with other people and didn't find the winners to be much happier over the long haul. It's a small, old study, but the broader idea has been explored in a lot of research since.

Other work, like psychologist Tim Kasser's, has linked a strong focus on materialistic values (wealth, image, status) with lower well-being on average. And research by Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich suggested that, in many cases, money spent on experiences brings more satisfaction than money spent on things.

None of this means money doesn't matter. It means that once the basics are covered, the things we buy for the story rarely deliver what the story promised.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Stripping things bare sounds depressing. If everything is "just" something, nothing's special anymore.

**Author:** The exercise isn't for everything. It's for whatever has a hold on you: what puts you in debt, what makes you envious, what makes you feel small. You don't need to strip bare your kid's hug or a sunset. Marcus himself marveled at the beauty of natural things. What he strips is prestige, not beauty.

**Skeptic:** But brands, titles, and reputation matter in the real world. If I ignore them, I'll miss opportunities.

**Author:** Of course they matter, as tools. A good title can open a door. A good reputation can land you a client. The Stoics called these "preferred indifferents," things it's reasonable to prefer. The trouble starts when you mistake the tool for your worth as a person, and suddenly you're losing sleep over a logo or a job title.

**Skeptic:** Seneca was loaded. Isn't it hypocritical for a millionaire to tell people not to care about money?

**Author:** It's a real contradiction, and people called him on it in his own time. But you can separate the idea from the man. Besides, Seneca didn't say not to care about money; he said not to *depend* on it. A rich man who could lose everything without losing his head is freer than a poor man obsessed with getting more. Seneca's contradiction works as a warning: this is easy to preach and hard to live.

**Skeptic:** So I'm not allowed to buy myself something nice?

**Author:** You are. If you strip it bare and still want it, and you can pay for it without mortgaging your peace of mind, buy it and enjoy it. The exercise doesn't forbid anything. It clarifies. It makes sure you're buying the thing, not a story somebody sold you.

### In Your Relationship: The Wedding and the Feed

Lauren and Andrew have friends who spent a down payment's worth of money on their wedding. Others post vacation photos every month. Sometimes Lauren feels like their married life is dull by comparison.

She does the exercise. A lavish wedding, stripped bare, is a party that lasts one night. A vacation on Instagram, stripped bare, is one photo picked out of fifty, then edited. Neither one says anything about how those couples treat each other on an ordinary Tuesday.

**The flip side:** stripping things bare also works mid-argument. "You left your socks on the floor" is, stripped bare, two socks on the floor. It is not "you don't respect me." Sometimes a fight fizzles out just from describing the fact without the story.

### At Work: The Title and the Corner Office

Rick gets two job offers. One comes with the title "Director of Operations," a private office, and a 10 percent higher salary. The other is "Senior Manager" at a company with a better culture, flexible hours, and a boss he knows and respects.

The title dazzles him. So he does the exercise. "Director" is a word on a business card. The office is a room with a door. The extra 10 percent works out to about $400 a month, in exchange for a boss known for burning out his people and a two-hour daily commute.

Stripped bare, the second offer is worth more. Rick takes it. A year later, he doesn't miss the word.

### In Business: Real Value

Megan wonders why other studios charge three times what she does. She realizes many of them are selling the story: designer offices, slick pitch decks, buzzwords like "360-degree brand strategy." Behind it, sometimes, the work is no better than hers. Sometimes it's worse.

For a business, the exercise cuts two ways. First, it keeps her from being intimidated: the competition, stripped bare, is people doing similar work. Second, it helps her explain her own value without exaggerating. Megan stops selling "logos" and starts explaining what a good logo does for a business: it makes the business recognizable, trustworthy, distinct. She doesn't embellish; she translates. And she raises her prices without inflating a thing.

**For entrepreneurs:** good marketing doesn't dress things up as something they aren't. It explains what they are.

### At School: The Diploma and the Brand Name

**If you're studying as an adult:** George chose his MBA program for the university's prestige. Halfway through, he notices some professors barely prepare for class, and he starts wondering whether it's worth what he's paying.

Stripped bare, an MBA is a set of classes, readings, classmates, and a piece of paper at the end. The paper opens some doors, and that's worth something. But what he really walks away with is what he learns and who he meets. So George changes how he approaches it. He stops chasing grades alone and starts seeking out the good professors, the classmates who really know their stuff, and the material he can use at work.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie wants a pair of name-brand sneakers because "everybody in my class has them." Claire skips the lecture. She does the exercise with her instead: "What do those shoes do that these don't?" Sophie thinks about it. "Nothing. But they're the ones everyone wears." Claire explains, at a ten-year-old's level, that that's paying for the story. They make a deal: if Sophie still wants them, she can pay half out of her savings. Sophie decides they're not worth it. She's learned something a lot of adults never do.

### In Your Family: The Sweet Sixteen

Lauren's niece is turning sixteen, and Lauren's sister wants to throw a huge sweet sixteen: a rented ballroom, a designer dress, a sit-down dinner for three hundred. She asks Lauren for a loan.

Lauren wants to help, but she does the exercise first. A sweet sixteen, stripped bare, is a night to celebrate someone you love. What the ballroom and the banquet are really buying is something else: not looking bad in front of the family, proving you can pull it off. Lauren tells her sister she'll pay for the cake and help plan, but she won't lend money for the venue. Her sister is hurt for a while.

In the end they throw a smaller party in Grandma's backyard. The birthday girl dances all night. Years from now, nobody will remember whether the venue was fancy. Everybody will remember being there.

### In Money and Health: The New Car

Andrew wants to trade in his car. It's eight years old and runs just fine, but he's seen a new one he loves. It would mean a five-year loan.

Before he signs anything, he writes out the stripped-bare version: "A metal box that would take me to the same job, with a bigger screen and that new-car smell." Then he writes down the story he'd be buying: feeling successful, feeling like he's made it. And he asks himself whether there's another way to get that. He decides to keep his car tuned up, set aside the amount of the monthly payment for a year, and, if he still wants the new one at the end, buy it with a much bigger down payment.

A year later he has a healthy savings cushion, and his craving for the new car has mostly faded. He uses part of the money for a trip with Lauren. The rest goes into an emergency fund, just in case.

**"Nothing is worth anything."** No. The exercise deflates what's inflated, not what has real value.

**"Enjoying luxury is wrong."** No. The Stoics weren't monks. Seneca was very rich. What they criticized was depending on luxury and measuring your worth by it.

**"Brands, titles, and reputation don't matter."** They matter as tools. They don't matter as a measure of who you are.

# Chapter 8. Don't Turn into Caesar

> *Watch out that you don't turn into Caesar, that you don't get dyed purple, because it happens. Keep yourself simple, good, honest, serious, unpretentious, a friend to justice, reverent, kind, warm, and steady in your duty. Fight to stay the person philosophy set out to make you.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.30 (paraphrase)

We all know this guy. He used to be a down-to-earth coworker who loved a good joke. Then he got promoted. Six months later he doesn't say hi in the elevator, talks about "my team" as if he owned it, and stops answering texts from his old colleagues. It's not that he was a bad person. The job changed him.

Marcus Aurelius coined a word for this. In Greek he wrote something you could translate as "to get Caesarified": to turn into Caesar, to let power remake you. He didn't write it about anyone else. He wrote it as a warning to himself.

## The Context: The Man Who Had All the Power

Few people in history have had as much power as Marcus did. He could sentence people to death, declare war, rewrite the law. Before him, Rome had seen emperors go mad with power: Caligula, Nero, Domitian. Marcus knew their stories. He knew power doesn't only corrupt bad people. It wears down good ones too.

That's why, right after this warning, he sketches a long portrait of his adoptive father, Antoninus Pius: an emperor who listened patiently, who couldn't be flattered, who deferred to people who knew more than he did, who could change his mind, who was content with little, and who treated everyone with the same courtesy. Marcus doesn't tell himself "be a great emperor." He tells himself "be like *him*." One concrete role model beats a thousand pieces of advice.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Power Is a Character Test

There's a line that's been credited to all sorts of people: "If you want to know who someone really is, give them power." The Stoics would agree. Power, money, and success are "indifferents," but dangerous ones, because they tempt you to believe you're worth more than other people.

Marcus lists the virtues he has to protect: simplicity, goodness, honesty, seriousness, unpretentiousness, justice, reverence, kindness, warmth, and steadiness. Notice what's missing: effectiveness, intelligence, vision. For him, the danger of power wasn't getting worse at the job. It was becoming a worse person.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Leading by Serving

To Marcus, power was a responsibility, not a prize. To govern was to serve. You can hear an echo of that in what's now called "servant leadership": the leader exists so the team can do good work, not the other way around.

So a Stoic boss's question isn't "How do I get people to do what I say?" It's "What do my people need to do their jobs well?" And the warning sign that you're turning into Caesar is when you start asking the first question and forget the second.

Some concrete signs that power is starting to dye you:
- Nobody pushes back on you anymore, and that feels normal.
- It bugs you when people question you.
- You surround yourself with people who agree with you.
- You talk more than you listen.
- You treat the CEO differently from the night janitor.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: You Don't Have to Be the Boss to Turn into Caesar

It would be convenient to think this meditation is only for powerful people. It isn't. Everybody runs some small empire: the parent at home, the customer facing the server, the teacher facing the student, the expert facing the beginner, the person with more followers facing the one with fewer.

Any time you have even a little power over someone, you're taking Marcus's test. The way you treat people who can't give you anything back (the server, the delivery driver, the intern) says more about you than the way you treat your boss.

And there's a sneakier trap: moral power. The person who feels superior because they meditate, eat clean, or read philosophy. Yes, you can turn into Caesar through Stoicism, too.

**In a nutshell:** we all run a small empire; the test is how we treat people who can't give us anything back.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Tyrant's Tutor

Seneca's story is the best warning about power Stoicism has to offer, precisely because it's a story of failure. Seneca was tutor and adviser to the young Nero. He wrote him a treatise, *On Mercy*, to teach him that the best ruler is the one who masters himself and shows mercy. For the first few years, Nero governed with relative restraint. Then he turned cruel, had his own mother murdered, and eventually ordered Seneca to take his own life.

What does that tell us? First, that even the best advice can't save someone who refuses to listen. Second, that Seneca himself, to stay close to power, put up with things that contradicted everything he taught. It's an uncomfortable lesson and a valuable one: power can corrupt not only the person in charge but also the people standing next to them.

### Epictetus: What the Tyrant Can't Touch

Epictetus, from the opposite end of the social ladder, looks at power from below. In one of his lessons he imagines a tyrant threatening him: I'll throw you in chains. "You'll chain my leg? Fine. But not even Zeus can overpower my will." For Epictetus, any boss's power, however great, stops at the border of each person's will.

That cuts two ways. If you hold power: there's a part of every person you'll never control, so don't try to rule minds; try to win people over. If you're under someone else's power: your boss can decide your tasks, but not who you are.

### A Word from Antoninus: Equanimity

According to an ancient source, the *Historia Augusta*, as Antoninus Pius lay dying, the officer of the guard came to ask him for the day's watchword, as was the custom. Antoninus gave him a single word: *aequanimitas*, equanimity. It was the last message from the man Marcus chose as his model for how to handle power.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologist Dacher Keltner has spent years studying what he calls the *power paradox*: many people rise to power thanks to qualities like empathy and collaboration, but once they're there, those same qualities tend to fade. Various experiments have found that feeling powerful goes along with paying less attention to others and acting more on impulse, though the effects vary from person to person and setting to setting.

It shouldn't surprise us that science backs up Marcus's instinct. What should give us pause is that nobody is immune. Whoever is sure power won't change them has probably already started to change.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** This chapter is for bosses. I'm not in charge of anybody.

**Author:** You sure? Nobody depends on you at home, at work, in your family? You've never dealt with a server, a delivery driver, or someone behind a counter? We all run small empires. Marcus's test isn't just for emperors. It's for anyone who ever has a little power over someone, even for five minutes.

**Skeptic:** If I'm warm and down-to-earth as a boss, people will think I'm weak.

**Author:** Notice that Marcus's list includes "steady in your duty." Simple doesn't mean weak. Antoninus, his model, was an emperor who listened, who changed his mind when given good reasons, and who couldn't be flattered. Nobody thought he was weak. Firmness shows up in your decisions, not your tone of voice.

**Skeptic:** But power sometimes demands toughness. You have to fire people. You have to push them.

**Author:** True, and Marcus made brutally hard calls. He passed sentences; he went to war. The chapter isn't asking you to dodge hard decisions. It's asking you not to become someone who enjoys having power over others. You can let someone go respectfully. You can push people without humiliating them. A hard decision doesn't require a cruel delivery.

**Skeptic:** And if power's already changed me?

**Author:** The fact that you're asking is a good sign. People who've been completely Caesarified don't ask. Marcus's remedy is simple and humbling: find a role model, take an honest look at how you behave, and ask someone you trust to tell you the truth. Then, above all, listen to the answer without getting defensive.

### In Your Relationship: Power at Home

When Lauren left her job for a year to stay home with their newborn son, Andrew became the only one earning a paycheck. Without noticing, he started making the money decisions alone, asking her to account for grocery receipts, saying "my money."

One night Lauren called him on it: "Ever since you became the only one earning, you talk to me like I work for you." Andrew felt attacked at first. Later he admitted she was right. He'd turned into Caesar in his own home.

Couples have all sorts of power sources: whoever earns more, whoever knows more about something, whoever gets angrier, whoever loves less. The meditation asks you to look at what power you have over your partner and how you're using it. In a healthy relationship, power gets shared, or put to work for both of you.

### At Work: The Promotion

At his new job, Rick finally gets promoted to lead a team of six. In his first week, he notices people laughing at his jokes even when they're not funny, and nobody contradicting him in meetings.

He sets himself three rules so he won't turn into Caesar:

2. **Lunch with the team, not the other managers.** At least once a week.
3. **The Antoninus portrait.** He writes down the name of the best boss he ever had, the one he worked for at twenty-eight, plus three things that boss did well. Every month he checks whether he's doing them.

### In Business: Success Goes to Your Head

Megan's business is taking off. She hires two designers. One day she catches herself yelling at one of them over a small mistake, in front of a client. She can't sleep that night.

A growing business brings two temptations: believing you did it all yourself, and starting to treat people like tools. Megan decides to apologize to the designer the next day, in front of the same client if she can. It's hard. It's the most Stoic thing she does all year.

**For entrepreneurs:** as you grow, your character flaws get multiplied by the number of people who depend on you. One bad day for the owner turns into the company culture.

### At School: The One Who Knows More

**If you're studying as an adult:** after retaking statistics, George becomes the person in his cohort who understands it best. He starts helping the others. Then one day he catches himself correcting a classmate with a sigh: "That's obvious, isn't it?"

He remembers Alexander the grammarian, whom Marcus thanks in Book I for teaching him not to correct people with scorn, but to offer the right answer tactfully, almost in passing. George changes his approach. Instead of "that's wrong," he asks, "What if we look at it this way?" His classmate keeps asking questions.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie is class representative. At home she proudly reports that she scolded a classmate for misbehaving. Claire asks her, "And how do you think he felt?" ### In Your Family: The Sister Who Took Over

When Megan's father died, she became the sister who's "good with numbers" and took over the estate and all the family paperwork. Bit by bit, without noticing, she started deciding everything on her own: what to sell, what to keep, what to pay and when. Her siblings stopped weighing in.

One day her younger sister tells her: "Ever since Dad died, you're not my sister anymore. You're the administrator." It stings, but Megan knows it's true. She'd turned into Caesar in her own family. From then on, every estate decision gets made in a meeting with everyone, even when it takes longer. She loses some efficiency and gets her siblings back.

### In Money and Health: Whoever Pays Calls the Shots

Since her promotion, Lauren has been out-earning Andrew. At first nothing changed. Then one day, in an argument about vacation plans, it slips out: "At the end of the day, I'm the one paying." Andrew goes quiet and sleeps on the couch.

Lauren remembers what happened when the roles were reversed, when Andrew was the only one earning, which we described at the start of this chapter. She sees that money is a form of power and that she's fallen into the same trap. The next day she apologizes, and they agree on something: both their paychecks belong to the household, and big decisions get made together, no matter who brought in more.

# Chapter 9. Do Less, but Do What Matters

> *"If you want peace of mind, do few things," says the wise man. But isn't it better to do what's necessary, what reason demands of a social being, and in the way it demands? That brings the peace of doing things well and the peace of doing fewer of them. Most of what we say and do isn't necessary; cut it, and you'll have more time and less turmoil. So at every moment, ask yourself: is this necessary? And cut not only unnecessary actions but unnecessary thoughts, so that pointless actions don't follow them.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, IV.24 (paraphrase)

Open your calendar for the week. Count the meetings. Now count how many of last week's meetings actually changed anything. Open your inbox and count the unread messages. Open your to-do list and count the items that have been sitting there for over a month.

If you're like most adults, the answer is the same every time: too many. We're busy all the time, but not necessarily with what matters. Marcus Aurelius, who had an empire to run, found the question that cuts through the tangle: **is this necessary?**

## The Context: Correcting a Wise Man

The line Marcus quotes at the start, "do few things if you want peace of mind," comes from the philosopher Democritus. Marcus respects it, and then improves on it. The goal is to do the necessary things, however many that turns out to be. An emperor couldn't do few things: he had to hear cases, write laws, lead campaigns. But he could stop doing what served no purpose.

The distinction matters. "Doing little" can just be laziness. "Doing what's necessary" is a choice. And for Marcus, what's necessary is defined by two things: what reason requires, and what your role in the community requires.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Necessary, According to Reason

For the Stoics, every person plays roles: son or daughter, parent, citizen, professional, friend. Epictetus said every role comes with duties. What's necessary is what your roles ask of you. Everything else is noise.

Marcus's question works as a moral filter. Does one of my roles ask this of me? Does it help anyone? Or am I doing it out of vanity, fear, habit, or because I don't know how to say no?

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary is what your role asks of you; the rest is decoration.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Essentialism, Two Thousand Years Early

There are whole shelves of productivity books saying the same thing in different words: the 80/20 rule (20 percent of what you do produces 80 percent of your results), minimalism, "no" as a strategic tool. Marcus got there in one line.

The practical version goes like this: every "yes" you give to something unnecessary is a "no" to something necessary. Say yes to the pointless meeting, and you've said no to an hour of real work. Say yes to the social obligation you don't want, and you've said no to dinner with your partner.

And Marcus adds something almost no productivity book mentions: **unnecessary thoughts**. The worries on a loop, the imaginary conversations, the replaying of something someone said last week. They're invisible tasks clogging up your mental calendar.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Who Gets to Decide What's Necessary?

This one's a serious objection. What's unnecessary to me may be necessary to someone else. The meeting I think is pointless may matter to my boss. The family get-together that bores me may mean everything to my mother.

Marcus's answer is tucked into the phrase "what reason demands of a social being." What's necessary isn't what I feel like doing. It's what, thought through calmly, serves the whole. Sometimes going to the family dinner that bores you is necessary, because being a son or daughter asks it of you. Sometimes skipping the meeting is what's necessary, because your actual work is waiting.

The test is function, not comfort. And it takes some nerve to tell "I don't want to" apart from "it isn't necessary."

**In a nutshell:** what's necessary is whatever serves, which isn't always what you like.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Life Isn't Short; We Waste It

In *On the Shortness of Life*, Seneca writes a line that could have been written for us: it isn't that we have too little time, but that we waste so much of it. Life is long enough, he says, if we use it well. But when it gets spent on luxuries, on carelessness, on busywork that leads nowhere, we wake up to find it's gone and we never actually lived it.

Seneca describes the people he calls the *preoccupied*: people who rush from one obligation to the next, who take care of everyone except themselves, who are waiting for "retirement" to start living. And he asks, a little dryly, who promised them they'd make it that far.

### Epictetus: Be Willing to Look Foolish

Epictetus has advice for anyone who can't say no: if you want to make progress, be willing to look foolish and uninformed about outside matters. Don't pretend to know everything or be part of everything. If people start thinking you're important, be suspicious of yourself.

Saying no to what's unnecessary costs something. Sometimes you'll come across as rude, uncommitted, or out of the loop. Epictetus asks you to pay that price. Whoever tries to please everyone ends up with no time for what matters.

### What Science Says Today

Researcher Gloria Mark has spent years studying how people work at screens. One of her most widely cited findings is that after an interruption, it can take people quite a while to get fully back into what they were doing, around twenty minutes or more in some studies. Her research has also found that our attention on screens is jumping from thing to thing faster and faster.

Other research on multitasking suggests that, most of the time, we aren't doing two things at once. We're switching rapidly between them, and every switch has a cost. In Marcus's terms: every unnecessary thought and action eats up not only its own time but also pollutes the time you meant for what's necessary.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "Is this necessary?" is an easy question when you don't have a boss. I can't just cancel meetings.

**Author:** Not all of them, no. But a lot of people find they can do more than they thought: ask for an agenda, suggest an email instead of a meeting, ask for the notes. And even if you can't cancel anything on your calendar, you can always prune what's going on inside your head: unnecessary thoughts, replaying yesterday's meeting, imaginary arguments. Marcus insists on that second kind of pruning because it's the one that's always within your power.

**Skeptic:** What's unnecessary to me might matter to someone else. Isn't this just selfish?

**Author:** It would be if "necessary" meant "whatever I like." But Marcus defines it by what your role as a social being calls for. Sometimes what's necessary is going to the family dinner that bores you, because your mother needs you there. The question doesn't excuse you from your duties. It frees you from what you do out of habit, vanity, or fear.

**Skeptic:** If I say no to everything, I'll end up with no friends and no opportunities.

**Author:** Saying no to everything is just as thoughtless as saying yes to everything. You take it case by case. And here's something you discover with practice: when you say no to what's unnecessary, your "yes" starts to mean more. People know that if you agree to something, you'll actually show up.

**Skeptic:** What about downtime? Is watching a show unnecessary?

**Author:** Rest is necessary. Watching a show you love, with your partner, after a rough day, might be exactly what your life needs that night. ## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Packed Calendar

Lauren and Andrew's week is full: their son's activities, birthday parties, work obligations, dinner at his mother's, the gym, groceries. They hit Sunday exhausted, without having had a single conversation longer than ten minutes.

They sit down with the month's calendar and go through it, commitment by commitment: is this necessary? ### At Work: Meetings and Email

Rick runs the numbers: he spends fourteen hours a week in meetings. In half of them, he doesn't say a word. In a third of them, nothing gets decided.

He applies Marcus's question. Before accepting any meeting, he asks for an agenda. If there's no agenda, he asks whether it could be handled over email. He drops two weekly meetings where he just listens and asks for the notes instead. He checks email three times a day instead of every time a notification pops up.

Within a month he's gotten back six hours a week. He uses them for the things nobody asks for but everybody needs: planning, thinking, mentoring his team.

**For leaders:** the most Stoic meeting is the one that never gets called because it didn't need to happen.

### In Business: The Client Who Isn't Worth It

Megan realizes that 20 percent of her clients cause 80 percent of her headaches: endless revisions, late payments, constant complaints. And they barely bring in any money.

Letting them go scares her; she's afraid of losing income. But she asks: is this client necessary? She runs the numbers and finds that dropping the three worst would cost her 10 percent of her revenue and give her back 30 percent of her time. With that time, she could land a better client.

She raises her rates for those three. Two of them walk. The one who stays pays what her work is worth.

**For entrepreneurs:** not every client is a good client, not every product deserves a spot in the catalog, and not every opportunity is really an opportunity. Saying no is a business decision.

### At School: Studying What Counts

**If you're studying as an adult:** George doesn't have much time. He works, he has a family, and he's in school. At first he tried to read every assignment, underline every page, and write perfect summaries. He burned himself out.

He applies the question: what do I actually need to do to learn this well? He identifies the core concepts in each course, practices with problems instead of rereading, and accepts that he can't read everything with the same care. His grades go up while his study hours go down.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie is signed up for Spanish, swimming, piano, and robotics. She's worn out and cranky. Claire and Sophie go through the activities together, and Claire asks which ones Sophie actually enjoys. They keep two. Sophie gets her afternoons back for playing, which, when you're ten, is necessary.

### In Your Family: The Go-To Guy

Rick's family has a habit of handing him every piece of paperwork: his aunt's property tax payment, his cousin's tax return, his brother's driver's license renewal. "You know how to do that stuff." Rick never says no, so he spends his weekends running other people's errands.

He asks himself: is this necessary? Some of it is: his mother really does need help with her paperwork. Some of it isn't: his cousin and his brother can do it themselves. So he offers something different. Instead of doing it for them, he walks them through it once. They grumble at first. A year later, they don't need him, and Rick has his Saturdays back.

### In Money and Health: The Subscriptions

Megan looks at her bank statement and finds eleven subscriptions: three streaming services, two music apps, a meditation app she hasn't opened since March, design software she no longer uses, a digital magazine, an online fitness program, and two services she doesn't even remember signing up for.

She puts Marcus's question to each one: is this necessary? She cancels seven and saves about $90 a month. But she also notices something else. With fewer platforms, she spends less time picking what to watch and more time doing other things. Pruning her spending turned out to be pruning her time, too.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Do the bare minimum."** No. Sometimes what's necessary is a lot. Marcus worked incredibly hard. The point is cutting what's superfluous, not doing as little as possible.

**"Say no to everything."** No. Saying no to everything is just another way of not thinking. You take it case by case.

**"Necessary means whatever's convenient for me."** No. What's necessary includes what's yours to do for other people, even when it isn't convenient.

## This Week's Exercise: The Pruning

1. **Calendar:** go through next week's commitments. Put an X next to the ones that aren't necessary. Cancel or hand off at least one.
2. **To-do list:** delete every item that's been sitting there for more than a month with no consequences for not doing it. If it really mattered, it'll come back.
3. **Thoughts:** when you catch yourself replaying something (a conversation, a fear, a slight), ask quietly: "Is this thought necessary?" If it isn't, go back to what you were doing.
4. **The missing necessary thing:** write down one necessary thing you've been putting off. Put it on your calendar with a date and time.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** every morning, pick the three necessary things for the day and write them down before you open your email.
- **Intermediate:** practice one kind "no" a week. Turn down a commitment, a meeting, or a favor that isn't necessary, without a long explanation.
- **Advanced:** track your time for three days, writing down what you did in half-hour blocks. At the end, mark what was necessary and what wasn't. Figure out how many hours a week go to the unnecessary, and decide what you'd do with half of them.

## Key Ideas

- At every moment, ask: is this necessary?
- Every yes to the unnecessary is a no to what matters.
- Prune unnecessary thoughts, too.
- What's necessary is defined by your role toward others, not by your comfort.

## Journaling Question

> **What am I doing only because I'm afraid to say no?**

---

Open your calendar again. The meetings, the emails, the to-dos are all still there. But now you've got a three-word question that an emperor used to run an empire. You don't need to do less to have peace of mind. You need to do what matters. The rest can wait, or it can disappear.
# Chapter 10. The Future Isn't Yours

> *Don't let the future rattle you. You'll meet it, if you have to, armed with the same reason you're using right now to handle the present.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VII.8 (paraphrase)

> *Don't let the picture of your whole life crush you. Don't start tallying up every hardship that might be waiting for you. Instead, as each one comes, ask: what here is actually unbearable? You'll be embarrassed to answer. Remember that neither the future nor the past weighs on you, only the present, and the present gets smaller once you draw a line around it.*
> — *Meditations*, VIII.36 (paraphrase)

It's 3:14 a.m. and you can't sleep. Your mind is running the numbers: what if I get laid off, what if we can't make tuition, what if the business never gets off the ground, what if this pain in my chest is something serious, what if my partner doesn't love me anymore, what if my kids head down the wrong path. One thought leads to the next. By 4:00 you've lived through ten disasters that haven't happened.

Morning comes, and none of it happened. You're just tired.

Seneca wrote in one of his letters that we suffer more in imagination than in reality. Marcus Aurelius reached the same conclusion on a different sleepless night.

## The Context: Ruling Without Knowing What Came Next

Marcus ruled during one of Rome's most uncertain stretches. The plague came and went. The borders kept shifting. He never knew if the next campaign would be his last, if his health would hold up, or if his son Commodus, his heir, would be ready for the job. History says he wasn't.

Marcus couldn't control that future. What he could do was remember that whenever it arrived, he'd face it with the same tools he had today: reason, character, experience. And that the weight pressing on him at night wasn't coming from the present. It came from adding up every problem that might ever happen.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Trust Your Tools

VII.8 says something simple and deeply reassuring: your future self will have the same tools your present self has. If you could handle what came at you today, you'll be able to handle tomorrow. Not because things will go well, but because you'll be there, with your reason intact.

Think about it this way: you've survived 100 percent of your worst days so far. You didn't handle all of them well, but you made it here. That's a fact, not a motivational poster.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Break Up the Load

VIII.36 hands you a concrete technique: don't look at your whole life at once. When you see every hardship stacked together (the thirty-year mortgage, the kids' college, retirement, your parents' health), the weight is unbearable. But you'll never have to carry all of it at the same time. You only ever carry today's share.

Marcus offers a question: **what is unbearable right now?** Not this year, not this decade. Right now. The answer is almost always: nothing. You're sitting down. You're breathing. You can do something.

It's the same logic behind "one day at a time" in recovery programs, and behind every marathoner's advice: don't think about mile 26; think about the next one.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: So We Shouldn't Plan?

If the future shouldn't bother us, does that mean we don't save, don't make a will, don't plan for retirement? That would be absurd. And Marcus doesn't say that.

The difference is between **planning** and **worrying**. Planning is thinking about the future so you can act today: opening a savings account, getting a checkup, updating your résumé. Worrying is thinking about the future without doing anything: chewing on the same thing at 3 a.m.

The premeditation of adversity from Chapter 2 was useful because it ended in preparation. Worry is useless because it ends in more worry.

Here's a quick test. If, while you're thinking about the future, you can write down something concrete to do today, you're planning. If all you can write is another "what if...?", you're worrying.

**In a nutshell:** planning is thinking ahead so you can act today; worrying is living in a future you never act on.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: Hope and Fear Are Handcuffed Together

In one of his letters, Seneca quotes the Stoic Hecato: you'll stop being afraid when you stop hoping. Then he explains the image. Hope and fear walk along together, like a prisoner and the guard he's chained to. Both are looking ahead. Anyone anxiously hoping for something also fears not getting it; anyone afraid of something also hopes it won't come. The root of both, Seneca says, is that we don't settle into the present. We send our thoughts out far ahead of us.

That doesn't mean living without goals. It means not hanging your peace of mind on a future you don't control. You can work toward something with everything you've got without letting your calm today depend on getting it.

In another letter, Seneca writes the line we mentioned at the top of the chapter: more things frighten us than actually crush us, and we suffer more often in imagination than in reality. His advice is not to run out to meet our troubles. Many of the things we dread may never come, and the ones that do don't need to hurt in advance.

### Epictetus: The Singer Who Shakes

Epictetus notices that whenever he sees someone anxious, he asks himself what that person wants. If they weren't wanting something outside their control, they wouldn't be anxious. He uses the example of a musician. Singing alone, he's perfectly relaxed. Walking onstage, he trembles. Why? Because he doesn't just want to sing well, which is up to him. He also wants applause, which isn't.

In this reading, anxiety is almost always the sign of a desire parked in the wrong place.

### What Science Says Today

A 2019 study by researchers Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman asked people with generalized anxiety disorder to write down their worries over a period of days and then check which ones actually came true. The vast majority, about 90 percent, didn't. And in many of the cases where something did happen, it turned out better than they'd feared. It's a small study, but it's a perfect illustration of Seneca's point.

There's also a technique used in some treatments for excessive worry, built on the work of psychologist Thomas Borkovec: set aside a fixed time each day for worrying, and postpone any worry that crops up outside that window. That's the "planning hour" this chapter's exercise suggests.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Not worrying about the future is irresponsible. If I don't worry, I won't save.

**Author:** That's why the chapter separates worrying from planning. Saving *is* planning: a concrete action, today, with the future in mind. Worrying is spinning your wheels about the future without doing anything. In fact, worry often gets in the way of saving, because the fear gets so big that we'd rather not even open the bank statement.

**Skeptic:** But some futures really are scary: getting old, getting sick, the economy.

**Author:** Absolutely. The Stoics never pretended danger doesn't exist. Seneca recommended imagining it on purpose, through the premeditation of adversity. But Stoic imagination has an endpoint: it ends in getting ready. The 3 a.m. version doesn't end in anything. It just circles back on itself. The question is whether your thinking about the future leaves you better prepared or just more exhausted.

**Skeptic:** "You'll meet it with the same tools you have today" isn't much comfort when I feel like I don't have any.

**Author:** That's an honest objection. But think about what you've already come through: losses, crises, changes you were sure would break you. You got through them, not always gracefully, but you made it here. Those are your tools, even if you can't feel them today. And if you truly feel you have none, maybe what you need isn't philosophy but professional help. Asking for it is another way of arming yourself.

**Skeptic:** And hope? Seneca says to stop hoping. That's bleak.

**Author:** He's talking about anxious hope, the kind that hangs your peace on an outcome you can't control. He's not asking you to give up on goals or dreams. He's asking you not to let your calm today hinge on them coming true. You can give something everything you've got and still be at peace if it doesn't happen.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Afraid It Will End

After the crisis in Chapter 4, Lauren and Andrew are still together, and therapy has helped. But Lauren lives with a low hum of fear: what if it happens again? What if one day he decides he's done? Every time Andrew walks in looking serious, her mind jumps to the worst.

Her therapist tells her something very Stoic: "You can't love him well today if you're busy being afraid of losing him tomorrow." Lauren starts practicing the VIII.36 question: what's unbearable right now? Right now, Andrew is in the kitchen making coffee. Right now, the marriage is good.

There are no guarantees in love. But fearing the end doesn't protect a relationship. It wears it down. The only thing that does protect it is how the two of them treat each other today.

### At Work: The Rumor Mill

At Rick's company, there are rumors of layoffs. Nobody knows anything. Everybody speculates in the hallways. Productivity tanks because everyone spends the day running the numbers.

Rick makes two lists. **Planning:** update his résumé, figure out how long his savings would last, call two contacts. He knocks it out in one afternoon. **Worrying:** everything else. He decides to stay out of the hallway rumors.

While his coworkers spend three weeks in agony, Rick works. In the end, the layoffs don't touch him. But if they had, he'd have been ready. Either way, the same strategy covered him.

### In Business: Debt and Scenarios

Megan took out a loan to buy equipment. One slow month and she's picturing repossession, bankruptcy, and the shame of telling her family.

Her accountant shows her a tool Marcus would have loved: three scenarios. She writes down what happens if the year is good, average, or bad. For the bad one, she spells out what she'd do: cut costs, renegotiate the loan, sell a piece of equipment. Once the worst case has a plan, it stops being a monster. It becomes a to-do list.

**For entrepreneurs:** fear grows in the dark. Put numbers on the future, and half the fear disappears. The other half turns into a plan.

### At School: The Capstone and the Kids' Future

**If you're studying as an adult:** the week before his capstone presentation, George can't sleep. He pictures the panel failing him, two years of work down the drain, his family watching him fall short. He applies VIII.36: today there's no presentation. Today he has to review chapter three. So he reviews it. Tomorrow, chapter four. On presentation day, he'll have the same tools he used to prepare every chapter.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire worries about Sophie's future. Will her grades be good enough for middle school? Will she find a job when she grows up? What kind of world is she going to inherit? Claire realizes the worry is turning her into a control freak: she checks homework with a magnifying glass and compares her daughter to other girls. So she decides to do something more useful: give Sophie what she needs today, which is a calm mom, dinner together, and someone who listens. Sophie's future won't be built out of her mother's anxiety. It'll be built out of the days they actually live.

### In Your Family: Aging Parents

Lauren can't stop thinking about what'll happen when her parents can't take care of themselves anymore. Who'll look after them? Will they have enough money? Will she have to quit her job? Every visit is shadowed by that anxiety, and instead of enjoying her parents, she watches them for signs of decline.

She applies the difference between planning and worrying. **Planning:** a conversation with her siblings and her parents about what they want, checking whether they have health insurance and a will, finding out what care options exist nearby. They cover it in two meetings. **Worrying:** everything else.

With a plan in place, Lauren goes back to visiting her parents for what matters: having dinner with them, hearing her dad tell the same stories for the hundredth time, learning her mom's apple pie recipe. The future will come. Today, they're here.

### In Money and Health: Retirement

Andrew is forty-one and hasn't given retirement a thought. When he does, he panics. He hasn't saved enough, Social Security feels shaky, and he's heard stories about older people with nothing left. The anxiety is so big that he'd rather not think about it at all, and since he doesn't think about it, he doesn't do anything.

It's a textbook case of worry causing paralysis. Andrew decides to turn the fear into a plan. He checks his 401(k) balance, books an appointment with a financial adviser, and commits to putting in a small but fixed amount every month. He doesn't solve the future in one go. But for the first time, he's facing it with the tools he has today.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Don't think about the future."** No. Think about it so you can prepare. What doesn't help is living it before it gets here.

**"Everything will be fine."** No. Marcus never promises that. He promises you'll have what you need to face it, however it turns out.

**"Philosophy can cure anxiety."** Not always. If worry has been keeping you up for weeks, or it's affecting your work or your health, get professional help. Stoicism can go hand in hand with therapy. It can't replace it.

## This Week's Exercise: The 3 A.M. Page

Keep a notebook and a pen next to your bed. When worry wakes you up:

1. Write the fear down in one sentence: "I'm afraid that..."
2. Underneath, write: **What can I do about this tomorrow?** If there's something, write it down. Now it's a plan.
3. If there's nothing you can do, write: **"This isn't getting solved tonight."**
4. Ask yourself: **what is unbearable right now?** Take three breaths and go back to bed.

During the day, set aside fifteen fixed minutes for "planning the future": money, health, work. Outside those fifteen minutes, when a worry shows up, tell it: "I'll see you at planning hour."

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** keep a notebook by your bed and use the 3 a.m. page whenever you need it.
- **Intermediate:** for two weeks, write down your main worries with the date. At the end, check which ones happened and which didn't, like the participants in the LaFreniere and Newman study.
- **Advanced:** put Epictetus's question to your biggest anxiety: what do I want here that isn't within my power? Then write down what it would look like to go after the part that *is* within your power without letting your peace depend on the part that isn't.

## Key Ideas

- You'll face the future with the same tools you have today.
- Planning ends in action; worrying ends in more worry.
- Break up the load: what is unbearable right now?
- Most of what we dread never happens.

## Journaling Question

> **What disaster have I already lived through a dozen times in my head without it ever actually happening?**

It's 3:14 again. Your mind wants to start running the numbers. But now you have a notebook and a question. What is unbearable right now? The pillow is cool. The house is quiet. You're breathing. The future will come, if it has to, and when it does, you'll be there, with the same tools you have tonight.
# Chapter 11. What's Bad for the Hive

> *What's bad for the hive is bad for the bee.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.54 (paraphrase)

> *What doesn't harm the city doesn't harm the citizen.*
> — *Meditations*, V.22 (paraphrase)

This is the shortest line in the book, and maybe the most demanding. A bee can't survive on its own. Its life depends on the hive, and the hive depends on what each bee does. A bee that looks out only for itself dies, and the whole hive gets weaker.

Now think about your own life. The partner who "wins" the argument and loses the relationship. The employee who grabs the credit and wrecks the team's trust. The business that squeezes its customers and loses its reputation. The student who cheats and never learns the material. Every time, the bee thought it was winning. The hive lost. And in the end, so did the bee.

## The Context: Citizen of the World

The Stoics adopted a word usually credited to the Cynic Diogenes: *cosmopolitan*, "citizen of the world." They believed all human beings share reason, and that this makes us one community, beyond borders, classes, and languages. Marcus, who ruled an empire made up of dozens of peoples, said it over and over: we're parts of a single whole, like the limbs of one body.

A Stoic named Hierocles turned the idea into an image. Picture each person at the center of a set of circles. The innermost is yourself; then your family; then friends and neighbors; then your city; then your country; then all of humanity. The moral task, he said, is to pull the outer circles inward: to treat people far away a little more like the people close to you.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Enlightened Self-Interest

For Marcus, there's no real contradiction between your own good and the common good. What harms the whole ends up harming you; what helps the whole ends up helping you. There's no cosmic karma involved. It works that way because we're social creatures, and we live on trust, cooperation, and reputation.

From this angle, VI.54 is a decision rule. Before you act, ask whether it's good for the hive: your family, your team, your company, your community. If it's bad for the hive, it's probably bad for you too, even if it looks good in the short run.

**In a nutshell:** whoever wins at the group's expense loses in the long run.

### Reading 2. The Practical: The Couple and the Team as "Us"

There's a very useful reading here for relationships: stop thinking "me against you" and start thinking "us against the problem." In a couple, if one person wins the argument and the other loses, the relationship loses. On a team, if one department wins the budget and another gets nothing, the company loses.

The hive is more than the sum of its bees: something new exists between them. A couple is more than two people; it's a relationship that needs looking after, almost like a third party. A team is more than its members; it's a culture.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What If the Hive Is Rotten?

There's a danger lurking here. "What's bad for the hive is bad for the bee" can be used to crush individuals: "sacrifice for the company," "put up with it for the family's sake," "don't report it, you'll hurt the institution's image." Plenty of abuse has hidden behind "the good of the group."

But using it that way betrays the meditation. For Marcus, the real hive is the human community guided by reason and justice, not whatever group you happen to belong to. A company that exploits its employees isn't a healthy hive; it's a sick one. A family that covers up violence isn't protecting the hive; it's destroying it. In cases like that, exposing the injustice is what's good for the hive. And if the violence is happening inside your own home, protecting yourself and getting help (in the U.S., the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, or 911 in an emergency) doesn't betray your family. It's the first step toward stopping the harm.

Besides, a hive that mistreats its bees doesn't survive. The good of the group includes the good of every member.

**In a nutshell:** serving the hive doesn't mean obeying the group; it means protecting what makes the group good.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: We're Limbs of One Great Body

In one of his longest letters, Seneca sums up the Stoic ethic of living together in a single image: we are limbs of one great body. Nature made us family by building us from the same material and for the same purpose. And he quotes a line from the Roman playwright Terence that became famous: "I am human; nothing human is foreign to me."

Seneca compares society to a stone arch. It would collapse if the stones didn't hold each other up, and that mutual support is exactly what keeps it standing.

### Musonius Rufus: Marriage as a Partnership for Life

Musonius Rufus, Epictetus's teacher, was one of the few ancient philosophers who wrote in any detail about marriage, and he did it in a way that sounds surprisingly modern. For him, marriage wasn't just a way to have children. It was a partnership in life, where two people take care of each other in sickness and in health, each trying to outdo the other in attention and care. Musonius also argued, unusually for his time, that women should study philosophy just as men did.

If a couple is a hive of two, Musonius would be its best beekeeper. What counts is who takes better care of what they share, not who wins.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologist John Gottman has spent decades studying couples in his lab. One of his best-known observations is that certain behaviors during conflict (criticizing the person rather than the behavior, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling) are red flags for a relationship. Stable couples fight too. What tends to set them apart is how they fight, and how they repair things afterward.

At work, an internal research effort at Google known as Project Aristotle looked at what made its most effective teams tick. One of its most widely publicized conclusions was the importance of *psychological safety*: team members feeling they can speak up, ask questions, or make mistakes without being humiliated, a concept researcher Amy Edmondson had studied before. In Marcus's terms, that's a healthy hive.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** "What's bad for the hive is bad for the bee" is exactly what companies say when they want you to sacrifice yourself.

**Author:** They say it, and they often use it to exploit people. But that's a betrayal of the line. A hive that mistreats its bees dies. For Marcus, the real hive is a community guided by justice, not any group that demands your obedience. If your company is wringing you dry, the line doesn't say you have to take it. It gives you an argument for pointing out that the company is hurting itself.

**Skeptic:** In a relationship, thinking about "us" can make one person disappear.

**Author:** That's a real risk, especially for someone who's learned to always give in. That's why Musonius describes marriage as a partnership where both people take care of each other, not one where one person sacrifices for the other. A hive of two doesn't work if one bee does all the work. Thinking about "us" includes thinking about what you need, because you're part of the "us" too.

**Skeptic:** What about competition? In business, if you don't compete, you get eaten alive.

**Author:** Competing isn't the problem. What does the damage is winning at the expense of trust: lying to customers, squeezing suppliers, stealing a colleague's idea. That might give you an edge today, but it destroys what's holding your business up tomorrow. There's a way of competing that makes everyone better: doing better work. That's the kind of competition that's good for the hive.

**Skeptic:** Hierocles's circles sound nice, but I can't love all of humanity.

**Author:** Hierocles isn't asking you to. He's asking you to pull the circles in a little: treat a stranger a bit more like a neighbor, and a neighbor a bit more like family. Forget abstract universal love. Think of saying good morning to the security guard by name. It's small, and that's exactly why it's doable.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Winning the Argument

Lauren and Andrew have the same fight about vacations every year: she wants to go to the beach with her family; he wants to go to the mountains, just the two of them. Every year somebody "wins," and the other one spends the trip resentful. Neither of them has a good time.

In therapy they learn to ask the hive question: what's good for *us*? They discover the fight was never really about the beach or the mountains. It was about something deeper: Lauren needs to feel her family is welcome, and Andrew needs time alone with her. They land on a solution: a long weekend with her family, plus four days on their own somewhere else.

**A tool:** in your next argument, switch seats and sit on the same side of the table, with the problem written on a sheet of paper in front of you both. It sounds like a game. It changes the whole conversation.

### At Work: Credit and Information

On Rick's team, one coworker has a habit of presenting other people's ideas as his own. It works: he gets promoted. But within a year, the team stops sharing ideas in meetings. Everyone holds back their best stuff so they can present it solo. Innovation grinds to a halt.

When Rick becomes team lead (Chapter 8), he changes one rule: every presentation credits whoever came up with the idea. It seems minor. Within three months, the meetings are full of ideas again.

**For leaders:** your team is the hive. Reward people who win at others' expense, and you teach everyone to compete against their colleagues instead of against the problem.

### In Business: Clients, Suppliers, and Competitors

Megan is offered a big project on one condition: she has to cut her price, and the only way to do that is to pay less to the freelance illustrators she works with. She'd come out ahead; they'd lose.

Megan asks what's good for her hive: her network of collaborators, which took her years to build and has bailed her out more than once. If she cuts their pay, the best ones will leave. She turns down the project on those terms and proposes a different scope. The client goes for a smaller version.

A few months later, a competitor asks for help with a project she can't finish on her own. Megan could take advantage. Instead she pitches in, and they split the fee. The following year, that competitor sends her two clients.

**For entrepreneurs:** your business lives inside a hive of customers, suppliers, employees, competitors, and community. Squeeze any of them and you're squeezing your own future.

### At School: Cheating and Sharing

**If you're studying as an adult:** in George's MBA program, a classmate offers him the answers to an exam, which he got from someone in a previous semester. George is tired, and the help would be welcome. But he asks: what's good for the hive? If everyone cheats, the degree is worth less for everyone. And he won't learn what he came to learn. He says no. Instead he starts a study group and shares his notes with anyone who needs them. Sharing isn't the same as cheating: one makes the hive stronger, the other weakens it.

**If you're supporting your kids:** a new girl has joined Sophie's class, and nobody invites her to play. Sophie doesn't either; she's afraid her friends will leave her out. Claire tells her about Hierocles's circles: "Right now that girl is in your outer circle. What would happen if you pulled her in a little?" The next day, Sophie asks her to sit with them at lunch. Nothing bad happens. The hive gets a little bigger.

### In Your Family: The Inheritance

When Andrew's grandmother dies, his aunts and uncles start fighting over her house. Everyone has an argument: who took care of her most, who chipped in for the remodel, who lives closest. Within two months, siblings who loved each other have stopped speaking.

Andrew, the oldest grandchild, suggests they ask the hive question: what's good for the family? He reminds them that his grandmother spent her whole life gathering them in that house. Selling it and splitting the money might be fair, but losing the family over it would betray everything the house stood for. They agree to bring in a mediator. It takes almost a year, but they reach a deal. Not everyone is happy. But they sit down together again at Christmas.

### In Money and Health: Money as a Couple

Lauren and Andrew have always kept their finances separate: each paid their own expenses, and they split household bills down the middle. It worked, until Andrew went through a stretch of lower income after a job change, and half the household bills became a real burden for him.

They sit down and think like a hive. They decide to pay into a joint account in proportion to what each of them earns, keep a separate amount for personal spending, and make big decisions together. It's not the only way to do it, and every couple has to find its own. But switching the question from "what's fair to me?" to "what's good for both of us?" got rid of a source of resentment they'd been carrying around for years.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Always sacrifice yourself for the group."** No. A healthy group needs healthy members. Taking care of yourself is part of taking care of the hive.

**"Whatever the majority says is right."** No. The Stoic hive is guided by reason and justice, not by majority opinion.

**"Never compete."** No. Honest competition can make everybody better. What does the damage is winning at the expense of trust, fairness, or other people's well-being.

- Whatever hurts the "us" ends up hurting you.
- Look after your relationship as if it were a third person at the table.
- Serving the hive doesn't mean obeying the group; it means protecting what makes it fair.
- Pull the outer circles a little closer to the center.

# Chapter 12. What I Owe to Others

> *From my grandfather Verus: good character and an even temper. From my mother: devotion, generosity, keeping clear not only of doing wrong but even of thinking it, and living simply, far from the habits of the rich. From Rusticus: being introduced to the teachings of Epictetus. From Alexander the grammarian: not correcting people with reproach, but offering the right phrase tactfully. From Fronto: seeing up close what a tyrant's envy, cunning, and hypocrisy look like.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book I (selections, paraphrased)

Who taught you to drive? Who gave you your first job? Who explained something nobody else had the patience to explain? Who told you something twenty years ago that you still remember when you're about to give up?

Chances are you never thanked them the way they deserved. Hardly anyone does. Marcus Aurelius, the most powerful man in the world, devoted the entire first book of his *Meditations* to doing exactly that.

## The Context: A Book of Thank-Yous

Book I of the *Meditations* is unlike the rest. No reflections on death, anger, or the universe. It's a list. Seventeen short sections, nearly all starting the same way: "From my grandfather...," "From my mother...," "From my teacher..." Marcus goes through each person who shaped him and writes down what he learned from them.

What's remarkable is the detail. He doesn't write "my mother was a good woman." He writes that she taught him to live simply. He doesn't write "my teacher was wise." He writes that Alexander taught him how to correct people without humiliating them. And the portrait he paints of his adoptive father, Antoninus, is so detailed it reads like a manual for being a good person with power.

He even learns from the negative. From Fronto, his rhetoric teacher, he says he learned what envy and hypocrisy look like in tyrants, and that the people we call aristocrats often lack real warmth. Not everything we learn comes from good examples.

The book ends with thanks to the gods for everything he had, and even for the things that never happened to him.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Nobody Is Self-Made

To the Stoics, human beings are social by nature. Much of who we are, we owe to others: the people who raised us, taught us, corrected us, gave us a shot. For a Stoic, the "self-made man" is a myth.

Book I is an antidote to arrogance. An emperor who reminds himself every day "I got this from my grandfather, this from my mother, this from my teacher" is going to have a hard time believing he's better than anyone else. It's the vaccine against the "turning into Caesar" problem from Chapter 8.

### Reading 2. The Psychological: Gratitude as a Practice

Modern psychology has studied gratitude extensively. A number of studies suggest that regularly writing down what you're grateful for goes along with greater well-being, better sleep, and stronger relationships. But according to that research, the key is the same one Marcus models: be specific. "I'm grateful for my family" does less than "I'm grateful my sister called when I was sick."

Marcus doesn't write generic thank-you notes. He writes down specific behaviors he picked up. That turns gratitude into more than a feeling. It becomes a way of remembering who you want to be.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What If My Family Hurt Me?

Not everyone had an even-tempered grandfather and a generous mother. Some people grew up with violence, with neglect, or with people who taught them the worst of everything. Are they supposed to be grateful?

Marcus gives us a clue with Fronto: we also learn from what we don't want to repeat. Gratitude doesn't mean excusing harm. It means recognizing what you took away, even from a bad experience: "From my father, I learned how I *don't* want to treat my kids." That isn't gratitude toward the father. It's gratitude for your own ability to learn.

Besides, Book I isn't only about family. It includes teachers, friends, a brother, colleagues. If your family let you down, maybe there were others: a teacher, a neighbor, a coach, a boss, a friend. Marcus's list is an invitation to go looking for those people.

**In a nutshell:** you also learn from the people who showed you what you don't want to become.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: The Art of Receiving

Seneca wrote seven books *On Benefits*, a whole treatise on how to give, receive, and be grateful. One of his finest insights is that gratitude is also a skill, and many people never learn it: they accept help with indifference, forget it quickly, or feel humiliated about owing anybody anything. For Seneca, receiving a kindness with real gratitude is already the first payment on the debt.

He also warns that the most common kind of ingratitude is simply forgetting. That's why, for the Stoics, gratitude isn't only a feeling. It's an exercise in memory.

### Epictetus: Thankful for What You Bring to the Fight

Epictetus devotes one of his lessons to being grateful for something we almost never think about: our own abilities. If we had any sense, he says, we'd do nothing but praise and give thanks, because we've been given the faculties we need to bear whatever happens to us: patience, courage, reason. Instead, he complains, most people skip the gratitude and just lament what happens to them.

It's a different kind of gratitude from Marcus's: not toward other people, but toward what you carry inside you to face life. The two go well together.

### What Science Says Today

In a widely cited 2003 study, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked one group of participants to write down things they were grateful for each week, a second group to write down hassles, and a third simply to list events. On average, the gratitude group reported feeling better about their lives and more optimistic. Later research has generally pointed the same way, though the effects are modest, and it's no miracle cure.

What's interesting is that several studies line up with Marcus's method: gratitude seems to work best when it's specific, and when it's expressed to the person rather than just felt privately.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Gratitude is nice and all, but sometimes it's just a way of settling. "Be grateful for what you have and don't ask for more."

**Author:** That use of it exists, and it's a con. But Marcus was grateful and, at the same time, worked tirelessly to improve both his empire and himself. Being grateful for what you've received doesn't stop you from fighting for what you're missing or calling out what's wrong. If anything, people who recognize what others have done for them tend to want to do more for others.

**Skeptic:** And if my parents hurt me? Do I have to thank them, too?

**Author:** No. You can acknowledge what you learned from a painful experience without thanking the person who caused it. Marcus learned from Fronto what a tyrant's hypocrisy looks like. He wasn't thanking him for being a hypocrite; he was acknowledging a lesson. Your Book I can include what you don't want to repeat. That shaped you, too.

**Skeptic:** Gratitude lists feel corny.

**Author:** They can be, if you just write "thanks for everything" every night. What Marcus does is different. He writes down concrete behaviors he learned from concrete people. It's almost an inventory of his character. It isn't an exercise in warm feelings; it's an exercise in memory. Knowing where your strengths came from helps you take care of them, and keeps you from believing you invented them all on your own.

**Skeptic:** Why tell the other person? It's enough that I know.

**Author:** Maybe it's enough for you. But think about them: a teacher, a former boss, a friend who has no idea they changed your life. Telling them costs you almost nothing and could mean a great deal to them. And there's one more reason, a very Stoic one: you won't always have time to say it.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: What I Learned from You

Lauren and Andrew have been together for ten years. Lately they've learned to fight better (Chapter 6) and to think in terms of "us" (Chapter 11). But it's been a long time since they told each other what they've gotten from one another.

For their anniversary, their therapist suggests an exercise: each of them writes a "Book I" about the other. Andrew writes: "From Lauren I learned to say what I feel instead of shutting down. To get organized. To apologize without making excuses." Lauren writes: "From Andrew I learned to laugh at myself. Not to take everything so seriously. To rest without feeling guilty."

They read them aloud. Both of them cry. After ten years, they realize, they've been shaping each other all along, and neither of them had noticed.

### At Work: Mentors

Rick, now a team lead, starts thinking about what kind of boss he wants to be. He writes his own professional Book I. From his first boss, he learned to walk into meetings prepared. From a coworker, he learned to negotiate without fear. From the director who used to humiliate him, he learned how not to treat his team. From last year's intern, he learned to try new tools without being embarrassed to ask questions.

Then he does something harder. He sends a message to his first boss, whom he hasn't seen in fifteen years: "I lead a team now, and every week I use something I learned from you. Thank you." The boss, long since retired, replies with a photo of the two of them at the office back in the day.

**For leaders:** gratitude flows downhill too. Telling your team what you've learned from them is worth more than any bonus.

### In Business: The People Who Hold You Up

Megan spends an evening looking back over the history of her studio. She realizes it wouldn't exist without a handful of people: the first client who trusted her before she had a portfolio; the accountant who taught her to keep her personal and business finances separate; the illustrator who gave up a whole weekend to save a deadline; her sister, who lent her the money for her first computer.

She decides to do something with that list. In December, instead of sending generic corporate gifts, she writes each of those people a handwritten letter telling them what they did for her business. It takes her an afternoon. Several of them write back that it's the best thing they've ever received from a vendor.

**For entrepreneurs:** your business has a Book I. Writing it reminds you that you didn't do it alone, and it reminds them why they're glad they stuck with you.

### At School: Teachers

**If you're studying as an adult:** as he wraps up his MBA, George remembers the elementary school teacher who told him he was a good writer, and the tutor who patiently walked him through statistics. In his capstone report, he writes an acknowledgments page that's more than a formality: he names each person and what he learned from them. A classmate tells him he's never read acknowledgments like that.

**If you're supporting your kids:** at the end of the school year, Claire suggests Sophie write a letter to her teacher. Not "thanks for everything," but three specific things she learned that year. Sophie writes: "You taught me not to be scared of getting things wrong when I go up to the board." The teacher keeps the letter. Without ever saying so, Claire has taught her daughter the exercise that opens the *Meditations*.

### In Your Family: Grandparents

Claire never had a good relationship with her father. He was distant, sometimes harsh. When he gets sick, she visits out of obligation. One day her therapist suggests she write a Book I about him, including both the good and what she doesn't want to repeat.

Claire writes: "From my father I learned to work hard and keep my word. I also learned how I don't want to talk to my daughter." Reading it over, she realizes both things are true, and that she can be grateful for the first without excusing the second. On her next visit, she tells her father only the first part. He doesn't say much. But that afternoon, he asks her to bring his granddaughter next time.

### In Money and Health: The Family Doctor

George has had the same family doctor since he was a kid. When he was diagnosed with a thyroid problem years ago, the doctor didn't just write a prescription. He patiently explained what was going on and talked straight with him about his habits. George never thanked him beyond the "thanks, Doc" at the end of every appointment.

In his Book I, George writes: "From Dr. Miller I learned that taking care of yourself is a responsibility to your family, not vanity." He brings the page to his next appointment. The doctor, who's about to retire, tucks it into his desk drawer. George realizes some people hold up our lives for decades without our ever telling them.

**"Feeling it is enough."** No. Marcus's gratitude is written down and specific. Gratitude that never gets expressed or remembered just fades.

# Chapter 13. Remember That You Will Die

> *You could leave this life right now. Let that shape what you do, what you say, and what you think.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, II.11 (paraphrase)

> *Don't live as if you had ten thousand years ahead of you. Death is hanging over you. While you're alive, while you still can, be good.*
> — *Meditations*, IV.17 (paraphrase)

There's a story told about Rome's victory parades. As the triumphant general rode through the streets to the roar of the crowd, a slave standing behind him whispered in his ear, reminding him that he was mortal. *Memento mori*: remember that you will die.

We don't know for sure that it happened just like that. But the idea has lasted two thousand years because it says something we all know and almost never think about: time runs out. And what we do with ours depends a great deal on remembering that.

## The Context: A Man Surrounded by Death

Marcus Aurelius didn't need to imagine death. It was everywhere around him. The plague killed millions across his empire. He watched soldiers die at the front. Several of his children died young. His wife, Faustina, died before him. His own health was fragile, and he died at fifty-eight, on campaign, far from Rome.

The *Meditations* are full of reminders of death. Marcus notes that Alexander the Great and his mule driver ended up in the same place. That hardly anyone remembers the mighty emperors who came before him. That a life is a single instant between two eternities. He isn't writing this to depress himself. He's writing it to get clear: if time is short, don't spend it on what doesn't matter.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Death as the Measure of What Matters

To the Stoics, death isn't a bad thing. It's a natural process, like being born or growing up. What is bad is living badly: in fear, in hatred, in vanity, without doing what you should.

Death works as a yardstick. If tomorrow were your last day, would you still be mad at your brother about the inheritance? Would you spend tonight scrolling? Would you put off calling your father? The question in II.11 isn't "What would you do with one day left?", because that invites you to blow everything on pleasure. It's "What would you do, say, and think if you knew this could end at any moment?" And the answer is almost always: the same things you always do, just with more care, more honesty, and more love.

**In a nutshell:** death doesn't drain life of meaning; it gives it a deadline.

### Reading 2. The Practical: Urgent Versus Important

There's a productivity tool usually credited to President Dwight Eisenhower: sorting tasks into urgent and important. Urgent things shout: the email, the phone call, the notification. Important things whisper: your health, your relationship with your kids, the work that gives your life meaning.

*Memento mori* works like an amplifier for that whisper. When you remember that your time is limited, the important stuff gets louder. Many people who go through a serious illness or lose someone close say the same thing: suddenly, they knew what mattered. The Stoics suggest not waiting for the illness to find out.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: Isn't This Morbid?

For a lot of people, thinking about death is depressing, even frightening. Is it really healthy to do it every day?

The Stoic answer is that the morbid thing isn't thinking about death. It's avoiding it. Our culture hides death away: in hospitals, in rushed funerals, in phrases like "we lost him." And by hiding it, we live as if we had ten thousand years, exactly as Marcus warned: putting things off, fighting over nothing, holding back what we ought to say.

That said, this needs care. If you're dealing with a recent loss, with depression, or with thoughts of hurting yourself, this exercise isn't for you right now. Please reach out for professional help. *Memento mori* is a practice for valuing life, not for pulling away from it. In the United States, you can call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) any time, free and confidential. Outside the U.S., contact your local crisis line or emergency number.

**In a nutshell:** remembering death isn't loving death; it's learning to love life before it's over.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Seneca: "I Have Lived"

Seneca wrote a great deal about death, and nearly always as a way of talking about life. In one letter he proposes a nightly exercise: go to bed cheerfully and say, "I have lived. I've run the course fortune gave me." If we're given tomorrow, he adds, let's take it as a bonus. Anyone who can say "I have lived" each night wakes up every morning to a day that's a gift.

In another letter, he says that we die a little every day: each day we lose a piece of our life, so death isn't some far-off event but something already underway. Again, he's not trying to depress himself; he wants to value the hours he actually has.

Seneca had to face death in a brutal way himself: Nero ordered him to take his own life. According to the historian Tacitus, he met it calmly, comforting his friends and reminding them of what he'd tried to teach them.

### Epictetus: Don't Say "I Lost It"

Epictetus gives a piece of advice that can sound harsh, though many grieving people have found comfort in it over time. Never say of anything, "I lost it." Say, "I gave it back." Did your child die? Your child was given back. Did your wife die? She was given back. For as long as it's entrusted to you, he says, take care of it as something that isn't yours, the way travelers take care of an inn where they're staying.

This needs to be read gently. Epictetus isn't telling us not to cry. He's asking us to remember that the people we love were never ours. They were a gift. And the pain of losing them is, in a way, a measure of how much they gave us.

### What Science Says Today

In the 1990s, psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec studied what people regret. They found that in the short run, we tend to regret the things we did wrong. But in the long run, looking back over a whole life, we regret what we *didn't* do: the chances we passed up, the words we never said, the roads we never took.

It's a modern echo of this chapter's question: what am I putting off as if I had ten thousand years? The things we skip because we assume there'll be time are the ones that weigh the most when the time runs out.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Thinking about death every day sounds morbid.

**Author:** It would be, if you did it for hours every day. But *memento mori* is a quick reminder, not an obsession. It's like the "low battery" warning on your phone. It's telling you to make good use of what's left, not that the phone is about to explode. What's actually morbid is living as if death didn't exist and then finding out all at once in a hospital room.

**Skeptic:** If I'm going to die anyway, why not spend it all and do whatever I want?

**Author:** Because Marcus doesn't say "live like you're dying tomorrow." He says "while you're alive, while you still can, be good." The conclusion he draws is seriousness, not reckless pleasure. If time is short, don't waste it on grudges, pointless busywork, or things that don't matter. People who've come close to death rarely say, "I should have spent more." They tend to say, "I should have said more, hugged more, forgiven more."

**Skeptic:** That line from Epictetus, "don't say I lost it, say I gave it back," feels cold when someone you love dies.

**Author:** I understand why it feels that way, especially early in grief. It's not something you say to someone at a funeral. It's an idea that, over time, has helped many people turn the anger of loss into gratitude for what they had. The person we loved was never ours; they were a gift. The pain is so enormous precisely because the gift was.

**Skeptic:** What if I don't believe in anything after death?

**Author:** Marcus wasn't sure either. In several passages he considers both possibilities: that something comes after, or that we simply dissolve back into nature. And he reaches the same conclusion either way: there's nothing to fear, and the only thing that matters is how we live while we're here. It's one of the most comforting ideas in this book, and it works just as well for believers as for nonbelievers.

**For entrepreneurs:** thinking about death is also thinking about continuity. A business that dies with you may never have been a business so much as a job you created for yourself. That's fine, if you chose it. But it's worth deciding on purpose, not finding out too late.

### At School: Learning for Its Own Sake

**If you're studying as an adult:** as he finishes his MBA, George wonders whether he'll keep learning. He's thirty-nine. A few years ago he'd have thought it was too late. Now he sees it the other way around: precisely because time isn't unlimited, he doesn't want to stop learning what interests him. He signs up for guitar lessons, something he's wanted to do since he was fifteen.

A late Roman historian named Ammianus Marcellinus tells a story about Socrates. In his final days, waiting to be executed, he heard someone singing a poem and asked to be taught it. When someone asked him why, since he was about to die, he answered something like: "So I'll know one more thing before I go." It may not have happened that way, but the idea is worth holding on to.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie's grandmother dies. It's the first time someone close to her has died. Claire doesn't tell her Grandma "went away on a trip" or "is sleeping." She explains, in simple words, that Grandma died, that it's natural and it's sad, and that they can remember her. Together they make a list of the things they learned from her, a little Book I. Sophie learns that death is something you can name, cry about, and turn into gratitude.

### In Your Family: The Conversation You Keep Putting Off

Rick and his brother, the one who lives near their mother and hardly ever visits, had a falling-out a year ago. Their last argument turned into a shouting match in their mom's kitchen, and since then they talk only when they absolutely have to. Each is sure he's right.

When an uncle, their father's brother, is diagnosed with a serious illness, Rick catches himself thinking something he didn't want to think: someday, one of the two brothers is going to bury the other, and the fight will still be sitting there, unresolved.

He sends a short text: "I don't know if I'm right or not. But I don't want this to be the last thing between us." His brother takes two days to answer. They meet up at a bar near their mother's house. They don't settle everything. But the next Sunday, for the first time in a year, they have lunch with her together.

### In Money and Health: The Checkup You Keep Skipping

Andrew hasn't had a checkup in four years. Something more urgent always comes up: work, errands, the hassle of going to the clinic. After his father's heart attack, which we described at the start of this chapter, he remembers Marcus's line: don't live as if you had ten thousand years.

He makes an appointment that same week. The results come back mostly fine: his cholesterol is a little high, nothing serious. The doctor says there's still plenty of time. Andrew walks out of the clinic thinking that taking care of his health isn't just a personal matter. It's a way of taking care of Lauren and their son, who are counting on him to be around.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"You shouldn't be afraid of death."** Fear is natural. What Stoicism asks is that the fear not keep you from living.

**"You should think about death all the time."** No. A quick reminder now and then is enough to rearrange your priorities. Thinking about it all day isn't Stoicism; it's anxiety.

# Conclusion: Stop Arguing About What a Good Man Is; Be One

> *Enough of arguing about what a good man should be like. Be one.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, X.16 (paraphrase)

You've reached the end of the book. Maybe you underlined sentences, folded down pages, texted someone a photo of a paragraph. Maybe you thought about your partner, your boss, your business, your kids. All of that is fine. And none of it matters if tomorrow you're exactly the same.

## What the Emperor Didn't Do

Marcus didn't write a philosophical treatise. He didn't found a school. He didn't give lectures on Stoicism. He wrote for himself, at night, in the middle of a war, and then got up to try to live what he'd written. He often failed. His notebooks are full of self-reproach, repeated resolutions, lessons he had to learn again and again.

That's the most valuable thing about the *Meditations*: they aren't the book of a sage, but of someone trying to become one. And that's why they're so useful to us. You don't need to be perfect to be a Stoic. You need to start again every morning.

## The Thirteen Reminders

If you had to carry this book on a single page, it would be this one:

1. **What is within your power:** separate what you control from what you don't; put your energy into the first.
2. **Difficult people:** expect them, understand them, and don't hand them your day.
3. **Get up:** don't consult your feelings; remember why you get up.
4. **The obstacle:** what blocks a plan opens a virtue.
5. **The dye of the mind:** watch what you think, see, and hear every day.
6. **Kindness:** anger costs more than the offense; calm firmness is invincible.
7. **Seeing things stripped bare:** pay for what things are, not for the story.
8. **Don't turn into Caesar:** power is a test of character.
9. **What's necessary:** every yes to the unnecessary is a no to what matters.
10. **The future:** plan, don't worry; you'll get there with the same tools you have today.
11. **The hive:** what harms the "us" ends up harming you.
12. **Gratitude:** no one is self-made; say it out loud.
13. **Death:** time is limited; while you can, be good.

## What Happened to Our Characters

Lauren and Andrew are still together. They fight less, and better. On Sundays they have breakfast without phones. One day, mid-argument, Lauren catches herself saying "pause" before he does.

Rick leads his team, leaves on time two days a week, and doesn't miss his son's games. On his desk there's a card with four words: "Is this really necessary?"

None of them became wise. All of them became a little better. For a Stoic, that's the only goal that matters.

# Appendix: A 30-Day Stoic Routine

This routine brings together the exercises in the book into a one-month program. You don't need more than fifteen minutes a day: five in the morning, five at some point during the day, and five at night. You can start on any day.

## The Daily Structure

### In the Morning (5 minutes)

Before checking your phone:

1. **The blanket sentence** (Chapter 3): read the sentence that answers "why do I get up?"
2. **The day's rehearsal** (Chapter 2): think of the most difficult person or situation today. What's likely to happen? How will you respond?
3. **What's necessary** (Chapter 9): choose the three necessary things for the day.

### During the Day (5 minutes, spread out)

- When you feel anger: **the card with the nine reminders** (Chapter 6).
- When you feel anxious: **what is unbearable in this moment?** (Chapter 10).
- When you're tempted to buy something, or you catch yourself envying someone: **the stripped-bare definition** (Chapter 7).
- Before an important decision: **is this good for the hive?** (Chapter 11).

### At Night (5 minutes)

Seneca tells us that the philosopher Sextius, every night before sleeping, asked himself: What bad habit did I cure today? What fault did I resist? In what way am I better? Seneca adopted the habit. You can do the same with three questions:

1. What did I do well today?
2. Where did I go wrong?
3. What would I do differently tomorrow?

And add one line of specific gratitude (Chapter 12): "Today I'm grateful that ___."

# Glossary of Stoic Terms

**Adiaphora (indifferents).** Things that are neither good nor bad in themselves, such as health, wealth, fame, or pleasure. Their value depends on how they're used.

**Preferred indifferents.** Among the indifferents, those it's reasonable to prefer: it's better to be healthy than sick, or to have enough than to live in poverty. But preferring them doesn't mean depending on them.

**Apatheia.** Literally, "without passions." It doesn't mean apathy or indifference, but freedom from excessive, irrational passions, such as runaway anger or paralyzing fear.

**Assent (synkatathesis).** The act of accepting or rejecting an impression as true. For the Stoics, this is where our freedom lies: we don't choose the impressions that come to us, but we do choose whether to accept them.

**Ataraxia.** Tranquility or calm of the soul. The term was also used by the Epicureans and the Skeptics.

**Cosmopolitanism.** The idea that all human beings are citizens of a single universal community, because we all share reason.

**Dichotomy of control.** The distinction between what is within our power and what isn't. It's the foundation of Epictetus's *Handbook* and of Chapter 1.

**Eudaimonia.** Usually translated as "happiness," but it means something closer to "a life well lived" or "flourishing." For the Stoics, it is reached through virtue.

**Eupatheiai (good emotions).** The healthy emotions that, according to the Stoics, a wise person feels: calm joy (instead of runaway pleasure), reasonable caution (instead of fear), and goodwill or reasonable wishing (instead of unchecked appetite). They show that Stoicism doesn't aim for coldness.

**First movements.** Immediate, involuntary reactions, such as being startled or blushing. Seneca calls them *primi motus* in Latin; later authors used the Greek term *propatheiai*, "pre-passions." They aren't within our power; what we do next is.

**Hegemonikon.** The ruling part of the soul, the faculty that reasons, judges, and decides. Marcus Aurelius often refers to it as an inner citadel.

**Logos.** The reason that, according to the Stoics, orders the universe and is also present in every human being.

**Memento mori.** "Remember that you will die." A Latin phrase summing up the practice of recalling one's own mortality in order to live better.

**Oikeiosis.** The process by which a being takes ownership of what belongs to it and cares for it. It starts with oneself and extends to family, friends, and humanity, as in Hierocles's circles in Chapter 11.

**Physical definition.** The exercise of describing something exactly as it is, without the value-laden adjectives we add. It's the method of "seeing things stripped bare" in Chapter 7.

**Premeditatio malorum.** "Premeditation of adversity." The exercise of imagining in advance what might go wrong, so as to be prepared and not caught off guard.

**Prohairesis.** Epictetus's term for the faculty of choice, the will. According to him, it is the only thing that is truly ours.

**Providence.** The Stoic belief that the universe is ordered by a divine reason that cares for the whole. Marcus Aurelius holds it, though he repeatedly considers the alternative of a universe of atoms without order.

**Reserve clause (hupexairesis).** Acting with an implicit condition: "I will do this, if nothing prevents it." It lets you commit without being at the mercy of the outcome.

**Stoa.** Porch or colonnade. The school takes its name from the *Stoa Poikile*, the Painted Porch in Athens where Zeno taught.

**View from above.** The exercise of imagining your own life and problems as seen from high above, as part of an immense whole. Marcus Aurelius practices it in several passages, for example IX.30.

**Virtue (arete).** Excellence of character. For the Stoics, the only true good. It's divided into four: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control.

# Recommended Reading

If this book has whetted your appetite, here are a few doors to keep going. Choose whichever edition and translation suits you best; there are many good ones.

## The Sources

- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*.** The book that inspired this one. Read it slowly, a few pages a night, the way it was written. Translations by Gregory Hays, Robin Hard, and Robin Waterfield are all widely recommended.
- **Epictetus, *Handbook* (*Enchiridion*).** Very short; you can read it in an afternoon and reread it for the rest of your life.
- **Epictetus, *Discourses*.** The complete lessons, longer and livelier than the *Handbook*.
- **Seneca, *Letters from a Stoic* (*Letters to Lucilius*).** One hundred twenty-four letters on how to live. The best place to get to know Seneca.
- **Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life*, *On Anger*, and *On Tranquility of Mind*.** Short and very practical essays.
- **Musonius Rufus, *Lectures*.** Epictetus's teacher, less known and surprisingly modern on topics like marriage and the education of women.

## Studies and Popular Introductions

- **Pierre Hadot, *The Inner Citadel*.** The classic study of the *Meditations*, for those who want to go deeper.
- **Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*.** On ancient philosophy as a way of living.
- **William B. Irvine, *A Guide to the Good Life*.** A practical, modern introduction, where the idea of the "trichotomy of control" appears.
- **Massimo Pigliucci, *How to Be a Stoic*.** A contemporary philosopher explains how he applies Stoicism, in dialogue with Epictetus.
- **Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning*.** Not a Stoic book, but one of the most powerful testimonies to inner freedom in the face of adversity.

A final caution: you don't need to read them all. Marcus Aurelius told himself to set aside his thirst for books and devote himself to living. Read one, practice what you read, and then, if you like, read another.
