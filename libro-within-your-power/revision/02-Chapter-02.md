# Chapter 2. At Dawn, Brace Yourself for Difficult People

> *When the day begins, tell yourself: today I'll run into people who are nosy, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and rude. They're like this because they can't tell good from bad. But I've seen what good is, and I know that whoever wrongs me shares my nature. None of them can actually hurt me, and I can't be angry with someone who is, in a real sense, family. We were made to work together, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the upper and lower rows of teeth.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, II.1 (paraphrase)

The alarm goes off at 6:30. Before you've even opened your eyes, you know how the day is going to go: the meeting with the director who talks over everyone, the supplier who'll swear the order "already shipped," the parents' group chat fighting about the class holiday party, your mother-in-law coming over for Sunday dinner. You pull the pillow over your face.

Marcus Aurelius knew that feeling. That's why he opened one of his notebooks with an exercise that sounds gloomy and works more like a vaccine.

## The Context: Writing in Enemy Territory

An old note in the manuscripts says these entries were written "among the Quadi, on the river Granua," which is to say in the middle of a military campaign against Germanic tribes, a long way from Rome. Marcus spent much of the last decade of his life at the front. Yet the people who worried him most weren't the barbarians. They were his own: ambitious generals, flattering senators, officials who said one thing and did another.

He didn't write "today everything will go well." He wrote the opposite: today you're going to deal with difficult people. Then he reminded himself of three things. They act this way out of ignorance. They share his nature. And we were all made to work together.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Rehearsing the Bad Stuff

The Stoics practiced *premeditatio malorum*, the premeditation of adversity. Seneca recommended it constantly: picture ahead of time what could go wrong, so that if it happens, it doesn't blindside you. The point isn't to invite trouble or live in fear. A blow you saw coming simply hurts less than one you didn't.

When Marcus tells himself "today I'll meet ungrateful people," he isn't being bitter. He's clearing out false expectations. Most of our anger at other people comes from a quiet demand we never say out loud: "people ought to be reasonable." They aren't always. Knowing that at 6:30 in the morning spares you the outrage at 11.

### Reading 2. The Socratic: Nobody Does Wrong on Purpose

Marcus says difficult people behave the way they do because they "can't tell good from bad." That idea goes back to Socrates: nobody picks what's wrong knowing it's wrong; they pick it because they've convinced themselves it's good for them. The coworker who steals your idea thinks it's protecting his job. The client who haggles you down to an insult thinks he's being shrewd.

Seeing it this way changes the emotion underneath. If the other person is acting out of a mistake, the fitting response looks less like fury and more like the patience you'd have with someone who took a wrong turn. It doesn't let them off the hook. It explains them.

Psychologists today have a name for something similar: the *fundamental attribution error*. We chalk up our own slipups to circumstances ("I was late because of traffic") and other people's to their character ("he was late because he's irresponsible"). Marcus is asking you to give others the same benefit of the doubt you give yourself.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: So I'm Supposed to Put Up with Everything?

The objection writes itself. If everyone is "family" and "can't hurt me," am I supposed to tolerate the abusive boss, the partner who belittles me, the business partner who's skimming off the top?

No. Read Marcus closely. The other person *can't hurt you* in the Stoic sense: they can't make you a worse person. Your character stays yours. That doesn't stop you from protecting yourself, keeping your distance, reporting someone, or walking away from a relationship. Feet and hands cooperate, sure. But if one hand got infected, the body would treat it. It wouldn't pretend the hand was fine.

The meditation is a rule for keeping resentment out of your system. It's no agreement to be a doormat. In practice, the difference looks like this: you can walk away from someone without hating them, and you can demand something without humiliating anybody.

**In a nutshell:** understanding a difficult person doesn't mean you have to stay close to them.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: "That's How It Looked to Him"

Epictetus offers advice almost identical to Marcus's for moments when someone mistreats us or talks behind our back: remember that they're doing what they believe is right. They can't follow what seems right to you, only what seems right to them. If they've got it wrong, they're the ones who pay, because they're the ones deceived. Start from there, he says, and you'll be gentle with whoever insults you. Each time, you'll simply say, "That's how it looked to him."

That little phrase is a tool you can carry in your pocket. It doesn't say the other person is right. It says they acted on what they understood, and that your job isn't to fix their thinking from a place of outrage. Your job is to decide what *you* will do.

### Seneca: Don't Get Mad at Someone Stumbling in the Dark

In his treatise *On Anger*, Seneca gives us a useful picture: a wise person doesn't get angry at people for making mistakes, any more than he'd get angry at someone tripping in the dark, or at a sick person for groaning. Human errors are so many and so common that getting angry at each one would mean living in a permanent state of anger.

Then he adds something refreshingly realistic: stop expecting people to be different from how they are. Walk out the door expecting everyone to be kind, and you'll spend the day offended. Walk out knowing you'll meet every kind of person, and you'll be able to enjoy the good ones and put up with the rest.

### What Science Says Today

In 1977, psychologist Lee Ross put a name to a bias we all fall into: the *fundamental attribution error*. We tend to explain other people's behavior by their character ("he's rude") and our own by our situation ("I was exhausted"). Knowing about the bias won't make it go away, but it helps you catch yourself, which is exactly what Marcus is doing when he reminds himself that people act out of ignorance, not malice.

Psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, meanwhile, has spent years studying what she calls *mental contrasting*: picturing not just the goal you want but the specific obstacles likely to get in the way. Her research suggests that people who imagine the obstacles and plan their response tend to make more progress than people who only picture success. It's a modern take on *premeditatio malorum*. Preparing for trouble isn't pessimism. It's strategy.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Making a list of difficult people every morning sounds like a great way to start the day in a bad mood.

**Author:** It would be, if you did it with a grudge. Do it the way a pilot checks the weather before takeoff, and it's a different thing. The pilot isn't rooting for a storm; he just doesn't want to be surprised by one. Seeing difficult people coming won't ruin your mood. Getting caught flat-footed will.

**Skeptic:** Isn't it unfair to think badly of someone before you've even seen them?

**Author:** You're not thinking badly of anyone in particular. You're accepting something close to a statistic: on any given day, someone is going to be rude, late, or unfair. Could be your boss, could be a stranger, could be you. Marcus doesn't write "my colleague is an idiot." He writes "I'll run into people like this." That's humility about how the world works, not a verdict on any one person.

**Skeptic:** "They act out of ignorance" sounds naive. Some people know exactly what they're doing.

**Author:** That's the strongest objection, and you're partly right. Some people know perfectly well they're hurting you. But the Socratic point is subtler: that person believes hurting you is good *for them*. They're wrong about what's good for them, even if they're right about the effect on you. That doesn't make them innocent. It just helps keep their mistake from becoming your obsession.

**Skeptic:** And what if the difficult person is my partner? Or my mom?

**Author:** Then the exercise matters even more, because you can't just walk away from them. Getting ready for the hard side of someone you love isn't loving them less. It's loving them with your eyes open.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: Sunday Dinner

Lauren knows that on Sunday they're having dinner at Andrew's mother's house, and that, like every month, her mother-in-law will comment on the house, on Lauren's job, or on how "it's about time" they had another baby. Lauren usually shows up tense, sits through it in silence, and then unloads on Andrew in the car on the way home.

**The Stoic version of Sunday morning:** Lauren tells herself, "Today my mother-in-law is going to make some comment. She does it because she thinks she's looking out for her son and their family, even if her delivery is terrible. She can't make me rude, and she can't ruin my day."

Then something practical: she talks to Andrew ahead of time. "If your mom brings up the baby thing again, can you change the subject?" Preparing is a mental exercise, sure, but it's also planning.

**The flip side:** it works in both directions. Andrew can brace himself for the evening Lauren comes home wiped out from work and snaps at him. Getting ready for the hard side of someone you love is its own kind of love.

### At Work: The Director Who Talks Over Everyone

Every week, Rick has a meeting with a director who interrupts, jumps from topic to topic, and shoots down ideas before he's heard them out. Rick walks out of every one of these meetings with a headache.

At 7 a.m. on Monday, Rick tries meditation II.1. He writes on an index card: "The director is going to interrupt me today. It's not personal; he does it to everyone. My job is to make my proposal clear, not to change him."

With that expectation set, he changes his approach. He brings his proposal on a single page, leads with the bottom line, and sends a follow-up email afterward summarizing what was agreed. The meeting is the same as ever. Rick isn't.

### In Business: The Client Who Haggles

Megan has a prospect who always wants a discount, compares her quote with his nephew who "also does design," and needs everything yesterday. Before every call, she can feel her blood pressure climbing.

The Stoic version: before the call, Megan writes down three things the client is *likely* to say ("that's too expensive," "my nephew will do it for less," "I need it by Friday") and drafts an answer to each one. She also sets her floor: the lowest price she'd accept, and the point at which she'll politely say no.

That's what a Stoic business looks like. It doesn't expect perfect clients; it builds its process around real ones. And it understands something else: the client who haggles isn't insulting her. He's doing what he thinks serves him. Sometimes the right move is to negotiate. Sometimes it's to let him go, no hard feelings.

### At School: The Group Project and the Parents' Chat

**If you're studying as an adult:** George has a group project with three classmates in his MBA program. One never answers, one insists on doing everything his way, and one turns things in late. George, who works all week, feels like he's dragging the whole team.

Before the first meeting, George tells himself: "Somebody on this team won't pull their weight. That's how group projects go." Then he proposes something concrete: a shared document listing tasks, owners, and deadlines. If someone drops the ball, everyone can see it, and nobody has to pick a fight.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Claire is in the parents' group chat for Sophie's class. Every week there's a blowup: the money for the holiday party, too much homework, the dad who lectures the teacher in all caps. Claire decides to run through the meditation before she opens the chat: "Someone in here is going to be angry. I don't have to respond to everything." She mutes the chat and checks it once a day. She answers only what actually involves her. She's gotten back two hours a week and a little peace of mind.

### In Your Family: The Holiday Dinner

Every December, Lauren's family gets together at her mother's house. And every December, her Uncle Ernie brings up politics, mocks anyone who disagrees, and ends up in a fight with somebody. Lauren walks into dinner with a knot in her stomach.

This year she starts on meditation II.1 first thing in the morning: "My uncle is going to talk politics and try to get a rise out of people. He thinks that's how you stand up for what you believe. He can't make me argue." She prepares three things: a friendly line to sidestep the topic ("Uncle Ernie, today I'm not debating, I'm eating"), an ally (her cousin, who hates fights just as much), and an exit (helping with the dishes when things heat up).

Her uncle talks politics. Lauren smiles, delivers her line, and heads to the kitchen. For the first time in years, she gets home without a headache.

The next December, though, the plan doesn't hold up as well. Her uncle skips the general politics and goes straight after Lauren's job, in front of everyone. She uses her line; he keeps pushing. Lauren ends up snapping back harder than she meant to and leaves before dessert. In the car, Andrew asks whether the Stoic thing "failed." Lauren thinks about it and says not completely. This time she got angry, but she didn't spend three days replaying the fight, and the next morning she texted her uncle and calmly asked him not to make comments like that again. The practice didn't make her bulletproof. It helped her bounce back faster.

### In Money and Health: The Call to the Bank

Rick needs to call his bank about a charge on his credit card that shouldn't be there. He knows how it'll go: twenty minutes on hold, three transfers, and a rep who says there's nothing they can do. Last time, he ended up yelling and hanging up with nothing fixed.

This time he prepares the way Marcus would. He writes down the details first: card number, date and amount of the charge, the reference number from his earlier claim. He lists what they'll probably tell him ("you'll need to wait 45 days," "we don't see that in our system") and his answer to each. He decides not to yell, because the rep isn't the bank, and yelling at him won't get the money back.

It takes forty minutes and two transfers. But he hangs up with a case number and a resolution date. The call was just as annoying as last time. He wasn't.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Assume the worst about everyone."** No. Bracing for difficult people simply means you won't be shocked when someone turns out to be one. Most people, most days, are reasonable.

**"If people act out of ignorance, they aren't responsible."** No. Marcus explains other people's behavior so that he won't hate them, not so they can skip the consequences. An employee who steals still gets fired, however well you understand him.

**"You have to suffer in silence."** No. Often the Stoic move is to speak up, set a boundary, or ask for something to change. What you're avoiding is the knee-jerk reaction, not action itself.

## This Week's Exercise: The Morning Rehearsal

For seven days, before you look at your phone:

1. Think about the people you'll see or talk to today.
2. Pick the most difficult one and write one line about what they're likely to do ("criticize," "show up late," "ask for a discount").
3. Write one sentence that explains why they might act that way, without excusing it.
4. Decide on your response: what you'll do and what you won't.

At night, check back: did what you imagined actually happen? How did you handle it? Over time you'll find that difficult people get predictable, and predictable is something you can manage.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** each morning, name just one difficult person for the day and write one sentence of understanding.
- **Intermediate:** add a concrete plan: what you'll say, what you won't say, and your way out if things get heated.
- **Advanced:** prepare for your own difficult side, too. Write: "Today I'll probably ___ (interrupt, get impatient, judge)." The Stoics knew that the difficult person we run into most often is ourselves.

## Key Ideas

- Preparing for difficult people isn't pessimism. It's strategy.
- Almost nobody does wrong knowing it's wrong; they think it's good for them.
- Explain other people's behavior as generously as you explain your own.
- Understanding someone doesn't mean you have to stay close to them.

## Journaling Question

> **What am I silently demanding from someone who never promised to give it to me?**

---

Tomorrow the alarm will go off at 6:30 again, and the difficult people will still be on your calendar. You're not going to change them. But before you take the pillow off your face, you can do what an emperor did in a tent on the frontier: name what's coming, remember that those people belong to your own species, and get up to work alongside them, or to draw a line with them, without letting them steal your day.
