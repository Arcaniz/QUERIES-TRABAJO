# Chapter 7. See Things Stripped Bare

> *When you're served meat or fish, think: this is the dead body of a fish, a bird, a pig. The finest wine is grape juice. The purple robe is sheep's wool dyed in shellfish blood. Thoughts like these cut to the core of things and show them for what they are, and we should think this way all through life: whenever something looks impressive, strip it bare, see how little it's worth, and peel off the story it's puffing itself up with.*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, VI.13 (paraphrase)

In a department store window sits a $2,500 handbag. Next to it is one that looks nearly identical for $120. The difference is a logo about an inch wide. Some people spend a year paying off that logo.

At a high-end restaurant, the server spends a full minute describing the dish: "a reduction of," "an emulsion of," "on a bed of." What arrives is an enormous plate with three bites in the middle.

On LinkedIn, someone calls himself a "visionary, disruptor, and transformation leader." He's a sales manager.

Marcus Aurelius had an exercise for all of this: strip things bare. Take away the name, the packaging, and the story, and look at what's left.

## The Context: The Man in the Purple Robe

It's no accident that Marcus writes about the purple robe. Purple was the color of imperial power. Only the emperor could wear it head to toe. It was the ultimate status symbol of the Roman world. And Marcus, who wore it, reminded himself in private that it was sheep's wool stained with snail blood.

In the same passage he turns the exercise on sex, describing it with almost clinical bluntness as friction between bodies. He isn't sneering at pleasure. He's making sure he doesn't become its slave. His method never changes: strip away the emotion, the fame, the decoration, and look at the bare fact. Scholars sometimes call this "physical definition," describing something exactly as it is, minus the value-loaded adjectives we pile on.

## Three Ways to Read This Meditation

### Reading 1. The Classic: Value Lives in Character, Not in Things

For the Stoics, wealth, fame, pleasure, and status are "indifferents." They aren't bad; many of them are "preferred" (being healthy beats being sick, and having money beats not having it). But they aren't good in themselves, because they can be used well or badly. The only true good is virtue.

Stripping things bare is a way of keeping that ranking straight. If the purple robe is dyed wool, the person wearing it isn't worth more for wearing it. If "director" is a chair and a word on a business card, the person who holds the title isn't a better human being for holding it.

**In a nutshell:** things are worth what they are, not what they look like.

### Reading 2. The Practical: An Antidote to Marketing

These days we're surrounded by machinery built to do the exact opposite of what Marcus recommended: dress things up. Advertising doesn't sell you a car; it sells you freedom. It doesn't sell perfume; it sells seduction. It doesn't sell a phone; it sells belonging.

VI.13 makes a great filter for spending decisions. Before you buy something expensive, describe it stripped bare: "a piece of leather with a logo on it." "A laptop that does what mine already does, with a better camera." "An SUV I'll drive to work, alone, every day." If you still want it after that, buy it and don't feel guilty. Often, though, you'll find it was the story you wanted, not the thing.

### Reading 3. The Uncomfortable One: What About Beauty?

This exercise comes with a risk: cynicism. If everything is "just" something, then a symphony is "just" vibrating air, a kiss is "just" skin touching skin, and a child is "just" a bundle of cells. Follow that road far enough and nothing is worth anything.

Marcus isn't headed there. Elsewhere he marvels at the beauty of ordinary natural things: bread splitting open as it bakes, ears of wheat bending under their own weight, the foam at a wild boar's mouth. The exercise isn't meant to make you despise everything. It's meant to let the air out of what's inflated, above all the things that stir up excessive desire, envy, or vanity.

A good rule of thumb: strip bare whatever enslaves you; leave clothed whatever makes you better.

**In a nutshell:** stripping things bare isn't looking down on them; it's breaking their spell.

## What the Other Stoics Said

### Epictetus: Remember What It Is You Love

Epictetus suggests a similar exercise, aimed at the things we're attached to. With anything you enjoy or find useful, he says, remind yourself what it actually is, starting with the smallest things. If you're fond of a jug, say: "I'm fond of a jug." Then if it breaks, you won't be shattered along with it.

The example sounds trivial, but the method is powerful. Describing something as it actually is takes away its power to overwhelm you. A new car is a car. A phone is a phone. You can enjoy them both, and neither will wreck you when it gets scratched or lost.

### Seneca: Poor Isn't Having Too Little

Seneca wrote a line people have been quoting for two thousand years: it isn't the person with too little who's poor, but the one who always wants more. For him, wealth isn't measured by what you own. It's measured by the gap between what you have and what you want. Someone whose wants have no limit will always be poor, even with millions.

Seneca, it should be said, was one of the richest men in Rome, and his critics never let him forget it. His answer was that a wise man can own riches as long as he doesn't keep them in his heart. It's a debatable answer, and it's worth reading him with that tension in mind: a rich philosopher preaching simplicity is a reminder that this idea is easier to write than to live.

### What Science Says Today

Psychologists call it *hedonic adaptation*: our tendency to get used to good things. The new car, the raise, the bigger house give us a lift that, over time, settles back to roughly where we started. A classic 1978 study by Philip Brickman and colleagues compared lottery winners with other people and didn't find the winners to be much happier over the long haul. It's a small, old study, but the broader idea has been explored in a lot of research since.

Other work, like psychologist Tim Kasser's, has linked a strong focus on materialistic values (wealth, image, status) with lower well-being on average. And research by Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich suggested that, in many cases, money spent on experiences brings more satisfaction than money spent on things.

None of this means money doesn't matter. It means that once the basics are covered, the things we buy for the story rarely deliver what the story promised.

## A Dialogue with a Skeptic

**Skeptic:** Stripping things bare sounds depressing. If everything is "just" something, nothing's special anymore.

**Author:** The exercise isn't for everything. It's for whatever has a hold on you: what puts you in debt, what makes you envious, what makes you feel small. You don't need to strip bare your kid's hug or a sunset. Marcus himself marveled at the beauty of natural things. What he strips is prestige, not beauty.

**Skeptic:** But brands, titles, and reputation matter in the real world. If I ignore them, I'll miss opportunities.

**Author:** Of course they matter, as tools. A good title can open a door. A good reputation can land you a client. The Stoics called these "preferred indifferents," things it's reasonable to prefer. The trouble starts when you mistake the tool for your worth as a person, and suddenly you're losing sleep over a logo or a job title.

**Skeptic:** Seneca was loaded. Isn't it hypocritical for a millionaire to tell people not to care about money?

**Author:** It's a real contradiction, and people called him on it in his own time. But you can separate the idea from the man. Besides, Seneca didn't say not to care about money; he said not to *depend* on it. A rich man who could lose everything without losing his head is freer than a poor man obsessed with getting more. Seneca's contradiction works as a warning: this is easy to preach and hard to live.

**Skeptic:** So I'm not allowed to buy myself something nice?

**Author:** You are. If you strip it bare and still want it, and you can pay for it without mortgaging your peace of mind, buy it and enjoy it. The exercise doesn't forbid anything. It clarifies. It makes sure you're buying the thing, not a story somebody sold you.

## Six Scenes from Adult Life

### In Your Relationship: The Wedding and the Feed

Lauren and Andrew have friends who spent a down payment's worth of money on their wedding. Others post vacation photos every month. Sometimes Lauren feels like their married life is dull by comparison.

She does the exercise. A lavish wedding, stripped bare, is a party that lasts one night. A vacation on Instagram, stripped bare, is one photo picked out of fifty, then edited. Neither one says anything about how those couples treat each other on an ordinary Tuesday.

Lauren and Andrew decide their "luxury" will be the things that never make it onto social media: Sunday breakfast together with the phones put away, and one simple trip a year, just the two of them.

**The flip side:** stripping things bare also works mid-argument. "You left your socks on the floor" is, stripped bare, two socks on the floor. It is not "you don't respect me." Sometimes a fight fizzles out just from describing the fact without the story.

### At Work: The Title and the Corner Office

Rick gets two job offers. One comes with the title "Director of Operations," a private office, and a 10 percent higher salary. The other is "Senior Manager" at a company with a better culture, flexible hours, and a boss he knows and respects.

The title dazzles him. So he does the exercise. "Director" is a word on a business card. The office is a room with a door. The extra 10 percent works out to about $400 a month, in exchange for a boss known for burning out his people and a two-hour daily commute.

Stripped bare, the second offer is worth more. Rick takes it. A year later, he doesn't miss the word.

### In Business: Real Value

Megan wonders why other studios charge three times what she does. She realizes many of them are selling the story: designer offices, slick pitch decks, buzzwords like "360-degree brand strategy." Behind it, sometimes, the work is no better than hers. Sometimes it's worse.

For a business, the exercise cuts two ways. First, it keeps her from being intimidated: the competition, stripped bare, is people doing similar work. Second, it helps her explain her own value without exaggerating. Megan stops selling "logos" and starts explaining what a good logo does for a business: it makes the business recognizable, trustworthy, distinct. She doesn't embellish; she translates. And she raises her prices without inflating a thing.

**For entrepreneurs:** good marketing doesn't dress things up as something they aren't. It explains what they are.

### At School: The Diploma and the Brand Name

**If you're studying as an adult:** George chose his MBA program for the university's prestige. Halfway through, he notices some professors barely prepare for class, and he starts wondering whether it's worth what he's paying.

Stripped bare, an MBA is a set of classes, readings, classmates, and a piece of paper at the end. The paper opens some doors, and that's worth something. But what he really walks away with is what he learns and who he meets. So George changes how he approaches it. He stops chasing grades alone and starts seeking out the good professors, the classmates who really know their stuff, and the material he can use at work.

**If you're supporting your kids:** Sophie wants a pair of name-brand sneakers because "everybody in my class has them." Claire skips the lecture. She does the exercise with her instead: "What do those shoes do that these don't?" Sophie thinks about it. "Nothing. But they're the ones everyone wears." Claire explains, at a ten-year-old's level, that that's paying for the story. They make a deal: if Sophie still wants them, she can pay half out of her savings. Sophie decides they're not worth it. She's learned something a lot of adults never do.

### In Your Family: The Sweet Sixteen

Lauren's niece is turning sixteen, and Lauren's sister wants to throw a huge sweet sixteen: a rented ballroom, a designer dress, a sit-down dinner for three hundred. She asks Lauren for a loan.

Lauren wants to help, but she does the exercise first. A sweet sixteen, stripped bare, is a night to celebrate someone you love. What the ballroom and the banquet are really buying is something else: not looking bad in front of the family, proving you can pull it off. Lauren tells her sister she'll pay for the cake and help plan, but she won't lend money for the venue. Her sister is hurt for a while.

In the end they throw a smaller party in Grandma's backyard. The birthday girl dances all night. Years from now, nobody will remember whether the venue was fancy. Everybody will remember being there.

### In Money and Health: The New Car

Andrew wants to trade in his car. It's eight years old and runs just fine, but he's seen a new one he loves. It would mean a five-year loan.

Before he signs anything, he writes out the stripped-bare version: "A metal box that would take me to the same job, with a bigger screen and that new-car smell." Then he writes down the story he'd be buying: feeling successful, feeling like he's made it. And he asks himself whether there's another way to get that. He decides to keep his car tuned up, set aside the amount of the monthly payment for a year, and, if he still wants the new one at the end, buy it with a much bigger down payment.

A year later he has a healthy savings cushion, and his craving for the new car has mostly faded. He uses part of the money for a trip with Lauren. The rest goes into an emergency fund, just in case.

## What This Chapter Does *Not* Say

**"Nothing is worth anything."** No. The exercise deflates what's inflated, not what has real value.

**"Enjoying luxury is wrong."** No. The Stoics weren't monks. Seneca was very rich. What they criticized was depending on luxury and measuring your worth by it.

**"Brands, titles, and reputation don't matter."** They matter as tools. They don't matter as a measure of who you are.

## This Week's Exercise: The Stripped-Bare Definition

1. Pick three things that stir up too much desire, envy, or admiration in you: something you want to buy, someone you envy, a position you're after.
2. Describe each one "stripped bare," in a single sentence, with no value-loaded adjectives. For example: "A luxury car: a metal machine with four wheels that would take me to the same job."
3. Next to each one, write the story you'd be buying: status, security, belonging, admiration.
4. Ask yourself whether there's another way, cheaper or healthier, to get what you're actually after.

Bonus: the next time someone makes you angry, describe what they did in one stripped-bare sentence, with no interpretation. You'll find a lot of offenses lose half their weight.

### The Exercise at Three Levels

- **Beginner:** before any purchase bigger than what you'd spend on an ordinary day, write its stripped-bare definition in a single sentence.
- **Intermediate:** use the 72-hour rule. If something dazzles you, wait three days before buying it. If you've stripped it bare and waited and still want it, buy it guilt-free.
- **Advanced:** strip bare something of yours you're proud of: your title, your house, your degree. Ask yourself what would be left of your worth as a person if you lost it tomorrow. That answer is what truly belongs to you.

## Key Ideas

- Describe things as they are, without the story that inflates them.
- Ask how much you're paying for the thing and how much for the story.
- Wealth and fame are "indifferents": tools, not a measure of your worth.
- Strip bare what enslaves you; leave clothed what makes you better.

## Journaling Question

> **What in my life am I paying for more because of the story than because of what it actually is?**

Go back to that store window. The $2,500 bag is still sitting next to the $120 one. Now you can see them both stripped bare: two pieces of leather, one with an inch of prestige stamped on it. You don't have to look down on whoever buys the expensive one. You just have to know, with the clarity of an emperor wrapped in purple, what you're paying for, and why.
