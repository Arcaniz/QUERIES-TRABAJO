# Before We Begin: A Brief History of Stoicism

## A Shipwrecked Merchant in Athens

The story of Stoicism begins with a shipwreck. Around 300 BCE, a Phoenician merchant named Zeno, from Citium on the island of Cyprus, lost his cargo at sea and arrived in Athens with nothing. According to Diogenes Laertius, an ancient biographer, Zeno walked into a bookshop, began reading about Socrates, and asked where he could find men like that. The bookseller pointed to Crates, a Cynic philosopher who happened to be walking by. Zeno followed him.

Years later, Zeno began teaching on his own. He didn't do it in a closed school but in a public spot in the Athenian marketplace: a colonnade decorated with paintings, called the *Stoa Poikile*, the "Painted Porch." That's where the name comes from: Stoics, "the people of the porch." It was a philosophy born in the street, open to anyone who walked by.

Zeno is credited with a line about his own story: "I made a prosperous voyage when I was shipwrecked." It may be the first Stoic lesson: what stands in the way becomes the way.

## What They Taught

The early Stoics divided philosophy into three parts and compared it to an orchard: logic was the fence, physics was the tree, and ethics was the fruit. Logic was for thinking clearly and not being fooled by appearances. Physics studied nature and the universe, which the Stoics saw as an ordered whole governed by reason, which they called the *logos*. Ethics, the fruit, taught how to live well.

To live well, they said, you must live in agreement with nature. And since human nature is rational and social, living well means using reason and living for the good of the community. They called this *virtue*, and divided it into four: **wisdom** (knowing what is good, what is bad, and what is neither), **justice** (giving each person their due and treating others well), **courage** (facing fear and pain), and **self-control** (mastering desire and keeping measure in all things).

Everything else (health, wealth, fame, even life itself) was, for them, *indifferent*. Not in the sense that it didn't matter, but in the sense that it is neither good nor bad in itself: it depends on how it is used. A millionaire can be generous or cruel; a sick person can be brave or bitter. What makes a life good isn't what happens to you, but how you respond.

## What About the Gods?

There's something many modern books on Stoicism prefer not to mention, and it's worth saying up front: the ancient Stoics were deeply religious, in their own way. They believed the universe was ordered by a divine reason, the *logos*, which they sometimes called Zeus, nature, or providence. For them, everything that happens is part of that order, so accepting what is not within our power wasn't surrender to chaos but cooperation with a whole that makes sense.

Marcus Aurelius shared that belief. Book I of the *Meditations* ends with thanks to the gods, and many passages speak of "the nature of the whole" and of welcoming what life brings. But he was also honest about doubt. Several times he poses a dilemma: either the universe is governed by providence, or it is just a jumble of atoms coming together and drifting apart by chance (see, for example, IV.3). And he reaches a remarkable conclusion: in either case, the right way to live is the same. If there is providence, live in harmony with it. If there isn't, all the more reason to govern yourself by your own reason and not be swept along.

This book offers a practical reading of Stoicism that can serve believers of any faith and nonbelievers alike. That's not because Stoic theology doesn't matter, but because, as Marcus himself sensed, the part of his philosophy that touches our daily life works either way. Still, you should know that the emperor was not a modern secular thinker: he prayed, he gave thanks, and he believed the universe had an order.

## From Athens to Rome

After Zeno, the school had other leaders in Athens, such as Cleanthes, a former boxer who carried water at night to pay for his studies, and Chrysippus, a thinker so prolific that it was said that without him there would have been no Stoa. Over time Stoicism traveled to Rome, where it found fertile ground. The Romans, a practical people, cared less about logic and physics and more about ethics: how to govern, how to face adversity, how to die with dignity.

That's how the three great Roman Stoics emerged, whose writings we still have nearly complete and who appear throughout this book:

**Seneca** (c. 4 BCE–65 CE) was a writer, a politician, one of the richest men in Rome, and tutor to the emperor Nero. He wrote letters, essays, and tragedies. His prose is the warmest and most elegant of the three. His life was full of contradictions: he preached simplicity surrounded by luxury and advised an emperor who became a tyrant. Nero ordered him to take his own life, and Seneca, according to the historian Tacitus, did so calmly.

**Epictetus** (c. 50–135 CE) was born a slave in Phrygia, in what is now Turkey. He studied with the Stoic philosopher Musonius Rufus and, once freed, founded a school in Nicopolis, in Greece. He wrote nothing himself; his lessons were recorded by a student, Arrian, in two works: the *Discourses* and a short summary, the *Handbook* (or *Enchiridion*). His style is direct, sometimes blunt, always practical.

**Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE) was emperor of Rome for nearly twenty years. Adopted by his uncle Antoninus Pius at the request of the emperor Hadrian, he was trained in philosophy from a young age. He ruled in times of war and plague, and wrote, in Greek and for himself alone, the notebooks we now know as the *Meditations*. He probably would have titled them differently; one name found in the manuscripts is *To Himself*.

## How the Meditations Reached Us

Marcus never meant to publish his notes. We don't know exactly how they were preserved after his death. For centuries they are barely mentioned; a few Byzantine writers quote them. The first printed edition appeared in Zurich around 1558–1559, and they have been read ever since. They have sat on the nightstands of kings, presidents, soldiers, prisoners, and ordinary people looking, like Marcus, for a way not to lose their heads.

## Stoicism Today

In the twentieth century, Stoicism had an unexpected second life through psychology. Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, founders of the cognitive therapies, acknowledged the influence of Epictetus's idea that we are disturbed not by things but by our opinions about them. Viktor Frankl, the psychiatrist who survived the Nazi concentration camps, wrote that everything can be taken from a person but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude in any set of circumstances. He didn't call himself a Stoic, but the line could have come from Epictetus.

In recent decades, philosophers such as Pierre Hadot, Martha Nussbaum, Massimo Pigliucci, and William B. Irvine have helped recover Stoicism as a way of life rather than just an academic subject. And on social media it has become a trend, with all the good and bad that implies: more people know about it, but many know a distorted version.

This book tries to go back to the source. Not to the Stoicism of motivational quotes, but to that of a tired man in a tent, trying to be a little better each day.

## Where This Book Fits

Each chapter that follows takes a passage from the *Meditations* and sets it in conversation with Seneca, with Epictetus, and with some current research. But Marcus is always at the center, because he is the only one of the three who wrote for himself, and that makes him the most like us: someone with responsibilities, with fatigue, with difficult people around him, who didn't have all the answers and who still, every night, opened his notebook again.
