# Glossary of Stoic Terms

**Adiaphora (indifferents).** Things that are neither good nor bad in themselves, such as health, wealth, fame, or pleasure. Their value depends on how they're used.

**Preferred indifferents.** Among the indifferents, those it's reasonable to prefer: it's better to be healthy than sick, or to have enough than to live in poverty. But preferring them doesn't mean depending on them.

**Apatheia.** Literally, "without passions." It doesn't mean apathy or indifference, but freedom from excessive, irrational passions, such as runaway anger or paralyzing fear.

**Assent (synkatathesis).** The act of accepting or rejecting an impression as true. For the Stoics, this is where our freedom lies: we don't choose the impressions that come to us, but we do choose whether to accept them.

**Ataraxia.** Tranquility or calm of the soul. The term was also used by the Epicureans and the Skeptics.

**Cosmopolitanism.** The idea that all human beings are citizens of a single universal community, because we all share reason.

**Dichotomy of control.** The distinction between what is within our power and what isn't. It's the foundation of Epictetus's *Handbook* and of Chapter 1.

**Eudaimonia.** Usually translated as "happiness," but it means something closer to "a life well lived" or "flourishing." For the Stoics, it is reached through virtue.

**Eupatheiai (good emotions).** The healthy emotions that, according to the Stoics, a wise person feels: calm joy (instead of runaway pleasure), reasonable caution (instead of fear), and goodwill or reasonable wishing (instead of unchecked appetite). They show that Stoicism doesn't aim for coldness.

**First movements.** Immediate, involuntary reactions, such as being startled or blushing. Seneca calls them *primi motus* in Latin; later authors used the Greek term *propatheiai*, "pre-passions." They aren't within our power; what we do next is.

**Hegemonikon.** The ruling part of the soul, the faculty that reasons, judges, and decides. Marcus Aurelius often refers to it as an inner citadel.

**Logos.** The reason that, according to the Stoics, orders the universe and is also present in every human being.

**Memento mori.** "Remember that you will die." A Latin phrase summing up the practice of recalling one's own mortality in order to live better.

**Oikeiosis.** The process by which a being takes ownership of what belongs to it and cares for it. It starts with oneself and extends to family, friends, and humanity, as in Hierocles's circles in Chapter 11.

**Physical definition.** The exercise of describing something exactly as it is, without the value-laden adjectives we add. It's the method of "seeing things stripped bare" in Chapter 7.

**Premeditatio malorum.** "Premeditation of adversity." The exercise of imagining in advance what might go wrong, so as to be prepared and not caught off guard.

**Prohairesis.** Epictetus's term for the faculty of choice, the will. According to him, it is the only thing that is truly ours.

**Providence.** The Stoic belief that the universe is ordered by a divine reason that cares for the whole. Marcus Aurelius holds it, though he repeatedly considers the alternative of a universe of atoms without order.

**Reserve clause (hupexairesis).** Acting with an implicit condition: "I will do this, if nothing prevents it." It lets you commit without being at the mercy of the outcome.

**Stoa.** Porch or colonnade. The school takes its name from the *Stoa Poikile*, the Painted Porch in Athens where Zeno taught.

**View from above.** The exercise of imagining your own life and problems as seen from high above, as part of an immense whole. Marcus Aurelius practices it in several passages, for example IX.30.

**Virtue (arete).** Excellence of character. For the Stoics, the only true good. It's divided into four: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control.

# Recommended Reading

If this book has whetted your appetite, here are a few doors to keep going. Choose whichever edition and translation suits you best; there are many good ones.

## The Sources

- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*.** The book that inspired this one. Read it slowly, a few pages a night, the way it was written. Translations by Gregory Hays, Robin Hard, and Robin Waterfield are all widely recommended.
- **Epictetus, *Handbook* (*Enchiridion*).** Very short; you can read it in an afternoon and reread it for the rest of your life.
- **Epictetus, *Discourses*.** The complete lessons, longer and livelier than the *Handbook*.
- **Seneca, *Letters from a Stoic* (*Letters to Lucilius*).** One hundred twenty-four letters on how to live. The best place to get to know Seneca.
- **Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life*, *On Anger*, and *On Tranquility of Mind*.** Short and very practical essays.
- **Musonius Rufus, *Lectures*.** Epictetus's teacher, less known and surprisingly modern on topics like marriage and the education of women.

## Studies and Popular Introductions

- **Pierre Hadot, *The Inner Citadel*.** The classic study of the *Meditations*, for those who want to go deeper.
- **Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*.** On ancient philosophy as a way of living.
- **William B. Irvine, *A Guide to the Good Life*.** A practical, modern introduction, where the idea of the "trichotomy of control" appears.
- **Massimo Pigliucci, *How to Be a Stoic*.** A contemporary philosopher explains how he applies Stoicism, in dialogue with Epictetus.
- **Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning*.** Not a Stoic book, but one of the most powerful testimonies to inner freedom in the face of adversity.

A final caution: you don't need to read them all. Marcus Aurelius told himself to set aside his thirst for books and devote himself to living. Read one, practice what you read, and then, if you like, read another.
